# History: Post-Mauryan Period & Foreign Invasions

> The fragmentation of the Mauryan empire opened the floodgates—foreign dynasties, regional powers, and cultural syntheses that redefined Indian civilization.

## Chapter 1: The Shunga Dynasty—The Brahminical Revival

### Section 1 | The Overthrow of the Mauryas

The Mauryan empire did not collapse overnight—it *fragmented*. The last Mauryan king, *Brihadratha*, was assassinated in =185 BCE= by his own *commander-in-chief*, *Pushyamitra Shunga*. This was not a foreign invasion—it was an *internal coup*.

**Pushyamitra Shunga (≈185–149 BCE)**

- He was a *Brahmin*—the Shunga dynasty was the *first* Brahminical dynasty to rule Magadha.
- He performed the *Ashvamedha* sacrifice—a *Vedic* ritual that legitimized imperial authority.
- He *revived* the *Vedic* orthodoxy—the Shungas were *patrons* of the Brahmins.
- However, the Shungas *did not* restore the Mauryan empire's territorial extent—they ruled only *Magadha* and *central India*.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The SSC often asks: "Who overthrew the Mauryan empire?" The answer is =Pushyamitra Shunga=. However, the *Shunga* dynasty was *not* a *foreign* dynasty—they were *Indian*. The *foreign invasions* (Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Kushans) came *after* the Shungas.
[/alert]

**Buddhist Persecution: Myth and Reality**

The *Shungas* are often accused of *persecuting* Buddhists. The primary source for this is the *Divyavadana* (a Buddhist text), which claims Pushyamitra *destroyed* Buddhist monasteries and *massacred* monks.

[alert type="info"]
**Critical Nuance:**
The *Divyavadana* is a *Buddhist* text—it is *biased*. There is *no* archaeological evidence for widespread Buddhist persecution under the Shungas. The *Sanchi* stupa was *enlarged* during the Shunga period—this suggests that the Shungas *supported* Buddhism as well. The exam tests this *distinction*: the evidence for persecution is *textual* and *Buddhist-biased*—it is *not* confirmed by archaeology.
[/alert]

**Patronage of Patanjali**

Pushyamitra Shunga was the *patron* of *Patanjali*, the author of the *Mahābhāṣya* (the "Great Commentary" on Panini's grammar). Patanjali's work is *not* a religious text—it is a *linguistic* treatise. The Shungas were *patrons* of learning, not *sectarian* bigots.

---

### Section 2 | The Decline of the Shungas

The Shunga dynasty lasted for ≈112 years (185–73 BCE). The *last* Shunga king, *Devabhuti*, was assassinated by his *minister* *Vasudeva* (or *Vasudeva Kanva*)—who founded the *Kanva* dynasty.

[flashcards title="Shunga Dynasty Essentials"]
| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Founder | Pushyamitra Shunga |
| Reign | 185–149 BCE |
| Overthrew | Brihadratha Maurya (last Mauryan) |
| Patronage | Patanjali (Mahābhāṣya) |
| Religious policy | Brahminical revival (but also supported Buddhism) |
| Last king | Devabhuti (assassinated 73 BCE) |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 2: The Kanva Dynasty—The Brief Interlude

### Section 1 | The Rise and Fall

The Kanva dynasty (≈73–28 BCE) was a *short-lived* Brahminical dynasty that ruled Magadha after the Shungas.

**The Founder: Vasudeva Kanva**

- Vasudeva was the *minister* who *assassinated* Devabhuti (the last Shunga king).
- The Kanvas were *Brahminical*—they continued the *Vedic* traditions of the Shungas.
- However, they were *weak* rulers—they lacked the *military* power to resist the *foreign invasions* from the northwest.

**The End of the Kanvas**

The Kanvas were *overthrown* by the *Satavahanas* (the Andhras)—a *southern* dynasty that expanded *northward*.

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
The Kanvas were the *last* Brahminical dynasty to rule Magadha. The *Guptas* (who came later) were *also* Brahminical, but they were *not* directly descended from the Kanvas.
[/alert]

|------|--------|
| Founder | Vasudeva Kanva |
| Reign | 73–28 BCE |
| Overthrew | Devabhuti (last Shunga) |
| Overthrown by | Satavahanas |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 3: The Satavahana Dynasty—The Great Andhras

### Section 1 | The Rise of the Deccan Power

The Satavahanas (also called the *Andhras*) were the *most powerful* dynasty in the Deccan after the Mauryan decline. They ruled from ≈1st century BCE to 3rd century CE.

**The Geographical Base:**

- The Satavahanas originated in the *Deccan* (modern-day Maharashtra, Telangana, and Andhra Pradesh).
- Their *capital* was *Pratishthana* (modern Paithan in Maharashtra).
- They *controlled* the *western* and *eastern* coasts—and the *trade routes* that connected the Deccan to the *Arabian Sea*.

**The Great Satavahana Rulers:**

| King | Reign (approx.) | Key Achievements |
|------|-----------------|------------------|
| Simuka | 1st century BCE | Founder of the dynasty |
| Satakarni I | 1st century BCE | Performed Vedic sacrifices; expanded to Kalinga |
| Gautamiputra Satakarni | 106–130 CE | Greatest Satavahana king; defeated Shakas, Yavanas, and Pahlavas |
| Yajna Sri Satakarni | 152–181 CE | Patron of learning; trade with Rome |

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The Satavahanas are often confused with the *Cholas* (a *later* dynasty). The Cholas ruled the *Kaveri* region in the *south*—the Satavahanas ruled the *Deccan*. The SSC often tests this *geographic* distinction.
[/alert]

---

### Section 2 | Gautamiputra Satakarni—The Greatest Satavahana

Gautamiputra Satakarni (≈106–130 CE) is the *most celebrated* Satavahana king. His *title*—"the destroyer of the Shakas, Yavanas, and Pahlavas"—is inscribed in the *Nasik* and *Pandharpur* inscriptions.

**The Conquests:**

- He *defeated* the *Shaka* king *Nahapana* (of the Kshaharata dynasty) and *re-issued* Nahapana's coins with his *own* name—a classic act of *victory propaganda*.
- He *extended* the Satavahana empire from *Malwa* in the north to *Tungabhadra* in the south.
- He *controlled* the *western* coast and the *Arabian Sea* trade routes.

**The Nasik Inscription:**

The *Nasik Prashasti* (inscription) of Gautamiputra Satakarni is the *primary source* for his reign. It describes:
- His *victories* over the *foreigners* (Shakas, Yavanas, Pahlavas).
- His *patronage* of the *Brahmins* (he performed the *Ashvamedha*).
- His *generosity*—he donated villages and lands to the *Brahmins*.

---

### Section 3 | Trade with the Romans

The Satavahanas were *critical* to the *Roman-Indian trade* network. The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* (a Greek navigational text from the 1st century CE) describes the *Satavahana* kingdom as a *major* exporter of:
- **Cotton** (the finest cotton came from the Satavahana region)
- **Pepper** (the Malabar coast was the source)
- **Ivory** (from the Deccan)
- **Semi-precious stones** (carnelian, beryl, and diamonds)

[alert type="info"]
**Examiner's Focus:**
The *Periplus* mentions the Satavahana king as *Siritan*—a Greek corruption of *Sri Satakarni*. The exam often tests this *identification*: the "Siritan" of the Periplus is the Satavahana king. This is a *cross-cultural* reference that appears frequently in competitive exams.
[/alert]

**The Roman Gold:**

- The Romans paid for Indian goods in *gold* (Roman denarii).
- Large caches of Roman coins have been found in *southern India* (especially in the *Chera* and *Pandya* kingdoms).
- The Satavahanas also issued *punch-marked coins* and *cast coins*—their coinage shows a *mixture* of Indian and Roman motifs.

---

### Section 4 | The Decline of the Satavahanas

After Yajna Sri Satakarni (≈152–181 CE), the Satavahana dynasty *declined*. The *successors* were *weak*—the empire *fragmented* into *regional* kingdoms:
- The *Vakatakas* rose in the *Deccan*.
- The *Pallavas* rose in the *south*.
- The *Kadambas* rose in the *western* Deccan.

[flashcards title="Satavahana Dynasty Essentials"]
| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Founder | Simuka |
| Greatest king | Gautamiputra Satakarni (106–130 CE) |
| Defeated | Nahapana (Shaka) |
| Patronage | Vedic sacrifices; Brahminism; Buddhism |
| Trade | Roman trade (cotton, pepper, ivory) |
| Overthrown by | Regional kingdoms (Vakatakas, Pallavas) |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 4: The Indo-Greeks—The Hellenistic Encounter

### Section 1 | The Bactrian Greeks in India

The *Indo-Greeks* (also called *Yavanas*) were the *first* foreign dynasty to establish a *kingdom* in India after Alexander.

**The Historical Context:**

- The *Bactrian Greeks* (Greek kings of Bactria—modern Afghanistan) were *separated* from the *Seleucid* empire in ≈250 BCE.
- They *expanded* into India in ≈180 BCE—taking advantage of the *weakness* of the Mauryan successors.
- The *first* Greek king to invade India was *Demetrius* (≈180–165 BCE)—he conquered the *Indus* valley and parts of *Punjab*.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The Indo-Greeks are often confused with *Alexander's* Greeks. Alexander's Greeks were *Macedonians*—they did *not* settle in India. The Indo-Greeks were *Bactrian Greeks*—they were *later* and they *established* kingdoms in India. The distinction is *chronological*: Alexander (326 BCE) vs. Indo-Greeks (180 BCE onwards).
[/alert]

**Demetrius: The First Greek King in India**

- Demetrius was the *first* Greek king to *rule* in India.
- He is often identified with the *Yavana* kings mentioned in the *Ashtadhyayi* of Panini.
- He *conquered* Gandhara, Taxila, and parts of Punjab.
- However, his *successors* were *not* as capable—the Indo-Greek kingdom *fragmented*.

---

### Section 2 | Menander (Milinda)—The Buddhist King

*Menander* (≈155–130 BCE) is the *most famous* Indo-Greek king—he is the *only* Greek king to have a *Buddhist* text named after him.

**The Milindapanha (The Questions of Milinda)**

- The *Milindapanha* is a *dialogue* between King Menander and the Buddhist monk *Nagasena*.
- Menander asks *questions* about Buddhist philosophy—Nagasena answers them.
- At the end of the dialogue, Menander *converts* to Buddhism.

**The Historical Menander:**

- Menander ruled a *large* Indo-Greek kingdom—from *Taxila* to *Mathura*.
- He issued coins with *Greek* and *Indian* inscriptions—the coins show *Buddhist* symbols (the *dharma-chakra*).
- He is mentioned in the *Periplus* as a *powerful* king.

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
Menander's *conversion* to Buddhism is the *first* evidence of a *foreign* king embracing an *Indian* religion. This is *significant* for the exam—it shows that the Indo-Greeks *assimilated* into Indian culture.
[/alert]

**The Legacy of the Indo-Greeks:**

- The Indo-Greeks introduced *Hellenistic* art (the *Gandhara* style).
- They introduced *Greek* astronomy and *Greek* coinage (with *portraits* of kings—a practice that was *not* Indian).
- They *facilitated* the spread of Buddhism to *Central Asia*.

[flashcards title="Indo-Greeks Essentials"]
| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| First Greek king in India | Demetrius (≈180 BCE) |
| Greatest king | Menander (≈155–130 BCE) |
| Buddhist text | Milindapanha (Questions of Milinda) |
| Buddhist monk | Nagasena |
| Legacy | Gandhara art; Greek coinage; spread of Buddhism |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 5: The Shakas (Scythians)—The Central Asian Invaders

### Section 1 | The Scythian Invasions

The *Shakas* (also called the *Scythians*) were a *Central Asian* nomadic tribe. They *invaded* India in the 1st century BCE—taking advantage of the *weakness* of the Indo-Greek kingdoms.

**The Shaka Migration:**

- The Shakas were *driven* out of Central Asia by the *Yueh-chi* (Kushans).
- They *occupied* the Indo-Greek territories in *Gandhara* and *Punjab*.
- They *established* two *major* dynasties in India:
  1. The *Kshaharata* dynasty (western India—Gujarat and Rajasthan).
  2. The *Kshatrapa* dynasty (central India—Ujjain and Malwa).

---

### Section 2 | The Kshaharata Dynasty: Nahapana

*Nahapana* (≈119–124 CE) was the *most powerful* Shaka king of the *Kshaharata* dynasty.

**Nahapana's Rule:**

- He controlled a *large* territory—from *Malwa* to the *Konkan* coast.
- He issued *punch-marked coins* with Greek and Indian inscriptions.
- He was *defeated* by *Gautamiputra Satakarni* (the Satavahana king) in ≈124 CE.

**The Junagadh Rock Inscription:**

The *Junagadh Rock Inscription* (Gujarat) is the *most famous* Shaka inscription—it records the *repair* of a *dam* (the Sudarshana lake) built by the Mauryans.

---

### Section 3 | Rudradaman I—The Great Shaka King

*Rudradaman I* (≈130–150 CE) was the *greatest* Shaka king. He ruled the *Kshatrapa* dynasty from *Ujjain*.

**The Junagadh Rock Inscription (Continued):**

- Rudradaman's inscription at Junagadh is the *first* major inscription in *Sanskrit* (not Prakrit).
- It describes the *repair* of the Sudarshana lake—a *public work* that shows the Shaka king's *administrative efficiency*.

**Rudradaman's Achievements:**

- He *defeated* the *Satavahanas* (though he did not *destroy* them).
- He *reconquered* the territories *lost* to the Satavahanas.
- He was a *patron* of Sanskrit and *Brahminism*—the Junagadh inscription is written in *polished* Sanskrit.

[alert type="info"]
**Examiner's Focus:**
Rudradaman's Junagadh inscription is *significant* because it is the *earliest* evidence of *Sanskrit* being used as a *court language*—previous inscriptions were in Prakrit (Ashoka's edicts) or Greek/Aramaic. The *Sanskrit* inscriptions became *common* only in the *Gupta* period.
[/alert]

[flashcards title="Shaka (Scythian) Essentials"]
| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Origin | Central Asia (Scythians) |
| Major dynasty | Kshaharata (Nahapana), Kshatrapa (Rudradaman) |
| Nahapana | Defeated by Gautamiputra Satakarni |
| Rudradaman I | Greatest Shaka king (≈130–150 CE) |
| Junagadh inscription | First major inscription in Sanskrit |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 6: The Kushan Empire—The Great Central Asian Power

### Section 1 | The Rise of the Kushans

The *Kushans* (also called *Yueh-chi*) were a *Central Asian* nomadic tribe that *conquered* the Shaka territories and established the *largest* empire in India after the Mauryas.

**The Kushan Migration:**

- The Kushans were *driven* out of China by the *Huns* (the Xiongnu).
- They *conquered* Bactria, Gandhara, and parts of India.
- The *first* Kushan king in India was *Kujula Kadphises* (≈30–80 CE).

**Kujula Kadphises (≈30–80 CE):**

- He was the *first* Kushan king to *rule* in India.
- He *united* the Kushan tribes.
- He issued coins with *Greek* and *Indian* motifs.

---

### Section 2 | Kanishka—The Greatest Kushan King

*Kanishka* (≈127–150 CE) is the *most famous* Kushan king—he is the *second* great emperor of India (after Ashoka).

**Kanishka's Reign:**

- **Saka Era**: Kanishka began the *Saka Era* in =78 CE=. This is the *most* important chronological anchor for the Kushan period.
- **Territory**: His empire extended from *Gandhara* (northwest) to *Varanasi* (east), and from *Kashmir* (north) to *Malwa* (south).
- **Capital**: *Purushapura* (modern Peshawar).

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The *Saka Era* (78 CE) is often confused with the *Vikrama Samvat* (57 BCE). The Saka Era is *Kanishka's* era; the Vikrama Samvat is *Chandragupta Vikramaditya's* era. The SSC tests this distinction *frequently*—know both dates and their *associated* kings.
[/alert]

**The Fourth Buddhist Council**

Kanishka convened the *Fourth Buddhist Council* at *Kundalvana* (near Srinagar, Kashmir) under the presidency of *Vasumitra*.

- The council *compiled* the *Vibhasha Shastras* (commentaries on the Buddhist canon).
- The council *divided* Buddhism into two *schools*: *Mahayana* (the "Great Vehicle") and *Hinayana* (the "Lesser Vehicle").

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
Kanishka was a *Mahayana* Buddhist—he *supported* the *Mahayana* school. The *Third Buddhist Council* (under Ashoka) had *promoted* the *Hinayana* school (the *Theravada*). This is a *critical* distinction for the exam.
[/alert]

**Patronage of Gandhara Art**

Kanishka *patronized* the *Gandhara* school of art—which blended *Greek* and *Indian* elements.

- The *Buddha images* of Gandhara are *Hellenistic* in style—they show the Buddha with *Greek* features (wavy hair, toga-like robes).
- The *Mathura* school (discussed below) was *indigenous*—it showed the Buddha with *Indian* features (thicker lips, fleshy body).

**Kanishka's Legacy:**

- The Kushans *united* the *Silk Road*—from Central Asia to China.
- They *facilitated* the spread of Buddhism to *China* and *Japan*.
- They *brought* Central Asian *cultural* influences into India—the *Buddhist* art of Gandhara was *exported* to China.

[flashcards title="Kushan Empire Essentials"]
| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Founder in India | Kujula Kadphises (30–80 CE) |
| Greatest king | Kanishka (127–150 CE) |
| Saka Era | 78 CE (began by Kanishka) |
| Capital | Purushapura (Peshawar) |
| Fourth Buddhist Council | Kundalvana (Kashmir); presided by Vasumitra |
| Patronage | Gandhara art; Mahayana Buddhism |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 7: Gandhara and Mathura Schools of Art

### Section 1 | The Gandhara School—Hellenistic Synthesis

The *Gandhara* school of art (≈1st century BCE–5th century CE) was a *synthesis* of *Indian* and *Hellenistic* artistic traditions.

**Key Features:**

- *Greek* influence: the *Buddha* image had *wavy hair*, *a pointed nose*, and *classical* drapery (the *toga* style).
- *Indian* influence: the *Buddha* was depicted *seated* in the *lotus* position (dhyana asana).
- The *medium* was *grey-blue schist* (a stone that was *easily* carved).

**Major Centers:**

- *Taxila* (modern Pakistan)
- *Peshawar* (modern Pakistan)
- *Hadda* (Afghanistan)

**The "Hellenistic Buddha":**

The Gandhara school produced the *earliest* images of the Buddha *in human form*. Before Gandhara, the Buddha was *symbolized* by the *dharma-chakra*, the *Bodhi tree*, or the *footprints*.

[alert type="info"]
**Examiner's Focus:**
The *earliest* Buddha images are from the *Gandhara* school—this is a *frequently* tested fact. The *Mathura* school produced *contemporary* Buddha images—but the *style* is *different*: Mathura is *indigenous*, Gandhara is *Hellenistic*.
[/alert]

---

### Section 2 | The Mathura School—Indigenous Tradition

The *Mathura* school of art (≈1st century BCE–3rd century CE) was *contemporary* with Gandhara, but it was *indigenously* Indian.

**Key Features:**

- *Indian* influence: the *Buddha* image had *thicker lips*, *a broader nose*, and *fleshy* body.
- The *medium* was *red sandstone* (the "Mathura red").
- The *style* was *robust*—the images are *heavier* and *more dynamic* than the *serene* Gandhara images.

**Major Centers:**

- *Mathura* (Uttar Pradesh)
- *Bharhut* (Madhya Pradesh)

**The "Yaksha" and "Yakshini" Images:**

The Mathura school also produced *non-Buddhist* images—the *yakshas* and *yakshinis* (nature spirits) of *folk* religion.

**The Hindu-Buddhist Synthesis:**

The Mathura school *influenced* the *later* Gupta art—the *Gupta* Buddha images are *continuations* of the Mathura tradition (not Gandhara).

[flashcards title="Gandhara vs. Mathura Schools"]
| Feature | Gandhara | Mathura |
|---------|----------|---------|
| Influence | Hellenistic (Greek) | Indigenous Indian |
| Medium | Grey-blue schist | Red sandstone |
| Buddha features | Wavy hair, pointed nose | Thicker lips, broader nose |
| Style | Classical, serene | Robust, dynamic |
| Patron | Kushans (especially Kanishka) | Shungas, Kushans |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 8: The Regional Kingdoms

### Section 1 | The North-West: The Indo-Greeks, Shakas, and Kushans

The *north-west* was the *frontier* of India—it was *invaded* repeatedly by *Central Asian* tribes.

- **Indo-Greeks**: Introduced Hellenistic art and coinage.
- **Shakas**: Introduced the *Saka era* (78 CE—though this is *Kanishka's* era, the Shakas also used it).
- **Kushans**: Established the *largest* empire after the Mauryas.

---

### Section 2 | The Deccan: The Satavahanas

The Satavahanas were the *dominant* power in the Deccan—they *controlled* the *trade routes* to the *west*.

- They *traded* with the Romans.
- They *patronized* both *Brahminism* and *Buddhism*.
- They *defeated* the Shakas (Gautamiputra Satakarni) and *lost* to them (Rudradaman).

---

### Section 3 | The South: The Early Tamil Kingdoms

The *south* was *not* conquered by any of the *northern* powers (except the Satavahanas, who *partially* controlled the *Andhra* region).

**The Three Tamil Kingdoms:**

| Kingdom | Capital | Trade |
|---------|---------|-------|
| Chola | Puhar (Kaveripattanam) | Pepper, pearls, silk |
| Chera | Vanji (Karur) | Pepper, precious stones |
| Pandya | Madurai | Pearls, textiles |

**The Sangam Literature:**

The *Sangam* literature (≈3rd century BCE–3rd century CE) is the *earliest* Tamil literature—it is a *body* of poetry, epics, and treatises.

- The *Tolkappiyam* is the *earliest* Tamil grammar.
- The *Silappatikaram* (The Tale of an Anklet) is the *most famous* Tamil epic.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The *Sangam* literature is often *ignored* by students—but the SSC frequently tests the *three Tamil kingdoms* and their *capitals*. Memorize the *Chola* (Puhar), *Chera* (Vanji), and *Pandya* (Madurai).
[/alert]

## Chapter 9: Synthesis—The Cultural Impact of the Foreign Invasions

### Section 1 | The Assimilation of Foreigners

The *foreign* invasions did *not* destroy Indian civilization—they *assimilated* into it.

- The Indo-Greeks *converted* to Buddhism (Menander).
- The Shakas *adopted* Indian *culture*—Rudradaman wrote his inscription in *Sanskrit*.
- The Kushans *patronized* Indian *religions* (Buddhism and Hinduism).

**The Concept of "Foreigner" in Indian Texts:**

- The *Yavanas* (Greeks) were *originally* considered *barbarians*—but by the *Gupta* period, they were *accepted* as *Kshatriyas* (warriors).

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
The *Manusmriti* (the ancient law code) *derogatorily* describes the Yavanas as *fallen* Kshatriyas. But by the *Kushan* period, the *Buddhist* texts *accept* the *Indo-Greek* kings as *legitimate* rulers.
[/alert]

---

### Section 2 | The Examination-Ready Mental Model

1. **Mauryan → Shunga → Kanva**: The *Indian* dynasties.
2. **Indo-Greeks → Shakas → Kushans**: The *foreign* dynasties.
3. **Satavahanas**: The *Deccan* power.
4. **Tamil Kingdoms**: The *south* power.

**The "Cultural Layers" of the Post-Mauryan Period:**

- *Shunga*: Brahminical revival—but *support* for Buddhism.
- *Indo-Greek*: Hellenistic influence—Buddhist conversion.
- *Shaka*: Sanskrit inscriptions—Central Asian *nomadic* culture.
- *Kushan*: Mahayana Buddhism—Gandhara art—Silk Road trade.
- *Satavahana*: Deccan power—Roman trade—Brahminical/Buddhist *coexistence*.

---

### Section 3 | Solved Examples

[example]
**Question:** The *Saka Era* (78 CE) was begun by which king?
- A) Chandragupta Maurya
- B) Kanishka
- C) Rudradaman I
- D) Menander

**Solution:** The correct answer is =B) Kanishka=. The Saka Era is *named* after the Shakas, but it was *begun* by Kanishka (the Kushan king). The *trap*: Students often associate the "Saka Era" with the *Shakas*—but it is *Kanishka* who *initiated* it.
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** Which of the following is a *feature* of the *Gandhara* school of art?
- A) Red sandstone medium
- B) Indian facial features
- C) Greek-style drapery
- D) Buddhist symbolism

**Solution:** The correct answer is =C) Greek-style drapery=. The Gandhara school is *characterized* by Hellenistic features—the *toga*-style robe is a *Greek* influence. The *Mathura* school used *red sandstone* (A) and *Indian* facial features (B). Both schools used Buddhist symbolism (D)—so it is *not* a differentiating feature.
[/example]

### Section 2 | The Examination Mindset

1. **Identify the "Who":** Is the dynasty *Indian* (Shunga, Kanva) or *foreign* (Indo-Greek, Shaka, Kushan)? This is the *first* diagnostic question.

2. **Identify the "What":** What is the *cultural contribution*? Gandhara art (Kushans), Sanskrit inscriptions (Shakas), Buddhist councils (Kanishka, Ashoka).

3. **Identify the "When":** The *chronological sequence*: Mauryas → Shungas → Kanvas → Indo-Greeks → Shakas → Kushans → Satavahanas. This *sequence* is *essential* for the exam.

4. **Avoid the "Isolationist Trap":** India was *not* isolated—the *foreign invasions* brought *new* cultures, *new* artistic styles, and *new* religious currents. The exam tests this *assimilation*—the *synthesis* of Indian and foreign traditions.
