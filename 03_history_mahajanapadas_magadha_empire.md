# History: Mahajanapadas & Magadha Empire

> From tribal republics to the first imperial state—the political consolidation of ancient India and the rise of Magadha as the epicenter of power.

## Chapter 1: The Age of Mahajanapadas—The Political Map Transforms

### Section 1 | The Sixteen Mahajanapadas: A Political Revolution

Around =600 BCE=, the political landscape of the Indian subcontinent underwent a fundamental transformation. The small tribal *janapadas* of the Later Vedic period evolved into larger, more organized territorial states—the *Mahajanapadas*. This transition marks the shift from *kinship-based* polity to *territorial* kingdoms.

**What Makes a Mahajanapada?**

Unlike the earlier tribal polities, a Mahajanapada was characterized by:
- *Defined territory* with fixed boundaries
- *Permanent capital city* (often fortified)
- *Standing army* and *regular taxation*
- *Codified law* and *bureaucratic administration*
- *Monarchical or republican* governance structure

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The terms *Janapada* and *Mahajanapada* are not interchangeable. Janapada = "foothold of the tribe" (kinship-based). Mahajanapada = "great state" (territorial, centralized). The SSC frequently tests this conceptual evolution—do not treat them as synonyms.
[/alert]

**The Sixteen Mahajanapadas: Systematic Survey**

The earliest list of the 16 Mahajanapadas appears in the *Anguttara Nikaya* (Buddhist text) and the *Bhagavati Sutra* (Jain text). This indicates that the concept was well-established by the time of the Buddha (≈6th century BCE).

| Mahajanapada | Capital | Location (Modern) | Political Structure |
|--------------|---------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Magadha | Rajagriha (later Pataliputra) | Bihar | Monarchy |
| Avanti | Ujjayini (or Mahishmati) | Malwa (MP) | Monarchy |
| Kosala | Shravasti | UP | Monarchy |
| Vatsa | Kaushambi | UP (near Allahabad) | Monarchy |
| Gandhara | Takshashila | NWFP (Pakistan) | Monarchy |
| Kashi | Varanasi | UP | Monarchy (later annexed by Kosala) |
| Malla | Kusinara, Pava | UP (Gorakhpur) | Republic (Gana-Sangha) |
| Vajji | Vaishali | Bihar | Republic (Gana-Sangha) |
| Anga | Champa | Bihar (Bhagalpur) | Monarchy (later annexed by Magadha) |
| Kuru | Indraprastha | Haryana/Delhi | Monarchy |
| Panchala | Ahichhatra, Kampilya | UP | Monarchy |
| Surasena | Mathura | UP | Monarchy |
| Chedi | Sothivati (or Shuktimati) | MP/UP border | Monarchy |
| Matsya | Viratanagara | Rajasthan | Monarchy |
| Ashmaka | Pratishthana (Paithan) | Maharashtra (Godavari) | Monarchy |
| Vajji (Vaishali) | Vaishali | Bihar | Republic (Gana-Sangha) |

*Note: The list has variations across sources—the Jain list substitutes some names.*

**The Republics (Gana-Sanghas) vs. Monarchies**

A critical distinction:
- *Monarchies* (16 of the 16, except two) were ruled by a *single king*.
- *Republics* (Gana-Sanghas) were governed by a *council of elders*—the *Vajji* confederacy (with Vaishali as its center) and the *Malla* confederacy were the prominent republican states.

[alert type="info"]
**Examiner's Focus:**
The republics are historically significant because they are the *oldest* known republics in India—predating the Greek city-states. The *Vajji* confederacy was a *league of eight clans*, of which the *Licchavis* were the most powerful. The SSC often asks for the *two republican Mahajanapadas*—Vajji and Malla. Memorize these firmly.
[/alert]

**The Power Struggle: The "Four Great Kingdoms"**

By the 6th century BCE, the political map was dominated by *four major powers*—the *Mahajanapadas* that mattered for imperial consolidation:
1. **Magadha** (east, under Bimbisara)
2. **Kosala** (north, under Prasenajit)
3. **Vatsa** (centre, under Udayana)
4. **Avanti** (west, under Pradyota)

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
These four kingdoms were *contemporaries* of the Buddha. The Buddha spent his early life in Kapilavastu (near Kosala) and his later years in Magadha. The Pali canon contains extensive *diplomatic correspondence* between these kings—showing that the political and religious spheres were *intertwined* in the 6th century.
[/alert]

### Section 2 | The Strategic Importance of Magadha

Why did Magadha—not Kosala or Avanti—become the nucleus of the first Indian empire? The answer lies in *geographic determinism*. Magadha possessed a combination of natural advantages that no other Mahajanapada could match.

**The Four Pillars of Magadhan Power:**

1. **Strategic Location**: Magadha was situated in the *Gangetic plains* with the *Ganga* and *Son* rivers as natural highways. The region was *protected* by the *Vindhyas* in the south and the *Himalayas* in the north.

2. **Iron Ore Resources**: The region of *Rajgir* (ancient Rajagriha) had *iron ore deposits* at what is now *Gaya*. Iron was the *steel* of the ancient world—it made better tools (for agriculture) and better weapons (for warfare).

3. **Riverine Trade Network**: The *Ganga* provided *cheap water transport*—the bulk transportation of *grain*, *timber*, and *metals* was possible on the river. Magadha controlled the *Ganga-Yamuna* confluence—a *choke point* for trade.

4. **Agricultural Surplus**: The fertile *Gangetic alluvium* produced *surplus grain*—which allowed the kings to maintain a *standing army* and *support non-agricultural specialists* (priests, administrators, artisans).

**The Cognitive Model:**

For any empire to form, you need:
- *Surplus food* (to support non-producers)
- *Administrative technology* (to govern large territory)
- *Military technology* (iron weapons > bronze)
- *Transportation networks* (rivers > overland)

Magadha had *all four*.

## Chapter 2: The Magadha Empire—The Three Dynasties

### Section 1 | The Haryanka Dynasty (≈544–412 BCE)

The first historically documented dynasty of Magadha—and the one that began the process of imperial expansion.

**Bimbisara (≈544–492 BCE)**

- **Reign**: 52 years (according to Buddhist tradition)
- **Capital**: Rajagriha (modern Rajgir, Bihar)
- **Conquests**:
  - Annexed *Anga* (with the capital at Champa)—this gave him control over the *Ganges delta* and the *trade route to the east*.
  - Married *Kosala Devi* (sister of King Prasenajit of Kosala)—this *diplomatic marriage* secured the western border.
  - Also married *Chellana* (daughter of the Licchavi chief of Vaishali)—this forged an alliance with the *Vajji* republic.
- **Relationship with the Buddha**: Bimbisara was a *patron* of the Buddha—he donated the *Veluvana* (Bamboo Grove) monastery in Rajagriha.
- **Death**: Assassinated by his son *Ajatashatru*.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
Bimbisara is often confused with *Shrenika*—a title he is given in Jain texts. He was not a "Buddhist" by conversion—he was a *patron* of multiple religious traditions. The SSC tests the *degree* of his religious affiliation—he was *supportive* of both Buddhism and Jainism. Do not oversimplify.
[/alert]

**Ajatashatru (≈492–460 BCE)**

The name means "he whose enemy is unborn"—a curious name for a man who *patricided* his own father.

**Key Conquests:**
- **War with Kosala**: Initially lost; later *captured* and *married* the Kosalan princess (and apparently *released* the Kosalan king).
- **War with Vajji**: The *most significant* war—Ajatashatru used *military engineering* to defeat the republican confederacy. He built a *fort* at the confluence of the Ganga and Son (later to become *Pataliputra*). He also used *two new siege weapons*—the *mahashilakantaka* (a giant catapult throwing stones) and the *rathamushala* (a chariot with a sharp-edged scythe).

**Patronage of Buddhism and Jainism:**
- Ajatashatru is mentioned in both Buddhist and Jain sources.
- The *First Buddhist Council* was held at *Rajagriha* (under his reign, after the Buddha's death).
- He built *stupas* over the Buddha's relics.

**The Shift of Capital to Pataliputra:**
Ajatashatru constructed a *fort* at *Pataligrama* (Pataliputra—modern Patna). The strategic location—at the confluence of the *Ganga*, *Son*, and *Gandaki* rivers—made it *impregnable*. The capital was later shifted permanently to Pataliputra by his son *Udayin*.

**Udayin (≈460–440 BCE)**

- Built the *permanent fortifications* of Pataliputra.
- Erected a *consecrated pillar* (not an Ashokan pillar) at the site.
- The Haryanka dynasty declined after Udayin—a series of *weak kings* led to the *usurpation* of power by the Shishunagas.

[flashcards title="Haryanka Dynasty Essentials"]
| King | Reign | Key Achievement |
|------|-------|-----------------|
| Bimbisara | 544–492 BCE | Annexed Anga; diplomatic marriages; patron of Buddha |
| Ajatashatru | 492–460 BCE | Defeated Vajji; built Pataliputra fort; First Buddhist Council |
| Udayin | 460–440 BCE | Moved capital to Pataliputra permanently |
[/flashcards]

### Section 2 | The Shishunaga Dynasty (≈412–344 BCE)

The Shishunaga dynasty is often *overlooked* in standard histories, but it played a *crucial transitional role*—it expanded Magadhan power *westward* into Avanti (Malwa).

**Shishunaga (≈412–394 BCE)**

- Originally a *viceroy* of the Haryankas at Varanasi.
- He *usurped* the throne and established his own dynasty.
- His significant achievement: *Conquered Avanti* (the Pradyota dynasty)—thus bringing the *western* kingdom into the Magadhan fold.

[alert type="info"]
**Examiner's Focus:**
The conquest of *Avanti* is *critical*—it gave Magadha control over the *Ujjain* trade route to the Arabian Sea. This expanded Magadhan commerce to the *west* (Persia, Arabia). The SSC sometimes tests the *chronological* sequence: Avanti fell to Magadha under the Shishunagas, *not* under Bimbisara.
[/alert]

**Kalasoka (≈394–367 BCE)**

Also called *Kakavarna*.
- He is remembered for the *Second Buddhist Council* held at *Vaishali*.
- The council was convened to resolve *disputes* over monastic discipline (the *Vinaya*).

**The Death of the Shishunagas:**

The last Shishunaga king was *defeated* by the *Nanda* usurper—the Nandas had risen to power in the Magadhan bureaucracy under Kalasoka.

---

### Section 3 | The Nanda Dynasty (≈344–322 BCE)

The Nandas represent the first *imperial* dynasty of India—Mahapadma Nanda is described as the "destroyer of the Kshatriya clans." The Nandas expanded the Magadhan empire from *Bengal* in the east to *Gandhara* in the northwest.

**Mahapadma Nanda (≈344–329 BCE)**

- He was of *Shudra* origin—the first low-born ruler to establish an empire in India (according to the Puranas).
- He defeated *all 16 Mahajanapadas* and consolidated the empire.
- He is called *Ugrasena* ("terrible general") in some texts—suggesting a *military-minded* ruler.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The Puranas describe Mahapadma as *Ekarat* ("sole king") and the "destroyer of all Kshatriya clans." This is *hyperbole*—the Kshatriya clans were not *exterminated*; they were *subjugated*. The SSC tests the *interpretation* of Puranic verses—do not take them literally.
[/alert]

**The Nanda Administration:**

- The Nandas introduced a *systematic taxation* system—a *bureaucracy* that collected revenues from agriculture, trade, and forests.
- They amassed *enormous wealth*—the Puranas state that Mahapadma Nanda stored *900,000 gold coins* in the treasury.

**Dhana Nanda (≈329–322 BCE)**

- The *last Nanda king*.
- He was known for his *avarice*—he demanded *excessive taxes* and was *unpopular* with the people.
- His reign coincided with the *invasion of Alexander the Great* in 326 BCE—he *refused* to support Alexander against the northwestern kings (Porus, Ambhi). This *isolation* allowed Alexander to move through the northwest.
- **Overthrown by**: *Chandragupta Maurya* with the *assistance of Kautilya (Chanakya)*.

[flashcards title="Nanda Dynasty Essentials"]
| King | Reign | Key Achievement |
|------|-------|-----------------|
| Mahapadma Nanda | 344–329 BCE | First empire builder; annexed all Mahajanapadas |
| Dhana Nanda | 329–322 BCE | Last Nanda; contemporary of Alexander; overthrown by Chandragupta |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 3: Alexander's Invasion—The Catalyst from the West

### Section 1 | The Macedonian Advance into India

In =326 BCE=, Alexander the Great crossed the Hindu Kush and marched into the Indian subcontinent. This was not an invasion of the *entire* Indian subcontinent—it was a *campaign* in the northwestern frontier (modern-day Pakistan).

**The Strategy:**

Alexander's goal was to *reach the end of the earth*—the *Ocean* (which he believed was the eastern boundary of the world). He aimed to *conquer the known world*—India was the final frontier.

**The Battle of Hydaspes (Jhelum) =326 BCE=**

This was the *last major battle* fought by Alexander in India—it was against *King Porus* (Puru).

**Key Facts:**
- **Location**: On the banks of the *Hydaspes* (modern Jhelum) in Punjab.
- **Porus's Army**: Included *war elephants*—a weapon Alexander had not encountered before.
- **Alexander's Tactics**: He crossed the river *at night* during a *rainstorm*—the *surprise* attack caught Porus off guard.
- **Outcome**: Alexander *won*—but it was his *most costly victory*. His army *refused* to advance further east (against the Nanda empire) because they had heard rumors of its *enormous army* of 200,000 infantry, 20,000 cavalry, and 2,000 war elephants.

**The Encounter with Porus:**

After the battle, Alexander asked Porus how he wished to be treated. Porus replied: "Like a king." Alexander was *impressed* by this answer and restored Porus's kingdom—making him a *vassal*.

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
The *standard* depiction of Alexander "conquering" India is *incorrect*. He conquered only the *northwestern* part of the subcontinent—the *Nanda empire* (eastern India) remained *untouched*. The SSC tests this *geographical limitation*: Alexander's invasion did not reach Magadha.
[/alert]

**Ambhi of Taxila**

- Ambhi was the king of *Takshashila* (Taxila).
- He *surrendered to Alexander without a fight*—he recognized Alexander's superior military force and offered *tribute and alliance*.
- Ambhi is a *useful* figure for the exam because he represents the *political fragmentation* of the northwest—the kings did not unite against Alexander.

**The Return and Death of Alexander**

- Alexander's army *mutinied* at the *Beas River*—they refused to go further east.
- Alexander then turned south, marched down the Indus, and reached the Indian Ocean.
- He died in Babylon in =323 BCE= (at the age of 32).

---

### Section 2 | The Impact of Alexander's Invasion

**Direct Impact:**

1. **Trade Route Opening**: Alexander's campaign *opened up* the land routes between India and the Hellenistic world—Greek traders and diplomats began to visit India.
2. **Indo-Greek Artistic Influence**: The contact with Greek *sculpture* influenced later Indian art—the *Gandhara* school of art (first century CE) shows *Greek-inspired* drapery, facial features, and classical poses.
3. **Geographical Knowledge**: The Greek accounts (Arrian, Plutarch, Strabo) provided the *earliest* systematic descriptions of Indian geography, flora, and fauna.

**Indirect Impact:**

1. **Political Vacuum**: Alexander's *death* and the withdrawal of his forces created a *power vacuum* in the northwest.
2. **The Mauryan Opportunity**: The *timing* was perfect for Chandragupta Maurya—he used the *chaos* in the northwest to *overthrow* the Nandas and then *defeat* the Macedonian satraps (the Greek governors left behind).

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The SSC often asks: "Did Alexander's invasion directly lead to the Mauryan empire?" The correct answer is *No*—it was the *political vacuum* and the *availability of Macedonian military technology* that aided the Mauryan rise. The *direct* cause was Chandragupta's *political ambition* and *strategic alliance* with Kautilya.
[/alert]

## Chapter 4: The Birth of the Mauryan Empire

### Section 1 | Chandragupta Maurya (≈322–298 BCE)

The founder of the *first imperial dynasty* of India—the Mauryan empire marked the first time *most* of the Indian subcontinent was under a single political authority.

**The Sources:**

- **Megasthenes's *Indica***: Greek ambassador to the Mauryan court—his account is the *primary* source for Chandragupta's court.
- **Kautilya's *Arthashastra***: The *manual* of statecraft—though the authorship is debated, it gives insight into Mauryan administration.
- **Buddhist texts** (Mahavamsa, Dipavamsa): The Buddhist tradition records Chandragupta as a *patron* of the Buddha (in his later years).

**The Overthrow of the Nandas:**

- Chandragupta was *aided* by *Kautilya* (also known as Chanakya—the political strategist).
- The story: Kautilya was *insulted* by Dhana Nanda at a royal feast—he vowed to destroy the Nandas and found a new empire.
- Through *political manipulation* and *military strategy*, Chandragupta defeated Dhana Nanda and ascended the throne of Magadha.

**The Defeat of Seleucus Nicator (≈305 BCE)**

- *Seleucus Nicator*—the Greek general who had taken over the *Persian* empire after Alexander's death—attempted to reclaim the Indian territories Alexander had conquered.
- In ≈305 BCE, Chandragupta *defeated* Seleucus in the *Indus Valley*.
- **The Treaty**: Seleucus ceded to Chandragupta the territories of *Arachosia* (Kandahar), *Gedrosia* (Balochistan), and *Paropanisadae* (Kabul). In return, Chandragupta gave Seleucus *500 war elephants*.
- The treaty was sealed by a *marriage alliance*—a Greek princess was given to Chandragupta (or his son Bindusara).

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
The *war elephants* Chandragupta gave to Seleucus became *crucial* for the Greek army—the Seleucid empire used them in battles against the *Ptolemies* in Egypt. This is a classic example of *inter-civilizational exchange*—the elephants from India became the *tanks* of the Hellenistic world.
[/alert]

**The Administration of Chandragupta:**

- **Centralized bureaucracy**: The empire was governed through *officials* appointed by the king.
- **Provincial administration**: The empire was divided into *provinces* (with capitals at *Taxila*, *Ujjain*, and *Tosali*).
- **Municipal administration**: *Pataliputra* was governed by a *city council* of 30 members (in 6 committees of 5 each)—this is described in detail by *Megasthenes*.

**The Later Years: Jainism**

- Chandragupta became a *Jain* in his old age (according to the Jain tradition).
- He abdicated the throne and traveled to *Shravanabelagola* (in Karnataka) where he performed *sallekhana* (ritual death by fasting).
- He is said to have starved himself to death—a practice of *Jain asceticism*.

[flashcards title="Chandragupta Maurya Essentials"]
| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Reign | 322–298 BCE |
| Mentor | Kautilya (Chanakya) |
| Overthrew | Dhana Nanda (last Nanda) |
| Defeated | Seleucus Nicator (305 BCE) |
| Territories gained | Kandahar, Balochistan, Kabul |
| Later religion | Jainism (died at Shravanabelagola) |
[/flashcards]

### Section 2 | The Mauryan Administrative System

The Mauryan empire was *centralized*—all power emanated from the king. But it was *not autocratic*—a complex bureaucracy governed the territories.

**The Core Bureaucratic Structure:**

- **Mantriparishad**: The king's council of ministers.
- **Purohita**: Chief priest (spiritual advisor).
- **Senapati**: Commander-in-chief.
- **Sannidhatri**: Treasurer.
- **Samahartri**: Chief collector of revenue (the *tax-collector*).

**The Provincial Administration:**

The empire was divided into *four provinces* (with governors called *Kumara*—often royal princes):

| Province | Capital | Region |
|----------|---------|--------|
| Uttarapatha | Taxila | Northwest (Punjab, Gandhara) |
| Avantipatha | Ujjain | Central-west (Malwa) |
| Prachyapatha | Toshali | Eastern (Orissa, Bengal) |
| Dakshinapatha | Suvarnagiri | Southern (Deccan) |

**The Municipal Administration of Pataliputra:**

Megasthenes gives a detailed account of the *city administration*:
- Six committees of five members each:
  1. **Supervision of public works**
  2. **Registration of foreigners** (visitors and traders)
  3. **Collection of taxes**
  4. **Record of births and deaths** (census)
  5. **Supervision of commerce**
  6. **Maintenance of public buildings**

[alert type="info"]
**Examiner's Focus:**
The *registration of foreigners* committee is a *unique* feature of Mauryan administration—it indicates that Pataliputra was a *cosmopolitan city* with *international trade*.
[/alert]

### Section 3 | The Economy and Society of the Mauryan Age

**Agriculture: The Backbone**

The Mauryan state collected taxes on *all land*—the tax rate was approximately *one-fourth to one-sixth of the produce*.

**Trade and Commerce:**

- *Long-distance trade* with the Hellenistic world—Indian goods (cotton, silk, spices, ivory) reached the Mediterranean.
- *Standardized currency*: The Mauryan coinage (silver *punch-marked* coins) circulated across the empire.
- *Trade routes*: The *Uttarapatha* (the northern highway) and the *Dakshinapatha* (the southern highway) connected the provinces.

**The Social Structure:**

- The *varna* system was *rigidified*—the *Brahmins* and *Kshatriyas* were the elite.
- The *Shudras* were *excluded* from public rituals and land ownership.
- *Slavery* existed—but it was not as widespread as in the Roman empire.

---

### Section 4 | Kautilya's *Arthashastra*—The Manual of Empire

The *Arthashastra* (literally "the science of wealth") is the *treatise* on statecraft attributed to Kautilya.

**The Core Teachings:**

1. **The King's Role**: The king is the *protector* of the people; he must *ensure the welfare* of his subjects.
2. **The Seven Elements of State**: King, Minister, Country, Fortress, Treasury, Army, and Ally.
3. **Danda (Punishment)**: The state must enforce *law and order*—the king who uses *excessive* punishment is hated; the king who uses *too little* is despised.
4. **Espionage**: Kautilya recommends an elaborate *spy network*—the king must have *agents* in every province and every foreign court.

**The Arthashastra and Modern Statecraft:**

Kautilya's principles are *realpolitik*—they are *pragmatic*, not *moral*. His advice to the king: "The enemy is any neighboring state, regardless of its intentions."

## Chapter 5: Synthesis—From Mahajanapadas to Empire

### Section 1 | The Trajectory of Political Power

The movement from the *tribal republics* of the Vedic age to the *territorial empires* of the Mauryas represents a *logical progression*:

1. **Rigvedic Janas**: Tribal, mobile, leader-centered
2. **Later Vedic Janapadas**: Territorial, but still clan-centered
3. **Mahajanapadas**: Centralized, tax-based, military
4. **Magadhan Hegemony**: Conquest of the periphery
5. **Mauryan Empire**: Integration of all regions under a single authority

**The Examination-Ready Mental Model:**

- *Mahajanapadas* = the political *evolution* from tribes to states.
- *Magadha* = the *geographic beneficiary* of the Gangetic plain.
- *Alexander* = the *catalyst* for northwest instability.
- *Chandragupta* = the *strategic opportunist* who united the subcontinent.

### Section 2 | Solved Examples

[example]
**Question:** Which of the following Mahajanapadas was a *republic* (Gana-Sangha)?
1. Vajji
2. Malla
3. Kosala
4. Avanti

- A) 1 and 2 only
- B) 1, 2, and 3 only
- C) 2 and 4 only
- D) 1, 3, and 4 only

**Solution:** The correct answer is =A) 1 and 2 only=. Vajji (centered at Vaishali) and Malla (centered at Kusinara) were the *only* republican Mahajanapadas. Kosala and Avanti were *monarchies*. The trap: students often assume all Mahajanapadas were monarchies—this is a *frequent* exam error.
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** The *Second Buddhist Council* was convened during the reign of which king?
- A) Bimbisara
- B) Ajatashatru
- C) Kalasoka
- D) Dhana Nanda

**Solution:** The correct answer is =C) Kalasoka=. The First Buddhist Council was held at Rajagriha under Ajatashatru. The Second Buddhist Council was held at Vaishali under Kalasoka (the Shishunaga king). The trap: students often confuse the *council sequence*. Bimbisara (First Council), Kalasoka (Second Council), Ashoka (Third Council)—memorize the *sequence* of Buddhist councils as a *chronological anchor*.
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** Consider the following statements about Alexander's invasion:
1. He conquered the entire Indian subcontinent.
2. The Battle of Hydaspes was fought against King Porus.
3. His army refused to advance beyond the Beas River.

Which of the above statements is/are correct?
- A) 1 and 2 only
- B) 2 and 3 only
- C) 1 and 3 only
- D) 1, 2, and 3

**Solution:** The correct answer is =B) 2 and 3 only=. Statement 1 is false—Alexander conquered only the *northwestern* part of India; he never reached the Ganga valley or Magadha. Statement 2 is correct—the Battle of Hydaspes was against Porus. Statement 3 is correct—his army mutinied at the Beas. The trap: Alexander's *legend* often inflates his conquests—the exam tests the *factual limit* of his campaign.
[/example]

### Section 2 | The Examination Mindset

1. **Identify the "What":** Read the question: Is it asking about a *Mahajanapada*, a *dynasty*, or a *battle*? Your mental checklist: *Time*, *Place*, *Person*, *Event*.
2. **Identify the "When":** The chronology is *linear*: Mahajanapadas → Haryanka → Shishunaga → Nanda → Maurya. Memorize this *sequence* as a *chain*—one link leads to the next.
3. **Identify the "Where":** The geography of Magadha is *specific*—Rajagriha (early), Pataliputra (later). Avanti (west), Kosala (north), Vajji (east). Map the *direction* of conquest in your mind.
4. **Avoid the "Legendary Trap":** Alexander is *not* a conqueror of India—he only reached the *Beas*. The Nandas were *not* conquered by Alexander. Chandragupta *defeated* the Nandas *after* Alexander withdrew.
