# Embedded Figures

> The visual puzzle of concealment: training the eye to detect a shape that is perfectly camouflaged within a larger, more complex design. This is not about recognizing figures; it is about systematic scanning with precise mental templates.

## Chapter 1: The Foundations of Embedded Figure Logic

### Section 1 | What Is an Embedded Figure?

Embedded figures questions present a complex design (often a geometric or abstract pattern) and ask you to identify which of several simpler figures is "hidden" or "embedded" within it. The hidden figure may be rotated, scaled, or have its color changed, but its shape must be an exact subset of the larger design.

**The Core Principle**: The embedded figure must be an *exact match* in shape and proportion to the original figure, but it may appear at any orientation or scale. The exam tests your ability to ignore irrelevant details and focus on the underlying skeleton of shapes.

**The Cognitive Shift**: When scanning for an embedded figure, do not look for the entire figure as a whole. Instead, look for its *key vertices and edges*—the critical anchor points that define the shape. Once you find a matching angle or segment, the rest of the figure will follow.

[alert type="danger"]
**The Rotation Factor**: Many students miss embedded figures because they assume the figure is oriented the same way as the original. The embedded figure can be rotated by any angle, or even flipped. Always consider all possible orientations.
[/alert]

### Section 2 | The Components of a Figure

Every geometric figure can be broken down into:
- **Vertices**: The corners or intersection points.
- **Edges**: The lines connecting vertices.
- **Angles**: The turns between edges.

The hidden figure is found by tracing these components within the complex design. If all components match, the figure is embedded.

## Chapter 2: Pattern Overlay and Recognition

### Section 1 | The Mental Overlay Method

To determine if a figure is embedded in a design:
1. **Mentally overlay** the figure onto the complex design.
2. **Check if the figure's lines** align perfectly with lines in the design.
3. **Check if the figure's vertices** coincide with intersection points in the design.
4. **Ensure no extra lines** are required to form the figure.

**The Trap**: Some designs may contain similar but not identical shapes. The embedded figure must be an *exact* match. If the figure requires a line that is not present, it is not embedded.

### Section 2 | Scanning Strategy

| Step | Action | Purpose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Identify distinctive vertices | Find where the shape's corners might be |
| 2 | Trace the longest edge | Use a prominent edge as an anchor |
| 3 | Check adjacent edges | Verify the rest of the shape |
| 4 | Consider rotations | Try angles 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°, 180° |

The most efficient scanning strategy is to start with the *most distinctive feature* of the target figure (e.g., a long diagonal line, a right angle, or a unique intersection). Then look for that feature in the complex design.

[alert type="success"]
**The Edge Anchoring**: Find the longest or most unique edge in the figure. Then search for that exact edge length and orientation in the design. Once found, the rest of the figure can be traced from that anchor.
[/alert]

| Rotation Allowed | The figure may be rotated | Any angle is possible |

## Chapter 3: Finding a Hidden Figure

### Section 1 | The Systematic Search

When asked to find which figure is hidden in a complex design, use this systematic approach:

1. **Examine each option** separately.
2. **For each option**, identify its key features (longest edge, right angles, symmetry).
3. **Scan the design** for those features.
4. **Trace the figure** within the design.
5. **If a complete match is found**, that is the answer.

**Example**: A complex line drawing with many intersecting lines. The target figure is a triangle. The hidden triangle might be formed by lines that are already part of the design.

### Section 2 | The Intersection Point Method

A hidden figure is often formed by the *intersection points* of the design's existing lines. Instead of scanning for whole shapes, scan for vertices:

1. **Mark all intersection points** in the design.
2. **Connect points** that match the target figure's pattern.
3. **Check if the connection lines** are part of the design.

[alert type="warning"]
**The Overlap Illusion**: Sometimes, lines that appear to form a shape are actually separate lines that cross. The embedded figure must be formed by continuous lines or segments that are part of the design, not by accidental alignments of separate components.
[/alert]

[flashcards title="Scanning Techniques"]
| Technique | When to Use | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Edge Anchoring | Figure has a unique edge | Long diagonal in a square |
| Vertex Anchoring | Figure has unique corners | Right angle in a triangle |
| Symmetry Matching | Figure is symmetrical | Find one half, mirror the rest |
| Exclusion | Figure is not present | Eliminate options one by one |
[/flashcards]

[example]
**Question:** In the given complex design, which of the following figures is embedded? (Options: triangle, square, pentagon, circle)
**Solution:**
Step 1: Examine the design. Look for shapes formed by existing lines.
Step 2: Triangle: Are there any triangles? Look for three lines intersecting to form a closed shape. Yes, there is a triangle in the upper-left corner.
Step 3: Square: Look for four lines forming a closed shape with right angles. None are clearly visible.
Step 4: Pentagon: Five-sided closed shape? Not visible.
Step 5: Circle: Is there a curved shape? No, all lines are straight.
Step 6: The triangle is embedded.
**Final Answer:** Triangle.
[/example]

## Chapter 4: Figure Inside Another Figure

### Section 1 | The Nested Figure Problem

Sometimes the question shows a large figure and asks how many times a smaller figure is contained within it. This requires counting occurrences of the smaller shape within the larger.

**Example**: How many triangles are there in a given pentagon with diagonals drawn?

**The Counting Strategy**:
1. **Start with the smallest triangles** (formed by single segments).
2. **Combine small triangles** to form larger ones.
3. **Use a systematic labeling** of vertices to avoid missing shapes.

### Section 2 | The Vertex Combination Method

To count figures inside another:
1. **Label all vertices** of the larger figure.
2. **List all possible combinations** of vertices that could form the target figure.
3. **Check if the corresponding edges exist**.
4. **Count the valid combinations**.

[alert type="info"]
**The Overlap in Counting**: When counting figures, some shapes may overlap. Each distinct set of vertices counts as a separate figure. For example, two triangles that share a side but have different third vertices are counted as two triangles.
[/alert]

[flashcards title="Counting Figures Inside a Figure"]
| Figure Type | Counting Approach | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Triangles | Count by size: 1, 2, 3... | A large triangle divided into smaller ones |
| Squares | Count by size and orientation | A grid pattern |
| Rectangles | Count rows and columns | A matrix of lines |
| General | Label vertices, check combinations | Any polygon |
[/flashcards]

[example]
**Question:** How many triangles are there in a large triangle divided by a line from the apex to the base?
**Solution:**
Step 1: The large triangle has one line from the apex to the base, dividing it into two smaller triangles.
Step 2: Count: 2 smaller triangles + 1 large triangle = 3 triangles.
Step 3: If there are multiple lines, use the vertex combination method.
**Final Answer:** 3 triangles.
[/example]
