# Figure Analogy (Non-Verbal)

> The visual equation: deciphering the transformation that links one figure to another, and applying that same transformation to a third figure. This is not about recognizing shapes; it is about mapping structural relationships from one visual domain to another.

## Chapter 1: The Foundations of Visual Relations

### Section 1 | What Is a Figure Analogy?

A figure analogy presents two pairs of figures: a complete pair (Figure A and Figure B) showing a transformation, and an incomplete pair (Figure C and Figure D) where Figure D is missing. Your task is to identify the transformation rule and select the option that completes the second pair.

**The Core Principle**: The relationship between Figure A and Figure B is identical to the relationship between Figure C and Figure D. The transformation rule is consistent across both pairs.

**The Cognitive Shift**: When solving figure analogies, do not just compare the figures visually. Instead, analyze the *relationship*—what changed from A to B? Then apply that same change to C to find D.

[alert type="danger"]
**The Direct Matching Trap**: Many students try to match Figure D to Figure A or B directly, rather than applying the transformation rule. The relationship is the key, not the visual similarity.
[/alert]

### Section 2 | The Analogy Structure

Every figure analogy has three components:
1. **Figure A**: The starting figure of the first pair.
2. **Figure B**: The result after applying the rule to Figure A.
3. **Figure C**: The starting figure of the second pair.
4. **Figure D (missing)**: The result after applying the same rule to Figure C.

The rule can be thought of as a function: `f(A) = B`. You must find `f(C) = D`.

## Chapter 2: Shape Based Transformation

### Section 1 | Direct Shape Modification

In shape-based transformations, the shape itself changes—its size, orientation, number of sides, or internal features.

**Common Shape Transformations**:
- **Scaling**: The shape grows or shrinks.
- **Rotation**: The shape turns.
- **Addition**: A new element is added.
- **Deletion**: An element is removed.
- **Replacement**: One element replaces another.

**Example**:
- Figure A: A small circle.
- Figure B: A large circle.
- Transformation: Scaling (size increases).
- Figure C: A small square.
- Figure D: A large square.

### Section 2 | Internal Feature Transformation

The transformation may involve changes *inside* the shape—lines, dots, shading, or patterns.

**Example**:
- Figure A: A circle with a horizontal line.
- Figure B: A circle with a vertical line.
- Transformation: The line rotates 90°.
- Figure C: A square with a horizontal line.
- Figure D: A square with a vertical line.

[alert type="success"]
**The Feature Mapping**: Break each figure into its components: outer shape, inner elements, shading, lines, and dots. The transformation will typically affect one or more of these components.
[/alert]

[flashcards title="Shape Transformation Rules"]
| Transformation | Example (A → B) | Example (C → D) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Scaling | Small circle → Large circle | Small square → Large square |
| Rotation | Triangle up → Triangle right | Triangle left → Triangle down |
| Shape Change | Circle → Square | Triangle → Pentagon |
| Line Addition | Circle → Circle with line | Square → Square with line |
| Shading | White → Black | Striped → Checkered |
[/flashcards]

[example]
**Question:** Figure A: A circle with a dot inside. Figure B: A circle with a dot outside. Figure C: A square with a dot inside. What is Figure D?
**Solution:**
Step 1: Identify the transformation from A to B. The dot moves from inside the circle to outside.
Step 2: Apply the same transformation to Figure C. The dot moves from inside the square to outside.
Step 3: Figure D is a square with a dot outside.
**Final Answer:** Square with a dot outside.
[/example]

## Chapter 3: Pattern Based Comparison

### Section 1 | Structural Pattern Recognition

Pattern-based analogies involve repeating or alternating elements. The transformation rule may be about the arrangement of multiple elements.

**Example**:
- Figure A: A row of three circles: ○ ○ ○
- Figure B: A row of three circles: ● ● ●
- Transformation: All circles become filled.
- Figure C: A row of three squares: □ □ □
- Figure D: A row of three squares: ■ ■ ■

### Section 2 | Alternating and Repeating Patterns

Sometimes the transformation involves changing the pattern order or alternating between two states.

**Example**:
- Figure A: ○ ● ○ (circle, filled circle, circle)
- Figure B: ● ○ ● (filled circle, circle, filled circle)
- Transformation: The positions of filled and unfilled circles swap.
- Figure C: □ ■ □
- Figure D: ■ □ ■

**The Trap**: Patterns may involve multiple variables (shape, shading, size, position). Track each variable separately.

[alert type="info"]
**The Variable Decomposition**: For complex patterns, create a table listing each variable for each position. This makes the pattern immediately visible.
[/alert]

[flashcards title="Pattern Transformation Rules"]
| Pattern Type | Description | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Repeating | Same element repeats | ○ ○ ○ → ● ● ● |
| Alternating | Elements alternate | ○ ● ○ → ● ○ ● |
| Symmetric | Mirror image pattern | ○ ● ○ → ○ ● ○ |
| Shifting | Pattern shifts left or right | ○ ● ○ → ● ○ ○ |
[/flashcards]

[example]
**Question:** Figure A: A 2×2 grid with circles in top-left and bottom-right. Figure B: A 2×2 grid with circles in top-right and bottom-left. Figure C: A 2×2 grid with squares in top-left and bottom-right. What is Figure D?
**Solution:**
Step 1: Identify the transformation from A to B. The circles moved from the main diagonal to the anti-diagonal. This is a reflection across the vertical axis.
Step 2: Apply the same transformation to Figure C. The squares move from the main diagonal to the anti-diagonal.
Step 3: Figure D: A 2×2 grid with squares in top-right and bottom-left.
**Final Answer:** 2×2 grid with squares in top-right and bottom-left.
[/example]

## Chapter 4: Rotation Based Transformation

### Section 1 | Single Element Rotation

In rotation-based analogies, one or more elements rotate by a fixed angle. The rotation may be of the entire figure or of individual components.

**Example**:
- Figure A: An arrow pointing up.
- Figure B: An arrow pointing right.
- Transformation: 90° clockwise rotation.
- Figure C: An arrow pointing left.
- Figure D: An arrow pointing up (90° CW from left is up).

### Section 2 | Multiple Element Rotation

When multiple elements rotate, each may have its own rotation angle, or they may rotate together.

**Example**:
- Figure A: A square with a dot at the top and a line at the bottom.
- Figure B: A square with a dot at the right and a line at the left.
- Transformation: Both the dot and the line rotate 90° clockwise.

[alert type="warning"]
**The Rotation Center**: For figures with multiple components, identify the center of rotation. Is it the center of the entire figure, or does each component rotate around its own center?
[/alert]

[flashcards title="Rotation Rules for Analogies"]
| Type | Description | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Whole Figure | Entire figure rotates | Square with dot → Square with dot rotated |
| Component | Individual part rotates | Dot moves, square stays |
| Multiple | Multiple parts rotate | Dot rotates 90°, line rotates 90° |
| Mixed | Different parts rotate differently | Dot rotates 90°, line rotates 180° |
[/flashcards]

[example]
**Question:** Figure A: A circle with a line pointing up and a dot on the left. Figure B: A circle with a line pointing right and a dot on the bottom. Figure C: A circle with a line pointing down and a dot on the right. What is Figure D?
**Solution:**
Step 1: Identify the transformation from A to B. The line rotates 90° clockwise (up → right). The dot moves 90° clockwise (left → bottom).
Step 2: Apply the same transformation to Figure C. The line rotates 90° clockwise (down → left). The dot moves 90° clockwise (right → top).
Step 3: Figure D: A circle with a line pointing left and a dot on top.
**Final Answer:** Circle with a line pointing left and a dot on top.
[/example]

Q: Figure A: A circle. Figure B: A circle with a dot inside. Figure C: A square. What is Figure D?
- A) Square with a dot inside (Correct: The transformation is adding a dot inside the shape.)
- B) Circle with a dot outside
- C) Square with a dot outside
- D) Circle

Q: Figure A: A triangle pointing up. Figure B: A triangle pointing down. Figure C: A pentagon pointing up. What is Figure D?
- A) Pentagon pointing down (Correct: The transformation is a 180° rotation.)
- B) Pentagon pointing up
- C) Triangle pointing down
- D) Square pointing down

Q: Figure A: A 2×2 grid with a circle in the top-left. Figure B: A 2×2 grid with a circle in the bottom-right. Figure C: A 2×2 grid with a square in the top-left. What is Figure D?
- A) 2×2 grid with a square in the bottom-right (Correct: The circle moved diagonally from top-left to bottom-right. The square should do the same.)
- B) 2×2 grid with a square in the top-right
- C) 2×2 grid with a square in the bottom-left
- D) 2×2 grid with a circle in the bottom-right

Q: Figure A: A circle with a horizontal line. Figure B: A circle with a vertical line. Figure C: A square with a horizontal line. What is Figure D?
- A) Square with a vertical line (Correct: The transformation is rotating the line 90°.)
- B) Square with a horizontal line
- C) Circle with a vertical line
- D) Square with a diagonal line

Q: Figure A: A triangle with a dot on the left. Figure B: A triangle with a dot on the right. Figure C: A circle with a dot on the left. What is Figure D?
- A) Circle with a dot on the right (Correct: The dot moves from left to right.)
- B) Circle with a dot on the top
- C) Circle with a dot on the bottom
- D) Triangle with a dot on the right
[/quiz]
