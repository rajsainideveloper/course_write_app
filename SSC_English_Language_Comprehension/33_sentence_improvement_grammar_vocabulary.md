# Sentence Improvement (Grammar & Vocabulary)

> The surgeon's hand: precise replacements that excise the diseased and graft the correct, transforming ailing sentences into robust, flawless prose.

## Chapter 33: The Art of Precision Replacement

### Section 1 | The Cognitive Framework for Sentence Improvement

Sentence improvement questions present you with a sentence containing an underlined portion. Your task: select the option that best replaces the underlined segment. The critical insight that separates top scorers from the rest: =The correct answer is not merely 'grammatically correct'—it must be the *most appropriate* replacement given the context, style, and intended meaning=.

The Three-Dimensional Analysis

Each sentence improvement question demands evaluation along three axes:

1. **Grammatical Correctness**: Does the replacement violate any grammatical rule? Subject-verb agreement, tense logic, preposition usage, article application, and parallel structure must all be flawless.
2. **Semantic Precision**: Does the replacement convey the exact meaning intended by the original sentence? A grammatically correct option that shifts meaning is *wrong*.
3. **Stylistic Economy**: Is the replacement concise, elegant, and free from redundancy? In SSC exams, the shortest clear option is often correct—but not always. There is a fine line between concision and incompleteness.

The Cognitive Protocol

When approaching a sentence improvement question:

1. **Read the original sentence in full**—not just the underlined portion. Context from the non-underlined segments often dictates the correct choice.
2. **Diagnose the error**. Do not look at options immediately. Name the violation: Is it a tense error? Preposition error? Redundancy? Wrong word?
3. **Formulate your own correction** before examining options. This mental generation prevents options from contaminating your judgment.
4. **Scan options systematically**. Eliminate those with obvious errors. Compare remaining options for nuance.
5. **If 'No improvement' is an option**, test it rigorously. Do not default to changing a sentence that is already correct.

[alert type="warning"]
**Cognitive Bias Alert**: Students often assume the underlined portion *must* contain an error. This bias causes them to select a grammatically correct option when 'No improvement' is the right answer. SSC questions deliberately include sentences with no error. Treat 'No improvement' as a legitimate, frequently correct option.
[/alert]

### Section 2 | The 'No Improvement' Decision

The most psychologically challenging option is 'No improvement' (or 'No correction required'). Examiners exploit your assumption that something must be wrong.

When to Select 'No Improvement'

The original portion is correct when:

- All grammatical rules are satisfied.
- The wording is clear, concise, and idiomatic.
- Alternatives, while grammatically possible, introduce awkwardness or shift meaning.

[example]
**Question**: The company has decided to lay off (A) / off (B) / away (C) / No improvement (D) several hundred employees.

**Solution**: The underlined portion is 'to lay off'. This is the standard idiom for terminating employment. 'Lay off' is correct; 'off' is already the correct preposition. 'Lay away' means to reserve or store. 'Lay' without 'off' is incomplete. Therefore, the answer is **No improvement**.
[/example]

[example]
**Question**: He is working here since 2015.

**Options**: (A) has been working (B) worked (C) had worked (D) No improvement

**Solution**: The original 'is working since 2015' is incorrect—'since' requires the present perfect or present perfect continuous tense. Option A ('has been working') is correct. 'No improvement' is wrong because the original is grammatically flawed.
[/example]

[alert type="info"]
**Strategic Rule**: If the underlined portion reads naturally and there is no clear grammatical violation, scrutinize 'No improvement' carefully. It is correct more often than candidates expect—approximately 15-20% of sentence improvement questions have 'No improvement' as the answer.
[/alert]

### Section 3 | Grammar-Based Improvement: The Core Categories

The majority of sentence improvement questions target specific grammatical structures. The categories align closely with spotting error topics, but the format differs: you must select the best replacement rather than merely identify an error.

**Subject-Verb Agreement Improvements**

The same principles apply, but now you must select the correct verb form from multiple options.

[example]
**Question**: The list of candidates who have applied for the post are quite long.

**Options**: (A) is quite long (B) are quite lengthy (C) have been quite long (D) No improvement

**Solution**: The subject is 'list' (singular), not 'candidates'. The verb should be 'is'. Option A corrects the SVA error. Option B changes 'long' to 'lengthy' unnecessarily. Option C introduces the wrong tense. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

**Tense Sequence Improvements**

Errors in tense sequencing require selecting the correct temporal relationship.

[example]
**Question**: I will call you as soon as the meeting will end.

**Options**: (A) ends (B) ended (C) would end (D) No improvement

**Solution**: In time clauses introduced by 'as soon as', the present tense is used to express future time. The correct replacement is 'ends'. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

**Preposition Collocation Improvements**

These questions test your knowledge of fixed preposition pairings.

[example]
**Question**: She is very much fond in classical music.

**Options**: (A) fond of (B) fond for (C) fond at (D) No improvement

**Solution**: The adjective 'fond' is always followed by the preposition 'of'. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

**Article Application Improvements**

Article errors often appear in improvement questions, testing both omission and incorrect selection.

[example]
**Question**: He is a honest man who never tells lie.

**Options**: (A) an honest man who never tells a lie (B) honest man who never tells lie (C) a honest man who never tells a lie (D) No improvement

**Solution**: 'Honest' begins with a vowel sound, so 'an' is required. 'Lie' in the singular requires an article—'a lie' or 'lies' in the plural. Option A corrects both errors. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

**Modal Verb Improvements**

Modal auxiliaries must be selected based on the degree of necessity, possibility, or permission required by the context.

[example]
**Question**: You can take the examination if you have submitted your fees.

**Options**: (A) may (B) should (C) will (D) No improvement

**Solution**: The sentence expresses permission, not ability. 'May' is the appropriate modal for formal permission. However, in modern usage 'can' is often used informally for permission. SSC patterns typically prefer 'may' for formal permission contexts. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

[flashcards title="High-Frequency Grammar Improvement Categories"]
| Category | Common Errors | Corrective Pattern |
| SVA | Singular subject with plural verb | Match verb number to actual subject |
| Tense Sequence | Future tense in time/condition clauses | Use present tense for future time |
| Preposition | Wrong/missing preposition after verb/adjective | Memorize fixed collocations |
| Articles | A/An confusion; missing 'the' | Phonological rule; specific vs. general |
| Modals | Wrong modal for context | Match degree of necessity/permission |
| Pronouns | Case error; ambiguous reference | Subject vs. object; clear antecedent |
[/flashcards]

### Section 4 | Vocabulary-Based Improvement: Word Choice and Context

Vocabulary-based improvement questions require more than grammatical knowledge—they demand sensitivity to nuance, register, and contextual appropriateness.

**The Wrong Word Phenomenon**

Examiners select words that are either:

- **Near-synonyms** with different implications (e.g., 'reduce' vs. 'curtail')
- **Words with similar forms** but different meanings (e.g., 'affect' vs. 'effect')
- **Words that collocate incorrectly** with surrounding words

[example]
**Question**: The Government has taken several steps to lesson the impact of the economic downturn.

**Options**: (A) lessen (B) reduce (C) alleviate (D) No improvement

**Solution**: The original 'lesson' is a noun meaning a teaching session. The intended verb is 'lessen' meaning to reduce. Option A is the direct correction. Options B and C are also semantically possible but 'lessen' is the most direct and idiomatic replacement for the wrong word. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

[example]
**Question**: His behavior in the meeting was very childish and caused much embarrassment.

**Options**: (A) childlike (B) juvenile (C) immature (D) No improvement

**Solution**: The original 'childish' means immature and often carries a negative connotation. 'Childlike' is positive, suggesting innocence. 'Juvenile' can be neutral or negative. 'Immature' is more formal. The original is correct in its context (a critical description of behavior). The answer is **No improvement**.
[/example]

**Context Inappropriate Usage**

Even a grammatically correct word may be wrong if it doesn't fit the context. The key is understanding register—formal vs. informal, technical vs. general, literal vs. figurative.

[example]
**Question**: The scientist talked about the experiment in a very simple manner so that the audience could grab the concept.

**Options**: (A) grasp (B) catch (C) hold (D) No improvement

**Solution**: 'Grab' is too casual and physical for understanding an abstract concept. 'Grasp' is the standard verb for mental comprehension. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

**The Collocation Constraint**

Some word pairs are simply fixed by usage. You cannot arbitrarily substitute synonyms when they violate collocational patterns.

| Verb | Correct Object | Common Error |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| Make | a decision, an effort, a mistake | Do (wrong collocation) |
| Do | business, good, harm | Make (wrong collocation) |
| Take | a decision (British), responsibility | Make a decision (American—both acceptable) |
| Pay | attention, a compliment, a visit | Give attention (less idiomatic) |
| Catch | a cold, a bus, sight of | Get a cold (less formal) |

[alert type="warning"]
**Examination Strategy**: When you see a common verb collocation error, the correct option will often maintain the verb but change the object, or change the verb but keep the object. Test each option against standard usage.
[/alert]

### Section 5 | Style and Clarity: Eliminating Awkwardness

Style-based improvement questions test your ability to recognize and correct sentences that are grammatically acceptable but awkward, unclear, or convoluted.

**The Active-Passive Transformation**

Passive voice is not inherently wrong. However, overuse or inappropriate use creates wordiness and vagueness. The active voice is generally preferred for clarity and concision—but only when the agent is known and relevant.

[example]
**Question**: The decision was made by the committee to postpone the meeting.

**Options**: (A) The committee decided to postpone the meeting (B) The meeting was postponed by the committee (C) Postponement was the decision of the committee (D) No improvement

**Solution**: The original is grammatically correct but passive and wordy. Option A is active, concise, and clearer. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

[alert type="info"]
**When to Keep Passive**: If the agent is unknown or unimportant ('The building was built in 1890'), passive is appropriate. If the sentence focuses on the recipient of the action ('The patient was examined thoroughly'), passive may be preferred. Context dictates.
[/alert]

**Dangling and Misplaced Modifiers**

Modifiers must be placed close to the words they modify. Misplaced modifiers create absurd or ambiguous sentences.

[example]
**Question**: Walking through the park, the flowers were beautiful.

**Options**: (A) Walking through the park, I saw the beautiful flowers (B) The flowers were beautiful when I was walking through the park (C) Walking through the park, the beautiful flowers were seen (D) No improvement

**Solution**: The original creates a dangling modifier—the flowers were not walking. Option A corrects by making 'I' the subject of 'walking'. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

**Word Order and Sentence Flow**

English follows a relatively fixed word order (SVO). Inversion, fronting, and other variations must be used purposefully, not inadvertently.

[example]
**Question**: Never I have seen such a magnificent view.

**Options**: (A) Never have I seen (B) Never I saw (C) Never did I saw (D) No improvement

**Solution**: Inversion is required after a negative adverb placed at the beginning of the sentence. The correct structure is 'Never + auxiliary + subject + verb'. Option A is correct. Option C uses 'did' but incorrectly uses 'saw' (should be 'see'). The answer is **A**.
[/example]

### Section 6 | Redundancy and Wordiness: The Economy Principle

The principle of concision: =A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, and a paragraph no unnecessary sentences=. Redundancy is a frequent target in sentence improvement questions.

**Common Redundancies**

| Redundant Phrase | Correct Version |
|------------------|-----------------|
| Return back | Return |
| Repeat again | Repeat |
| Merge together | Merge |
| Cooperate together | Cooperate |
| Rise up | Rise |
| Circle around | Circle |
| Consensus of opinion | Consensus |
| New innovation | Innovation |
| Final outcome | Outcome |
| Unexpected surprise | Surprise |
| Past history | History |
| End result | Result |
| Advance planning | Planning |
| Period of time | Period or Time |

[example]
**Question**: The committee members finally reached a consensus of opinion on the matter.

**Options**: (A) reached a consensus (B) reached a consensus about opinion (C) reached a consensus in opinion (D) No improvement

**Solution**: 'Consensus' already means a general agreement of opinion. 'Consensus of opinion' is redundant. Option A removes the redundancy. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

[example]
**Question**: The two friends decided to cooperate together on the project.

**Options**: (A) cooperate (B) co-operate together (C) cooperate with each other (D) No improvement

**Solution**: 'Cooperate' already implies acting together. 'Cooperate together' is redundant. Option A is the correct concise version. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

**Tautology and Pleonasm**

These are more elaborate forms of redundancy where two phrases say the same thing in different ways.

- *Incorrect*: The reason is because... (Reason is already causal; use 'The reason is that...' or simply 'Because...')
- *Incorrect*: I personally believe... (Belief is inherently personal)
- *Incorrect*: Each and every... (Use one or the other for emphasis)
- *Incorrect*: Advance warning... (Warning is by definition advance)

[alert type="success"]
**The 10% Rule**: In sentence improvement questions, if two options are grammatically correct but one is shorter, the shorter one is usually correct—*provided* it preserves the full meaning. Examiners reward concision.
[/alert]

### Section 7 | SSC Pattern Sentence Improvement: Practice Sets

The following practice questions reflect the exact format and difficulty of SSC CGL, CHSL, and Bank PO exams. Each question presents an underlined portion with four alternatives (A, B, C, D), where D is frequently 'No improvement'. Select the best replacement.

[example]
**Question**: The price of essential commodities have risen sharply in the last month.

**Options**: (A) has risen (B) has been rising (C) rose (D) No improvement

**Solution**: The subject is 'price' (singular), so the verb should be 'has'. The tense should be present perfect or past—both are possible, but 'has risen' is the most appropriate for a recent event that has current relevance. Option A is correct. Option B changes the meaning to an ongoing process. Option C is past simple but lacks the present relevance. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

[example]
**Question**: He told me that he is working in this company since 2010.

**Options**: (A) has been working (B) worked (C) had worked (D) No improvement

**Solution**: 'Since' + point in time requires the present perfect continuous or present perfect tense. 'Has been working' indicates an action starting in the past and continuing to the present. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

[example]
**Question**: Inspite of his best efforts, he could not pass the examination.

**Options**: (A) In spite (B) Despite (C) Although (D) No improvement

**Solution**: The original has a spelling error—'Inspite' should be two words: 'In spite'. Option A corrects the spelling. Option B ('Despite') is also correct and would be a valid replacement. Option C ('Although') would require a clause, not a noun phrase. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

[example]
**Question**: The teacher asked the students to write a one-page summary on the chapter.

**Options**: (A) of (B) about (C) for (D) No improvement

**Solution**: The original preposition is 'on', which is idiomatic for a summary that covers or discusses a topic. 'Summary of' is also acceptable but the original is correct. The answer is **No improvement**.
[/example]

[example]
**Question**: She is more taller than her elder sister.

**Options**: (A) taller (B) much taller (C) more tall (D) No improvement

**Solution**: 'More taller' is a double comparative—incorrect. The correct form is 'taller'. Option A corrects it. Option B ('much taller') is also grammatically correct but introduces the modifier 'much', which changes the degree. The simpler correction is 'taller'. The answer is **A**.
[/example]

[quiz title="Sentence Improvement Practice Set 1"]
Q: The manager has asked the employees to revert back to him by evening.

- A) to revert to him
- B) to revert back him
- C) to revert him back
- D) No improvement

Correct: A (Feedback: 'Revert' already means to go back; 'revert back' is redundant)

Q: Unless you do not work hard, you will not succeed.

- A) Unless you work hard
- B) If you do not work hard
- C) Unless you will work hard
- D) No improvement

Correct: A (Feedback: 'Unless' already means 'if not'; using 'do not' with 'unless' creates a double negative. 'Unless you work hard' is correct)

Q: The jury has given their verdict in favor of the accused.

- A) its verdict
- B) his verdict
- C) her verdict
- D) No improvement

Correct: D (Feedback: Collective nouns can take plural verbs and pronouns in British English when the members are considered individually; 'their verdict' is acceptable here, especially in modern usage)

Q: He is one of the few people who has seen the Taj Mahal at sunset.

- A) who have seen
- B) who have saw
- C) who has saw
- D) No improvement

Correct: A (Feedback: The relative pronoun 'who' refers to 'people' (plural); the verb should be plural 'have seen')

Q: The Principal said that the exam will be conducted next week.

- A) would be
- B) shall be
- C) will have been
- D) No improvement

Correct: A (Feedback: When the reporting verb 'said' is in the past tense, the tense of the reported speech usually shifts to the past; 'will' becomes 'would')
[/quiz]

[quiz title="Sentence Improvement Practice Set 2"]
Q: He was prevented from attending the conference by his sudden illness.

- A) was prevented to attend
- B) was prevented for attending
- C) was prevented from attend
- D) No improvement

Correct: D (Feedback: 'Prevented from + gerund' is the correct pattern; original is correct)

Q: The police is investigating the case thoroughly.

- A) are investigating
- B) was investigating
- C) were investigating
- D) No improvement

Correct: A (Feedback: 'Police' is a plural noun in British English, taking a plural verb. In American English, it can be singular, but SSC follows British convention)

Q: She is senior than me in the department.

- A) senior to me
- B) more senior than me
- C) more senior to me
- D) No improvement

Correct: A (Feedback: 'Senior' is followed by the preposition 'to', not 'than'. Also, 'me' after 'to' is correct as an object pronoun)

Q: The amount of money he spent on the project were enormous.

- A) was enormous
- B) are enormous
- C) is enormous
- D) No improvement

Correct: A (Feedback: The subject is 'amount' (singular), not 'money'. Verb should be 'was' to match the singular subject and past tense 'spent')

Q: Although he was tired, but he continued working.

- A) Although he was tired, he continued
- B) Although he was tired, yet he continued
- C) Despite he was tired, he continued
- D) No improvement

Correct: A (Feedback: 'Although' and 'but' are both conjunctions; using both together is redundant. Option A removes 'but' correctly. 'Yet' is also redundant with 'although')
[/quiz]

[quiz title="Sentence Improvement Practice Set 3"]
Q: The chairman told that the meeting has been postponed.

- A) said that
- B) told to us that
- C) told us that
- D) No improvement

Correct: A (Feedback: 'Told' is a transitive verb and requires an object (e.g., 'told us'). 'Said' is intransitive and can be used without an object. 'Said that' is correct)

Q: The information provided by the report are reliable.

- A) is reliable
- B) were reliable
- C) have been reliable
- D) No improvement

Correct: A (Feedback: 'Information' is an uncountable noun and takes a singular verb)

Q: He is not only intelligent but also he is hardworking.

- A) not only intelligent but also hardworking
- B) not only intelligent but also he is hardworking
- C) not only intelligent but also hard working
- D) No improvement

Correct: A (Feedback: Parallelism error—'not only' and 'but also' must be followed by parallel structures; both should be adjectives or both should be clauses)

Q: She has bought a new furniture for her apartment.

- A) new piece of furniture
- B) a new piece of furniture
- C) new furnitures
- D) No improvement

Correct: B (Feedback: 'Furniture' is an uncountable noun. To express a singular countable item, use 'a piece of furniture'. Option A is missing the article; B adds it correctly)

Q: I wish I am a bird so that I could fly.

- A) I wish I were
- B) I wish I was
- C) I wish I have been
- D) No improvement

Correct: A (Feedback: 'Wish' + that-clause requires the subjunctive mood. For present hypothetical situations, use the past subjunctive—'were' for all persons)
[/quiz]

Your ear must be trained alongside your analytical mind.
