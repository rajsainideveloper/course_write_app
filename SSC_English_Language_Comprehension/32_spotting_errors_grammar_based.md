# Spotting Errors (Grammar Based)

> The architect's scalpel: dissecting sentences to expose structural flaws, logical breaches, and the subtle fractures that separate standard English from examination gold.

## Chapter 32: The Error Detection Framework

### Section 1 | The Cognitive Architecture of Error Spotting

Error detection is not passive reading; it is active interrogation. When you approach a sentence, your brain must perform a simultaneous multi-layered syntactic parse. The elite aspirant doesn't "look for errors"—they systematically verify each grammatical component against established rules while maintaining holistic sentence coherence.

**The Five-Second Protocol**

Every error detection question follows a predictable cognitive pathway:

1. **First Pass (2 seconds)**: Read for meaning. Does the sentence convey a logical proposition? If the meaning is absurd or contradictory, suspect *misplaced modifiers*, *wrong prepositions*, or *illogical conjunctions*.
2. **Second Pass (2 seconds)**: Identify the main clause. Find the *subject* and its *finite verb*. Verify number, person, and tense agreement.
3. **Third Pass (1 second)**: Scan function words—prepositions, articles, conjunctions. These are high-yield error zones in SSC exams.
4. **Final Verification**: Read the sentence aloud in your mind. If your internal ear detects awkwardness, trust it—then *name* the violation.

The fundamental principle: =Every error has a name=. If you cannot articulate *why* something is wrong, you have not truly detected an error—you have only sensed discomfort.

### Section 2 | Subject-Verb Agreement: The Spine of the Sentence

Subject-verb agreement errors constitute approximately 30-35% of all spotting error questions in SSC CGL and Bank PO exams. The rule is deceptively simple: =A verb must agree with its subject in number and person=. The execution, however, demands vigilance against numerous diversionary tactics.

**The Intervening Phrase Trap**

Examiners routinely insert long prepositional phrases between subject and verb to obscure the agreement relationship.

[alert type="warning"]
**Critical Insight**: The verb agrees with the *subject*, not with the noun nearest to it in the intervening phrase.
[/alert]

*Incorrect*: The quality of the mangoes **are** excellent.
*Correct*: The quality of the mangoes **is** excellent.

The subject is *quality* (singular), not *mangoes*. The prepositional phrase *of the mangoes* is merely decorative.

**The Collective Noun Conundrum**

Collective nouns (*team, committee, jury, audience, army, crowd, family*) take a singular verb when treated as a unified whole. They take a plural verb only when the members are acting individually.

*Unified action*: The committee **has** reached its decision.
*Individual action*: The committee **have** submitted their separate reports.

[alert type="info"]
**SSC Pattern Note**: Examiners frequently test collective nouns in sentences where the verb is placed far from the subject, or where the collective noun appears alongside plural nouns to create false agreement cues.
[/alert]

**Indefinite Pronouns: The Singular Giants**

These pronouns are *always* singular: *everyone, someone, anyone, no one, each, either, neither, every, anybody, somebody, nobody, anything, something, nothing, everything*.

*Incorrect*: Everyone in the two classes **have** submitted their assignments.
*Correct*: Everyone in the two classes **has** submitted **his or her** assignments. (Or, in modern usage, *their* if the context permits—though SSC traditionally prefers the singular pronoun.)

**The 'Either/Or' and 'Neither/Nor' Rule**

When compound subjects are joined by *or* or *nor*, the verb agrees with the subject *closer* to it.

*Incorrect*: Neither the manager nor his assistants **is** present.
*Correct*: Neither the manager nor his assistants **are** present. (Verb agrees with *assistants*)
*Correct*: Neither the assistants nor their manager **is** present. (Verb agrees with *manager*)

[alert type="danger"]
**Examination Trap**: Be suspicious when *or* or *nor* appears. Examiners often place the plural subject first, making the singular verb seem plausible to the untrained ear.
[/alert]

**The 'There' and 'Here' Construction**

When a sentence begins with *there* or *here*, the subject follows the verb. The verb must agree with this *delayed subject*.

*Incorrect*: There **is** three reasons for this decision.
*Correct*: There **are** three reasons for this decision.

**The 'One of Those Who' Construction**

This construction demands careful logical parsing:

The rule: =In 'one of those [plural noun] who/that [verb]', the verb is plural=. The relative pronoun *who* refers to the plural noun *those*, not to *one*.

*Incorrect*: She is one of those students who **is** always late.
*Correct*: She is one of those students who **are** always late.

However, when *the only* precedes *one*:

*Correct*: She is **the only** one of those students who **is** always late. (Here, *who* refers to *one*)

**The 'A Number' vs. 'The Number' Distinction**

*A number of* + plural noun = takes a plural verb.
*The number of* + plural noun = takes a singular verb.

*Incorrect*: A number of issues **has** been resolved.
*Correct*: A number of issues **have** been resolved.
*Correct*: The number of applicants **has** increased.

**The 'Many a' and 'More than one' Exception**

These expressions, despite their plural appearance, take singular verbs:

*Correct*: Many a student **has** failed this exam.
*Correct*: More than one candidate **has** submitted the form.

[flashcards title="Subject-Verb Agreement High-Yield Rules"]
| Rule Name | Core Principle |
| Intervening Phrases | Ignore prepositional phrases; verb agrees with the actual subject |
| Collective Nouns | Singular when unified; plural when individuals act separately |
| Indefinite Pronouns | Everyone, each, either, neither, etc. are always singular |
| Either/Or; Neither/Nor | Verb agrees with the nearer subject |
| There/Here | Verb agrees with the delayed subject after the verb |
| One of Those Who | Verb is plural (refers to *those*) |
| The Only One of Those | Verb is singular (refers to *one*) |
| A Number of | Plural verb |
| The Number of | Singular verb |
| Many a/More than one | Singular verb |
[/flashcards]

### Section 3 | Tense Errors: The Time-Space Continuum

Tense errors are the second most frequent category in error detection sections. The challenge lies not in memorizing tense forms but in understanding the logical relationships between actions in time.

**The Sequence of Tenses in Complex Sentences**

The tense of the subordinate clause must logically relate to the tense of the main clause—but this relationship is governed by meaning, not mechanical rules.

=In conditional sentences and time clauses, the present tense is used to express future time=:

*Incorrect*: If the train **will arrive** late, we will miss the connection.
*Correct*: If the train **arrives** late, we will miss the connection.

*Incorrect*: I will wait until he **will come**.
*Correct*: I will wait until he **comes**.

**The Past Perfect Necessity**

When two past actions occur at different times, the *earlier* action takes the past perfect tense; the *later* action takes the simple past. This is non-negotiable.

*Incorrect*: The train **left** before I reached the station.
*Correct*: The train **had left** before I reached the station.

[alert type="warning"]
**Common Cognitive Bias**: Students often use the simple past for both actions because both occurred in the past. The mental model must distinguish between *past* and *earlier past*—the sequence matters.
[/alert]

**The 'Since' and 'For' Constraint**

=With 'since' and 'for' indicating duration from the past to the present, use the present perfect or present perfect continuous tense=:

*Incorrect*: I **am studying** here since 2018.
*Correct*: I **have been studying** here since 2018.

*For* denotes duration; *since* denotes a starting point.

*Correct*: I have lived here **for** five years.
*Correct*: I have lived here **since** 2018.

**The Past Tense with 'Recently' and 'Lately'**

=With time adverbs like 'recently', 'lately', 'already', 'yet', 'ever', 'never', 'just', use the present perfect tense=:

*Incorrect*: I **saw** him just now.
*Correct*: I **have just seen** him. (In British English) / I **just saw** him (American—but SSC follows British convention generally).

*Incorrect*: She **did** not reply yet.
*Correct*: She **has** not replied yet.

**The 'Would' vs. 'Used to' Distinction**

*Used to* indicates a past habitual action that no longer occurs.
*Would* can also indicate past habits but is less flexible (cannot be used with stative verbs).

*Incorrect*: I **would** live in Delhi during my childhood. (Stative verb)
*Correct*: I **used to** live in Delhi during my childhood.

*Correct*: As a child, I **would** play cricket every evening. (Action verb)

**The 'No sooner...than' and 'Hardly...when' Structure**

These constructions require a specific tense pattern:

=No sooner + had + subject + past participle...than + simple past=
=Hardly + had + subject + past participle...when + simple past=

*Incorrect*: No sooner **did I reach** the station than the train left.
*Correct*: No sooner **had I reached** the station than the train left.

[alert type="danger"]
**SSC Pattern Trap**: The examiner will frequently test the 'No sooner...than' construction by reversing the subject-verb order incorrectly or by using the wrong conjunction ('No sooner...when' is *always* wrong).
[/alert]

**The 'Wish' and 'As if' Constructions**

Hypothetical or unreal situations require the past subjunctive:

=After 'wish', 'as if', 'as though', 'it is time', use the simple past to refer to present situations=:

*Incorrect*: I wish I **am** richer.
*Correct*: I wish I **were** richer. (Subjunctive—'were' for all persons)

*Incorrect*: He behaves as if he **is** the owner.
*Correct*: He behaves as if he **were** the owner.

### Section 4 | Preposition Errors: The Invisible Connectors

Preposition errors are pernicious because they often involve omission, addition, or substitution of small words that dramatically alter meaning. Approximately 15-20% of spotting error questions target prepositions.

**Omission of Required Prepositions**

Certain verbs, adjectives, and nouns have fixed prepositional partners. Omitting these prepositions is a common error.

*Incorrect*: We discussed **about** the matter. (No preposition required after 'discuss')
*Correct*: We discussed the matter.

But:
*Incorrect*: She insisted **to** pay the bill.
*Correct*: She insisted **on** paying the bill.

**Wrong Preposition Selection**

Many words are commonly mispaired:

| Word | Correct Preposition | Common Error |
|------|---------------------|--------------|
| Differ with (a person) | Differ with | Differ from |
| Differ from (a thing) | Differ from | Differ with |
| Angry with (a person) | Angry with | Angry at |
| Angry at (a thing) | Angry at | Angry with |
| Agree with (a person) | Agree with | Agree to |
| Agree to (a proposal) | Agree to | Agree with |
| Compare to (similarity) | Compare to | Compare with |
| Compare with (difference) | Compare with | Compare to |
| Prefer | Prefer X to Y | Prefer X than Y |
| Superior/Inferior | Superior to | Superior than |
| Accused | Accused of | Accused for |

[flashcards title="Critical Preposition Pairs"]
| Expression | Preposition | Example |
| Concerned with | With | The book is concerned with modern history |
| Concerned about | About | I am concerned about your health |
| Consist of | Of | Water consists of hydrogen and oxygen |
| Comprise | (No preposition) | The team comprises eleven players |
| Composed of | Of | The committee is composed of experts |
| Insist on | On | She insisted on attending |
| Prevent from | From | Prevent him from entering |
| Prohibit from | From | Prohibited from using phones |
| Congratulate on | On | Congratulated on her success |
| Blame for | For | Blamed for the mistake |
| Blame on | On | The blame was placed on him |
[/flashcards]

**The 'Between' vs. 'Among' Distinction**

=Use 'between' for two entities; use 'among' for more than two=:

*Incorrect*: Distribute the sweets **between** the three children.
*Correct*: Distribute the sweets **among** the three children.

*Correct*: The conversation was **between** the two leaders.

**The 'In' vs. 'Into' Distinction**

=Use 'in' to indicate position within; use 'into' to indicate movement toward the interior=:

*Incorrect*: She walked **in** the room.
*Correct*: She walked **into** the room.

*Correct*: She is **in** the room.

**Prepositions with Time Expressions**

=Omit prepositions before certain time expressions=:

*No preposition before*: today, tomorrow, yesterday, the next day, last week, next month, this year.

*Incorrect*: I will meet you **on** tomorrow.
*Correct*: I will meet you tomorrow.

*But*: On Sunday, in the morning, at 5 PM.

[alert type="info"]
**Mental Model**: Think of prepositions as spatial and temporal location markers. If a sentence seems to convey location but lacks a preposition, or uses the wrong one, the error is likely here.
[/alert]

**The 'Since' vs. 'For' vs. 'From' Distinction**

- **Since**: from a specific point in the past to the present (used with present perfect)
- **For**: duration of time (used with present perfect)
- **From**: starting point in the past (used with past tense)

*Incorrect*: I have been waiting **from** 5 PM.
*Correct*: I have been waiting **since** 5 PM.

*Incorrect*: The show will run **since** 6 PM to 10 PM.
*Correct*: The show will run **from** 6 PM to 10 PM.

### Section 5 | Article Errors: The Definite and Indefinite Pitfalls

Article errors constitute 8-10% of spotting error questions. The rules appear simple but contain numerous exceptions and subtle distinctions.

**The 'A' vs. 'An' Rule**

=Use 'a' before consonant sounds; use 'an' before vowel sounds=. The rule is phonological, not orthographic.

*Incorrect*: She is **a** honest woman.
*Correct*: She is **an** honest woman. ('Honest' begins with a vowel sound /ɒ/)

*Incorrect*: He is **an** European.
*Correct*: He is **a** European. ('European' begins with a consonant sound /jʊ/)

[alert type="warning"]
**Examination Trap**: Words beginning with 'h' require attention. If the 'h' is silent, use 'an' (an hour, an heir, an honest). If the 'h' is pronounced, use 'a' (a hotel, a house, a historian—though some variations exist).
[/alert]

**Omission of the Definite Article with Superlatives**

=Superlatives and ordinal numbers require 'the'=:

*Incorrect*: Mount Everest is **highest** peak in the world.
*Correct*: Mount Everest is **the highest** peak in the world.

*Incorrect*: He came **first** in the race.
*Correct*: He came **the first** in the race. (In some constructions, 'the' is optional; but SSC patterns often test this)

**Omission with Unique Entities**

=Use 'the' before names of unique entities=:

*The sun, the moon, the earth, the sky, the universe, the world.*

*Incorrect*: Sun rises in the east.
*Correct*: The sun rises in the east.

**The 'A' vs. 'The' with Countable Nouns**

=Use 'a' with a singular countable noun when it is mentioned for the first time; use 'the' for subsequent references or when the noun is specific=:

*Incorrect*: I saw **the** dog in the park. (If no prior mention, should be 'a dog')
*Correct*: I saw **a** dog in the park. **The** dog was brown.

**Zero Article Usage**

=Omit articles before plural countable nouns used in a general sense and before uncountable nouns used generally=:

*Incorrect*: The honesty is the best policy.
*Correct*: Honesty is the best policy.

*Incorrect*: The dogs are loyal animals.
*Correct*: Dogs are loyal animals.

**Article with 'Whole' and 'Entire'**

=Use 'the' before 'whole' and after 'all'=:

*Incorrect*: He ate whole cake.
*Correct*: He ate the whole cake.

*Correct*: He ate all the cake.

[flashcards title="Article Usage Quick Reference"]
| Category | Rule | Example |
| General reference (plural/uncountable) | No article | Dogs are friendly |
| General reference (singular countable) | A/An | A dog is friendly |
| Specific reference | The | The dog in the garden |
| Superlatives/Ordinals | The | The best, the first |
| Names of unique entities | The | The sun, the moon |
| Names of materials (general) | No article | Gold is precious |
| Names of materials (specific) | The | The gold of this ring |
| Proper nouns | No article | Delhi, India |
| Proper nouns with 'of' phrase | The | The United States |
[/flashcards]

### Section 6 | Conjunction Errors: The Logical Connectors

Conjunction errors test your understanding of logical relationships between clauses. The error may be a wrong conjunction, a missing conjunction, or a double conjunction.

**The 'Because' and 'Because of' Distinction**

=Use 'because' + clause; use 'because of' + noun phrase=:

*Incorrect*: He failed **because of** he was lazy.
*Correct*: He failed **because** he was lazy.

*Correct*: He failed **because of** his laziness.

**The 'Although' and 'Despite' Distinction**

=Use 'although' + clause; use 'despite' + noun phrase=:

*Incorrect*: **Despite** he was tired, he worked.
*Correct*: **Although** he was tired, he worked.

*Correct*: **Despite** his tiredness, he worked.

**The 'So...that' vs. 'Such...that' Distinction**

=Use 'so' + adjective/adverb; use 'such' + noun phrase=:

*Incorrect*: It was **so** a beautiful day that we went out.
*Correct*: It was **such** a beautiful day that we went out.

*Correct*: The day was **so** beautiful that we went out.

**The 'Not Only...But Also' Parallelism**

=The structures following 'not only' and 'but also' must be grammatically parallel=:

*Incorrect*: She is **not only** intelligent **but also** has charm.
*Correct*: She is **not only** intelligent **but also** charming.

*Correct*: She **not only** sings **but also** dances.

[alert type="danger"]
**SSC Pattern Trap**: 'Not only...but also' errors frequently involve non-parallel structures OR the placement of 'not only' before the wrong element. The rule: ='Not only' must be placed immediately before the element it introduces=.
[/alert]

**The 'No sooner...than' Conjunction**

*Incorrect*: No sooner had I arrived **when** the train left.
*Correct*: No sooner had I arrived **than** the train left.

**The 'Hardly...when' and 'Scarcely...when' Construction**

=Use 'when' after 'hardly' and 'scarcely'; use 'than' after 'no sooner'=:

*Incorrect*: Hardly had he arrived **than** the meeting began.
*Correct*: Hardly had he arrived **when** the meeting began.

**The 'As...as' and 'So...as' Distinction**

=Use 'as...as' in affirmative sentences; use 'so...as' in negative sentences=:

*Incorrect*: She is **so** beautiful **as** her sister. (Affirmative)
*Correct*: She is **as** beautiful **as** her sister.

*Incorrect*: She is not **as** beautiful **as** her sister. (Negative—'as...as' is acceptable here too; 'so...as' is more formal but both are correct in modern usage)
*Correct*: She is not **so** beautiful **as** her sister. (More formal)

**The 'Lest' Construction**

=Lest is followed by 'should' (not by 'should not') and takes the subjunctive=:

*Incorrect*: Walk slowly lest you **should not** fall.
*Correct*: Walk slowly lest you **should** fall.

*Correct*: Walk slowly lest you fall. ('Should' is often omitted)

### Section 7 | Modal Errors: The Auxiliary of Attitude

Modal auxiliaries (*can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must, ought to, need, dare*) express necessity, possibility, permission, or obligation. Errors typically involve wrong modal selection or incorrect tense formation.

**'Can' vs. 'Could' vs. 'May'**

- **Can**: ability or informal permission
- **Could**: past ability, polite request, or less certain possibility
- **May**: formal permission or possibility

*Incorrect*: I **may** swim very well. (Should be 'can' for ability)
*Correct*: I **can** swim very well.

*Incorrect*: Can I come in, sir? (Informal; in formal contexts, 'may' is preferred)
*Correct*: May I come in, sir?

**'Should' vs. 'Would'**

- **Should**: obligation, advice, or expectation
- **Would**: habitual past action, polite request, or conditional

*Incorrect*: I **should** like to meet you. (Should is incorrect; 'would like' expresses desire)
*Correct*: I **would** like to meet you.

*Incorrect*: He **would** obey his teachers. (If this is a moral obligation, 'should' is correct)
*Correct*: He **should** obey his teachers.

**'Must' vs. 'Have to'**

=Use 'must' for internal obligation or strong necessity; use 'have to' for external obligation=:

*Incorrect*: I **must** go to the office by 9 AM. (If this is a company rule, 'have to' is more appropriate)
*Correct*: I **have to** go to the office by 9 AM.

*But*: I **must** visit my mother this weekend. (Personal obligation)

**The 'Used to' vs. 'Would'**

=Use 'used to' for past states and habits; use 'would' only for past actions (not states)=:

*Incorrect*: She **would** have long hair as a child.
*Correct*: She **used to** have long hair as a child.

*Correct*: She **would** play in the garden every evening.

[alert type="info"]
**Exam Pattern Note**: Modal errors often appear in combination with tense errors. Examiners test whether candidates can identify the correct modal for a given context.
[/alert]

### Section 8 | Pronoun Errors: Case, Number, Gender, and Reference

Pronoun errors account for 8-10% of spotting error questions. These errors are often subtle, involving ambiguous reference, case misuse, or number disagreement.

**The Case Rule**

=Subject pronouns (I, he, she, we, they) are used as subjects; object pronouns (me, him, her, us, them) are used as objects=:

*Incorrect*: Between you and **I**, this is a bad idea.
*Correct*: Between you and **me**, this is a bad idea. ('Between' is a preposition; objects follow prepositions)

*Incorrect*: Me and Raj went to the market.
*Correct*: Raj and **I** went to the market. (Subject position)

**Reflexive Pronoun Usage**

=Reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, themselves) are used when the subject and object are the same person or for emphasis=:

*Incorrect*: I **myself** did it. (Acceptable for emphasis, but not as a substitute for the object)
*Incorrect*: He is between you and **myself**.
*Correct*: He is between you and **me**.

*Incorrect*: The children enjoyed **themselves** in the party. (Correct, if they enjoyed the party)
*Correct*: The children enjoyed **themselves** at the party. (The party is the location; 'themselves' is correct)

**The 'Each Other' vs. 'One Another' Distinction**

=Use 'each other' for two entities; use 'one another' for more than two=:

*Incorrect*: The two friends loved **one another**.
*Correct*: The two friends loved **each other**.

*Correct*: All the members respected **one another**.

**The 'Who' vs. 'Whom' Distinction**

=Use 'who' as the subject; use 'whom' as the object=:

*Incorrect*: **Whom** do you think is responsible?
*Correct*: **Who** do you think is responsible? ('Who' is the subject of 'is')

*Incorrect*: **Who** did you meet?
*Correct*: **Whom** did you meet? ('Whom' is the object of 'meet')

[alert type="warning"]
**Practical Tip**: For SSC exams, 'whom' is rarely tested; when it appears, the correct answer is often 'who' because the pronoun is in the subject position.
[/alert]

**The Possessive Pronoun with Gerund**

=When a gerund (verb + ing functioning as a noun) is used, use a possessive pronoun before it=:

*Incorrect*: I am tired of **him** complaining.
*Correct*: I am tired of **his** complaining.

*Incorrect*: She resented **me** interfering.
*Correct*: She resented **my** interfering.

**The 'One' Pronoun Agreement**

=When 'one' is used as an indefinite pronoun, subsequent references should use 'one' or 'one's' (not 'he' or 'his') in formal writing=:

*Incorrect*: One should do **his** duty.
*Correct*: One should do **one's** duty. (In modern usage, 'their' is sometimes accepted, but SSC traditionally prefers 'one's')

**The 'Either/Neither' Pronoun Agreement**

=Either and neither are singular pronouns and take singular verbs and singular pronoun references=:

*Incorrect*: Neither of the girls has submitted **their** assignment.
*Correct*: Neither of the girls has submitted **her** assignment. (If the context is gendered; otherwise, 'his or her' or 'their' in modern usage—SSC traditionally accepts singular references)

### Section 9 | Adverb/Adjective Confusion

The confusion between adjectives and adverbs is a classic error pattern, particularly with linking verbs and perception verbs.

**The Linking Verb Rule**

=Linking verbs (be, seem, appear, look, feel, smell, taste, become, grow, remain) take adjectives, not adverbs=:

*Incorrect*: The food tastes **deliciously**.
*Correct*: The food tastes **delicious**.

*Incorrect*: She looks **beautifully** today.
*Correct*: She looks **beautiful** today.

**The 'Good' vs. 'Well' Distinction**

=Use 'good' as an adjective; use 'well' as an adverb (except when meaning 'in good health')=:

*Incorrect*: She speaks English **good**.
*Correct*: She speaks English **well**.

*Correct*: I feel **good**. (Emotional state)
*Correct*: I feel **well**. (Health condition)

**The 'Hard' vs. 'Hardly' Distinction**

=Hard is both adjective and adverb; hardly is an adverb meaning 'barely'=:

*Incorrect*: She worked **hardly** to complete the project.
*Correct*: She worked **hard** to complete the project.

*Correct*: I **hardly** know her. (Barely)

**The 'Late' vs. 'Lately' Distinction**

=Late is both adjective and adverb meaning 'not on time'; lately means 'recently'=:

*Incorrect*: I have been busy **late**.
*Correct*: I have been busy **lately**.

*Correct*: I arrived **late** for the meeting.

**The 'Near' vs. 'Nearly' Distinction**

=Near is both adjective and adverb meaning 'close'; nearly means 'almost'=:

*Incorrect*: The project is **near** complete.
*Correct*: The project is **nearly** complete.

*Correct*: The station is **near** the office.

**The 'Most' vs. 'Almost' Distinction**

=Most is an adjective/adverb meaning 'the majority'; almost is an adverb meaning 'nearly'=:

*Incorrect*: **Most** all the students passed.
*Correct*: **Almost** all the students passed.

*Correct*: **Most** students passed.

### Section 10 | Comparative/Superlative Errors

Errors in degrees of comparison are common and often subtle.

**The Double Comparative**

=Never use a double comparative (more + -er, most + -est)=:

*Incorrect*: She is **more taller** than her sister.
*Correct*: She is **taller** than her sister.

*Incorrect*: This is the **most easiest** route.
*Correct*: This is the **easiest** route.

**The 'Than' vs. 'Then' Confusion**

=Use 'than' in comparisons; use 'then' for sequence=:

*Incorrect*: She is taller **then** her brother.
*Correct*: She is taller **than** her brother.

**The 'As...As' Comparison**

=In comparisons, use 'as...as' in affirmative sentences; use 'so...as' in negative sentences=:

*Incorrect*: She is not **as** tall **as** her sister. (Acceptable in modern English; but 'so...as' is more formal)
*Correct*: She is not **so** tall **as** her sister.

**The 'Senior/Junior' Preposition**

=Use 'to' (not 'than') with senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior, anterior, posterior=:

*Incorrect*: He is senior **than** me.
*Correct*: He is senior **to** me.

**The 'Prefer...to' Construction**

=Use 'to' (not 'than') with 'prefer'=:

*Incorrect*: I prefer tea **than** coffee.
*Correct*: I prefer tea **to** coffee.

**The 'Better of the Two' Construction**

=When comparing two entities, use the comparative degree (not the superlative) and include the article 'the'=:

*Incorrect*: She is the **best** of the two candidates.
*Correct*: She is the **better** of the two candidates.

### Section 11 | Parallelism Errors

Parallelism requires that items in a series or in certain paired constructions have the same grammatical form.

**The Series Rule**

=All items in a series must be in the same grammatical form=:

*Incorrect*: She likes **swimming**, **jogging**, and **to cycle**.
*Correct*: She likes **swimming**, **jogging**, and **cycling**.

*Incorrect*: He is **intelligent**, **hardworking**, and **has integrity**.
*Correct*: He is **intelligent**, **hardworking**, and **integrity-filled**.
*Better*: He is **intelligent**, **hardworking**, and **upright**.

**The Correlative Conjunction Rule**

=In pairs like 'either...or', 'neither...nor', 'not only...but also', 'both...and', the structures following each conjunction must be parallel=:

*Incorrect*: He **not only** ate the food **but also** the dessert. (Not parallel: 'ate' vs. 'the dessert')
*Correct*: He ate **not only** the food **but also** the dessert.

*Incorrect*: She is **either** intelligent **or** has talent.
*Correct*: She is **either** intelligent **or** talented.

**The Comparison Rule**

=Items being compared must be logically and grammatically parallel=:

*Incorrect*: Her salary is higher than **her husband**.
*Correct*: Her salary is higher than **her husband's** (salary).

*Incorrect*: The trees in the forest are taller than **the city**.
*Correct*: The trees in the forest are taller than **those in the city**.

[alert type="info"]
**SSC Pattern Note**: Parallelism errors are frequently tested in 'not only...but also' constructions and in comparative sentences. Watch for non-parallel structures in lists of three or more items.
[/alert]

### Section 12 | SSC Pattern Spotting Errors: Practice Sets

The following practice sets simulate the exact format and difficulty level of SSC CGL, CHSL, and Bank PO error detection sections. Each sentence contains either a grammatical error, a vocabulary error (wrong word), or no error.

The approach: Read each sentence. Identify the error in one of the four underlined parts (A, B, C, D) or mark 'No error' (E). Some sets follow the traditional format where the error is not underlined, requiring you to identify the part containing the error from the options.

[example]
**Question (SSC CGL Pattern)**: Find the error in the following sentence. If no error, mark option E.

The train had arrived (A) / at the station (B) / when we reached (C) / the platform. (D) / No error (E)

**Solution**: The error is in part A. The sentence describes two past actions—the train's arrival and our reaching the platform. The train arrived *before* we reached, so the earlier action should be in the past perfect: "The train **had arrived**" is correct. Wait—the sentence reads "The train had arrived...when we reached." This actually correctly uses the past perfect for the earlier action and simple past for the later action. The correct answer is **E (No error)**.

*Note*: Many students incorrectly identify an error because they forget the past perfect is used for the *earlier* past action.
[/example]

[example]
**Question**: Identify the error.

Neither the captain (A) / nor the players (B) / was present (C) / at the meeting. (D) / No error (E)

**Solution**: The error is in part C. With 'neither...nor', the verb agrees with the noun *closer* to it. The closer subject is 'players' (plural), so the verb should be 'were' (plural). Correct sentence: "Neither the captain nor the players **were** present at the meeting."
[/example]

[example]
**Question**: Identify the error.

The committee (A) / have decided (B) / to postpone (C) / the annual meeting. (D) / No error (E)

**Solution**: The error is in part B. 'Committee' is a collective noun. In this context, the committee is acting as a unified whole (making a single decision), so it takes a singular verb. Correct sentence: "The committee **has** decided to postpone the annual meeting." If the sentence had referred to individual members disagreeing, the plural verb might be acceptable, but in standard usage and SSC patterns, collective nouns with a single decision take singular verbs.
[/example]

[example]
**Question**: Identify the error.

She has (A) / been working (B) / since five years (C) / in this company. (D) / No error (E)

**Solution**: The error is in part C. 'Since' is used with a *point* of time; 'for' is used with a *duration* of time. 'Five years' is a duration, so the correct preposition is 'for'. Correct sentence: "She has been working **for** five years in this company."
[/example]

[example]
**Question**: Identify the error.

No sooner (A) / had I reached (B) / the station when (C) / the train left. (D) / No error (E)

**Solution**: The error is in part C. 'No sooner' is always followed by 'than', not 'when'. Correct sentence: "No sooner had I reached the station **than** the train left." (The correct structure is 'No sooner + had + subject + past participle + than + simple past')
[/example]

[alert type="success"]
**Key Strategy**: When reading a sentence for error detection, always:
1. Identify the main subject and verb first (SVA).
2. Check prepositions and articles next (high-yield categories).
3. Verify tense and modality.
4. Check pronouns for agreement and reference.
5. Finally, scan for parallelism and comparative errors.
This systematic approach prevents the cognitive bias of focusing on the first apparent error.
[/alert]
