# Subject-Verb Agreement
> Agreement is not a mechanical act of matching endings—it is a logical system where the verb conforms to the subject's number and person. Master this system, and you eliminate the single most common error in English examinations.

## Chapter 6: The Architecture of Agreement

### Section 6.1 | The Core Principle – Singular and Plural

At its heart, subject-verb agreement is deceptively simple: **a singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb.** This principle seems straightforward, yet competitive exams relentlessly exploit the situations where the subject is hidden, disguised, or structurally complex.

- =**Singular verbs** end in **-s** or **-es** in the present tense (e.g., *He **runs**, She **goes**, It **is**.*)
- =**Plural verbs** do **not** end in **-s** (e.g., *They **run**, We **go**, They **are**.*)
- **The Cognitive Trap:** Many students reverse this rule, assuming the verb with the '-s' is plural. The error is so common that examiners rely on it. Remember: **The verb's '-s' is not plural—it is third-person singular.**

[alert type="danger"]
**The Golden Rule:** The subject is never found inside a prepositional phrase. Phrases that begin with *of, in, with, by, for, at, from, to* often contain nouns that distract from the true subject.
- *The box **of chocolates** **is** on the table.* (Subject: *box* – singular)
- *The **chocolates** in the box **are** delicious.* (Subject: *chocolates* – plural)
[/alert]

### Section 6.2 | The Hunting Method – Finding the True Subject

When you encounter a sentence, follow this cognitive sequence:

1.  **Locate the verb.**
2.  **Ask "Who?" or "What?" before the verb.** The answer is the subject.
3.  **Eliminate all intervening phrases** (prepositional phrases, clauses, parenthetical elements).
4.  **Determine the number of the subject** (singular or plural).
5.  **Match the verb to that number.**

## Chapter 7: Compound Subjects

### Section 7.1 | Subjects Joined by 'And'

When two or more singular subjects are joined by **'and'**, the subject is plural and takes a plural verb.

- *The teacher **and** the student **are** in the classroom.*
- *He **and** she **have** arrived.*
- **Exception – A Single Entity:** When the compound subject refers to a single person, thing, or concept, use a singular verb.
- *Bread **and** butter **is** my favourite breakfast.* (One dish).
- *The singer **and** songwriter **is** here.* (One person with two roles).
- *Slow **and** steady **wins** the race.* (A single concept).

[alert type="warning"]
**The Article Test:** If two nouns are joined by 'and', the verb is usually plural. However, if the same article (a/an/the) is used before the first noun only, it often indicates a single person/entity. If articles are repeated, it indicates separate individuals.
- *The **principal and teacher** **is** coming.* (One person – both roles).
- *The **principal and the teacher** **are** coming.* (Two different people).
[/alert]

### Section 7.2 | Subjects Joined by 'Or' or 'Nor'

When subjects are joined by **'or'**, **'nor'**, **'either...or'**, or **'neither...nor'**, the verb agrees with the subject closest to it. This is the **Proximity Rule**.

- *Neither the teacher **nor** the students **are** present.* (Verb agrees with *students* – plural).
- *Neither the students **nor** the teacher **is** present.* (Verb agrees with *teacher* – singular).
- *Either the manager **or** his assistants **have** the authority.* (Verb agrees with *assistants* – plural).
- *Either the assistants **or** the manager **has** the authority.* (Verb agrees with *manager* – singular).

[alert type="danger"]
**The Proximity Rule Trap:** This rule is frequently tested because it creates a counterintuitive structure. The subject farther from the verb does not control the verb. The closest subject always decides. This is a high-frequency SSC error detection target.
[/alert]

## Chapter 8: Collective Nouns – The Unity vs Individuality Distinction

### Section 8.1 | The Collective Noun Principle

Collective nouns (*team, jury, committee, audience, crowd, fleet, flock, family, group, staff*) name a group of people or things considered as a single unit.

- =**Singular Verb** = The group acts as a *single unit*.
    - *The **committee has** reached its decision.* (One decision).
    - *The **team is** playing well.* (One team performance).
- =**Plural Verb** = The members of the group act *individually*.
    - *The **committee are** divided in their opinions.* (Members have different opinions).
    - *The **team are** arguing among themselves.* (Individuals are arguing).

**The Cognitive Filter:** Ask yourself: "Are they acting together or separately?" If the answer is "together," use singular. If "separately," use plural.

[alert type="info"]
**The 'Police', 'People', 'Cattle' Exception:** Certain nouns that look singular are always plural in English.
- =**Police, people, cattle, poultry** – always take a plural verb.
    - *The police **are** investigating the case.* (Not "is").
    - *The cattle **are** grazing in the field.* (Not "is").
- =**Civilians, mankind, humanity** – are singular and take a singular verb.
    - *Mankind **has** always sought knowledge.* (Not "have").
[/alert]

[flashcards title="Collective Noun Verb Selection - Quick Guide"]
| Noun | Singular (Unity) | Plural (Individuality) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *Team* | *The team has won.* | *The team are celebrating in different ways.* |
| *Jury* | *The jury has given its verdict.* | *The jury are divided.* |
| *Committee* | *The committee has approved the plan.* | *The committee are debating the issue.* |
| *Audience* | *The audience was silent.* | *The audience were clapping in turns.* |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 9: Indefinite Pronouns – The Singular Maze

### Section 9.1 | The Always Singular Indefinites

This is the single most heavily tested area of subject-verb agreement. A large group of indefinite pronouns are **always singular** and take a singular verb.

**The Singular List:**
- **Body/One/Thing Compounds:** *everybody, everyone, everything, somebody, someone, something, anybody, anyone, anything, nobody, no one, nothing, one.*
- **Each and Every:** *each, every, either, neither.*

- =*Everyone **has** a responsibility.*
- =*Somebody **is** at the door.*
- =*Each of the boys **was** given a prize.* (Subject: *each* – singular).
- =*Neither of the options **is** acceptable.* (Subject: *neither* – singular).
- =*Either of the two routes **leads** to the station.* (Subject: *either* – singular).

[alert type="danger"]
**The 'Every' Trap:** When 'every' is followed by two or more singular nouns joined by 'and', the subject is still singular.
- =*Every boy **and** every girl **is** invited.* (Not "are").
- =*Every student, teacher, and parent **has** been notified.*
[/alert]

### Section 9.2 | The Always Plural Indefinites

Some indefinite pronouns are **always plural** and take a plural verb.

**The Plural List:** *few, many, several, both.*

- =*Few **are** chosen for this honour.*
- =*Many **have** tried to solve this puzzle.*
- =*Both **were** present at the meeting.*

### Section 9.3 | The Variable Indefinites (Depends on the Reference)

Some indefinite pronouns can be singular or plural depending on the noun they refer to.

**The Variable List:** *all, any, none, some, more, most, half, plenty, enough.*

- =**Agreement Rule:** The verb agrees with the noun after the preposition 'of'.
- *All of the water **is** gone.* (Water – uncountable; singular).
- *All of the students **are** present.* (Students – plural; plural).
- *None of the money **was** found.* (Money – uncountable; singular).
- *None of the players **were** injured.* (Players – plural; plural). *('None' can take singular or plural in modern English, but the noun after 'of' is the determining factor for the exam).*
- *Some of the cake **is** left.* (Cake – singular).
- *Some of the cookies **are** missing.* (Cookies – plural).

[flashcards title="Indefinite Pronoun Agreement Chart"]
| Category | Pronouns | Verb |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Always Singular** | *everyone, everyone, each, either, neither, nobody, anybody, somebody, one* | Singular |
| **Always Plural** | *few, many, several, both* | Plural |
| **Variable** | *all, any, none, some, more, most, half* | Depends on the object of 'of' |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 10: Each, Every, and "Many a"

### Section 10.1 | The 'Each' and 'Every' Effect

When **'each'** or **'every'** is used before a noun, it forces a singular verb, regardless of the number of nouns that follow.

- =*Each student **has** a book.*
- =*Every teacher **is** respected.*
- =*Each of the students **has** a book.* (Subject: *each* – singular).
- =*Every man and woman **was** given a ticket.* (Singular because of 'every').

### Section 10.2 | The 'Many a' Structure

**'Many a'** is a literary structure that means "many" but takes a **singular** verb and a **singular** noun.

- =*Many a student **has** struggled with this rule.*
- =*Many a day **has** passed since we met.*
- **The Trap:** Do not confuse this with 'many' (plural).
    - *Many **students have** struggled.* (Plural verb).
    - *Many a **student has** struggled.* (Singular verb – 'a' forces the singular).

[alert type="warning"]
**The 'Each' vs 'Every' Distinction:** 'Each' emphasises individuals within a group; 'Every' emphasises the group as a whole. However, for subject-verb agreement, both are singular.
- *Each of the employees **is** responsible.*
- *Every employee **is** responsible.*
[/alert]

## Chapter 11: Relative Pronouns – Agreement with the Antecedent

### Section 11.1 | The Antecedent Rule

Relative pronouns (*who, which, that, whose*) take verbs that agree with their **antecedent** (the noun they refer to).

- =*He is the only one of the students who **has** passed.*
- *She is one of the women who **have** achieved success.*

**The Cognitive Process:**

1.  **Locate the relative pronoun.**
2.  **Find its antecedent** – the noun just before the pronoun.
3.  **Determine the number of that antecedent.**
4.  **Match the verb to that number.**

[alert type="danger"]
**The "One of the..." Trap:** This is an SSC favourite. The pattern 'one of the + plural noun + relative pronoun' takes a **plural** verb because the antecedent is the plural noun.
- *She is one of those people who **never give** up.* (Antecedent: *people* – plural).
- *He is one of the players who **have** been selected.* (Antecedent: *players* – plural).

**The Exception – "The only one of the...":** When 'only' is inserted before 'one', the antecedent becomes 'one' (singular).
- *She is the **only** one of the students who **has** been selected.* (Antecedent: *one* – singular).
- *He is the **only** one of the players who **has** won the award.* (Antecedent: *one* – singular).
[/alert]

## Chapter 12: Quantifiers – Agreement with the Object of 'Of'

### Section 12.1 | The 'A lot of', 'Plenty of', 'A large number of'

These quantifiers agree with the noun that follows them—the object of the preposition 'of'.

- **'A number of'** = plural (takes a plural verb).
    - *A number of students **are** waiting outside.*
- **'The number of'** = singular (takes a singular verb).
    - *The number of students **is** increasing.*
- **'A lot of' / 'Lots of'** = agrees with the noun after it.
    - *A lot of water **is** wasted.* (Water – uncountable; singular).
    - *A lot of people **are** coming.* (People – plural).
- **'Plenty of'** = agrees with the noun after it.
    - *Plenty of time **was** given.* (Time – singular).
    - *Plenty of opportunities **were** provided.* (Opportunities – plural).

[alert type="info"]
**The 'A large number of' vs 'The large number of' Test:**
- **'A large number of'** always takes a plural verb (refers to many individuals).
    - *A large number of volunteers **have** come forward.*
- **'The large number of'** always takes a singular verb (refers to the quantity as a whole).
    - *The large number of volunteers **is** impressive.*
[/alert]

## Chapter 13: Nouns That Are Plural in Form but Singular in Meaning

### Section 13.1 | The Deceptive '-s' Words

Several nouns end in **'-s'** but are actually singular. They take singular verbs. This is a high-frequency SSC trap.

**Categories:**

| Category | Examples | Correct Verb |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Academic Subjects** | *mathematics, physics, economics, politics, statistics, ethics* | Singular (*Mathematics **is** difficult.*) |
| **Games/Sports** | *billiards, darts, gymnastics, athletics* | Singular (*Billiards **is** his favourite game.*) |
| **Diseases** | *measles, mumps, rickets* | Singular (*Measles **is** contagious.*) |
| **News/Special Nouns** | *news, means, series, species, headquarters, works (factory)* | Singular (*The news **is** good; This series **is** popular.*) |

[alert type="danger"]
**The "Politics" Trap:** *Politics* is singular when referring to the subject/activity but can be plural when referring to political views or strategies.
- *Politics **is** a challenging profession.* (Singular).
- *His politics **are** quite radical.* (Plural – refers to his views).
[/alert]

### Section 13.2 | The Pair Nouns

Nouns that refer to a pair of items are plural and take plural verbs.

**The List:** *scissors, trousers, jeans, glasses (spectacles), pliers, tongs, tweezers.*

- =*The scissors **are** on the table.*
- =*His trousers **are** too long.*

**Exception:** When preceded by 'a pair of', the verb is singular.
- =*A pair of scissors **is** on the table.*
- =*A pair of trousers **was** found.*

## Chapter 14: Intervening Phrases – The Parenthetical Distraction

### Section 14.1 | The 'Along with', 'Together with', 'As well as' Exception

Phrases introduced by *along with, together with, as well as, in addition to, accompanied by, besides, unlike, rather than, including, plus* are **parenthetical**. They add extra information but do **not** change the number of the subject.

- =**The subject before the phrase determines the verb.**

- *The teacher, **along with** her students, **is** going on the trip.* (Subject: *teacher* – singular).
- *The students, **along with** their teacher, **are** going on the trip.* (Subject: *students* – plural).
- *She, **as well as** her friends, **is** responsible.* (Subject: *She* – singular).
- *The manager, **together with** his assistants, **was** present.* (Subject: *manager* – singular).

[alert type="danger"]
**The 'And' vs 'Along with' Trap:** Do not confuse 'and' (which creates a plural subject) with 'along with' (which does not change the subject's number).
- *The teacher **and** her students **are** coming.* (Plural – 'and' joins them).
- *The teacher, **along with** her students, **is** coming.* (Singular – the phrase is parenthetical).
[/alert]

## Chapter 15: Common Errors in Subject-Verb Agreement

### Section 15.1 | The Error Detection Patterns

1.  **Subject Hidden in a Prepositional Phrase:**
    - **Error:** *The group of students **are** excited.*
    - **Correction:** *The group of students **is** excited.* (Subject: *group* – singular).

2.  **Compound Subject with 'Or/Nor' (Proximity):**
    - **Error:** *Neither the teacher nor the students **is** in the classroom.*
    - **Correction:** *Neither the teacher nor the students **are** in the classroom.* (Verb agrees with *students* – plural).

3.  **Indefinite Pronoun Error:**
    - **Error:** *Everyone **are** going to the party.*
    - **Correction:** *Everyone **is** going to the party.* (Singular indefinite).

4.  **Relative Pronoun Error:**
    - **Error:** *He is one of those people who **is** always late.*
    - **Correction:** *He is one of those people who **are** always late.* (Antecedent: *people* – plural).

5.  **Intervening Phrase Error:**
    - **Error:** *The captain, together with his team, **were** honoured.*
    - **Correction:** *The captain, together with his team, **was** honoured.* (Subject: *captain* – singular).

6.  **Singular-form Plural-meaning Nouns:**
    - **Error:** *The news **are** disturbing.*
    - **Correction:** *The news **is** disturbing.*

[example]
**Question:** Choose the correct option: "A number of students ______ registered for the competition, but the number of participants ______ limited."
- A) have; are
- B) has; is
- C) have; is (Correct)
- D) has; are
**Solution:** 'A number of' takes a plural verb (*have registered*). 'The number of' takes a singular verb (*is limited*).
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** Identify the error: "The jury were divided in their opinion, and the verdict has been announced."
**Solution:** No error. 'Jury' is plural here because the members are divided in their opinions (individuality). 'The verdict has been announced' is also correct. If the sentence had said 'The jury has given its verdict,' that would also be correct (unity). The exam tests both possibilities.
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** Identify the error: "Each of the boys along with their parents are expected to attend the function."
**Solution:** The error is 'are'. The subject is 'Each' (singular). 'Along with their parents' is an intervening phrase. The correct verb is 'is'. The corrected sentence is: "Each of the boys along with their parents **is** expected to attend the function."
[/example]
