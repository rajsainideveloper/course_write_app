# Tenses (Past)
> The past is not a single point but a landscape of duration, sequence, and perspective. The four past tenses are your instruments for mapping that terrain with precision.

## Chapter 3: The Architecture of Past Time

### Section 3.1 | The Four Past Frames

The past tenses in English function differently from their present counterparts. While the present system focuses on current relevance and ongoing states, the past system is fundamentally about **narrative distance**—how the speaker positions an event relative to a past reference point. The four tenses construct this distance through aspect.

| Tense | Core Function | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Past Simple (Indefinite)** | Completed action at a specific past time | *I **finished** the report at 6 PM.* |
| **Past Continuous (Progressive)** | Ongoing action at a specific past moment; interrupted actions | *I **was finishing** the report when he called.* |
| **Past Perfect** | Completed action before another past action | *I **had finished** the report before he arrived.* |
| **Past Perfect Continuous** | Ongoing duration leading up to a past event | *I **had been finishing** the report for hours when he arrived.* |

[alert type="warning"]
**The Cognitive Starting Point:** Do not approach past tenses as "past versions" of present tenses. The Past Perfect, for instance, is not merely "Past + Perfect" in a mechanical sense. It exists to create a *relative* time reference—a past within the past. This distinction is the single most frequent source of errors on competitive exams.
[/alert]

## Chapter 4: Past Indefinite (Simple Past) Tense

### Section 4.1 | Structure and Formation

The Past Simple is the most straightforward of the past tenses. It places an action firmly in a completed past, with no connection to the present. Its structure is predictable, but its irregular verbs demand systematic memorisation.

**Structure:**

| Sentence Type | Formula | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Affirmative | Subject + V2 (Past Form) | *She **wrote** a letter.* |
| Negative | Subject + did + **not** + V1 | *She **did not write** a letter.* |
| Interrogative | Did + Subject + V1 + ? | ***Did** she write a letter?* |

**The "Did" Rule:** The auxiliary *did* always takes the base form (V1) of the verb. This is a common error point.

- =*I **did not went**.* (Incorrect)
- =*I **did not go**.* (Correct)

### Section 4.2 | Regular and Irregular Verb Forms

The Past Simple is built on the *V2* form of the verb. For regular verbs, this is straightforward: add **-ed** (or **-d** for verbs ending in -e).

| Regular Verb Rule | Example Base | Example V2 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Add -ed | *walk* | *walked* |
| Add -d | *love* | *loved* |
| Change y to i + ed | *carry* | *carried* |
| Double final consonant + ed | *stop* | *stopped* |

[alert type="danger"]
**The Irregular Verb Challenge:** Approximately 200 common verbs are irregular in English. The competitive exam does not test your ability to list them but expects you to recognise and use them correctly. The most frequently tested irregularities involve the confusion between the Past Simple (V2) and the Past Participle (V3).
- *I **have went**.* (Incorrect; should be *I have **gone**.*)
- *He **has came**.* (Incorrect; should be *He has **come**.*)
- *She **has wrote**.* (Incorrect; should be *She has **written**.*)
[/alert]

### Section 4.3 | The Four Core Uses

1.  **Completed Action at a Definite Past Time:** The action is over, and the time is specified or implied.
    - *She **arrived** at 10 AM yesterday.*
    - *We **visited** the museum last week.*
    - **Signal Words:** *yesterday, last week/month/year, in 2020, two days ago, earlier, just now.*

2.  **Sequential Past Actions:** Narration of a series of completed events.
    - *He **entered** the room, **turned** on the light, and **sat** down.*
    - **Cognitive Rule:** The Past Simple is the default tense for storytelling. It moves the narrative forward.

3.  **Past Habits or States:** Actions that were habitual or states that existed in the past but are no longer true.
    - *When I was a child, I **played** cricket every evening.*
    - *She **worked** at a bank for ten years.* (She no longer works there).
    - **Distinction from 'Used To':** Both express past habits. *Used to* emphasises the contrast with the present (*I used to live in Delhi* – but I don't now). The Past Simple can do this, but *used to* is more emphatic. The exam often tests the correct use of *used to* vs *would*.

4.  **With 'For/Since' for a Completed Period:** Used when the duration is in the past and is now over.
    - *I **lived** in Mumbai for five years.* (I don't live there anymore).
    - Compare: *I **have lived** in Mumbai for five years.* (I still live there).
    - **Exam Trap:** The presence of 'for' or 'since' does not automatically require the Present Perfect. If the action is completed and the time period is over, the Past Simple is correct. *"I worked here for 10 years"* means I no longer work here. *"I have worked here for 10 years"* means I still do.

[alert type="info"]
**The 'Used To' vs 'Would' Distinction:** This is a subtle but high-frequency SSC trap.
- **Used to:** Expresses past *states* and *habits*.
    - *I **used to** be shy.* (State).
    - *I **used to** play cricket.* (Habit).
- **Would:** Expresses past *habits* only (repeated actions), not states.
    - *I **would** play cricket every Sunday.* (Habit – correct).
    - *I **would** be shy.* (State – incorrect).
- **Cognitive Rule:** If you can replace the verb with a state verb (be, have, know, like, etc.), use *used to*, not *would*.
[/alert]

## Chapter 5: Past Continuous (Progressive) Tense

### Section 5.1 | Structure and the Concept of Background

The Past Continuous paints an action in progress at a specific moment in the past. It provides the *background* to which other events occur. It is never used for completed actions or states.

**Structure:**

| Sentence Type | Formula | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Affirmative | Subject + was/were + V1 + -ing | *She **was reading**.* |
| Negative | Subject + was/were + **not** + V1 + -ing | *She **was not reading**.* |
| Interrogative | Was/Were + Subject + V1 + -ing + ? | ***Was** she reading?* |

### Section 5.2 | The Three Core Uses

1.  **Action in Progress at a Specific Past Time:**
    - *I **was watching** TV at 8 PM last night.*
    - *She **was sleeping** when I arrived.*

2.  **Interrupted Action (The Most Heavily Tested Use):** A longer action (Past Continuous) is interrupted by a shorter action (Past Simple).
    - *I **was eating** dinner when the phone **rang**.*
    - *She **was driving** to work when she **had** an accident.*
    - **Cognitive Structure:** =Past Continuous (ongoing background) + **when** + Past Simple (interrupting event).
    - **The Trap:** The reverse order is also possible but requires a different structure. =*When I **arrived**, she **was cooking** dinner.* The interrupting event (arrival) is in the Past Simple; the background action (cooking) is in the Past Continuous.

3.  **Simultaneous Ongoing Actions:**
    - *While I **was cooking**, my wife **was setting** the table.*
    - **Signal Word:** *while* is the primary marker for simultaneous past actions.

[alert type="danger"]
**The 'When' vs 'While' Distinction:** This is a critical SSC exam trap.
- **'When'** can be used with both Past Simple and Past Continuous.
    - *When I **arrived** (short action), she **was sleeping** (long action).*
    - *I was sleeping **when** the phone rang.*
- **'While'** is primarily used with Past Continuous (or Past Simple for longer actions).
    - *While I **was cooking**, she **was reading**.* (Correct).
    - *While I **cooked**, she **read**.* (Also correct, but emphasises a longer duration).
- **The Golden Rule:** 'While' + Past Continuous for the longer action; 'When' + Past Simple for the interrupting action.
[/alert]

## Chapter 6: Past Perfect Tense

### Section 6.1 | Structure and the Past-in-the-Past Concept

The Past Perfect establishes a temporal hierarchy. It indicates that one past action occurred *before* another past action. This is the "past of the past."

**Structure:**

| Sentence Type | Formula | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Affirmative | Subject + had + V3 | *She **had left**.* |
| Negative | Subject + had + **not** + V3 | *She **had not left**.* |
| Interrogative | Had + Subject + V3 + ? | ***Had** she left?* |

### Section 6.2 | The Two Core Uses

1.  **Action Completed Before Another Past Action:**
    - *I **had finished** my work before he **arrived**.*
    - *She **had already eaten** when I **called** her.*
    - **Cognitive Rule:** The Past Perfect identifies which of two past actions happened first. Without it, the sequence is ambiguous. With it, the sequence is crystal clear.

2.  **Backshifted Tense in Reported Speech:**
    - *He said, "I **have finished**."* (Direct: Present Perfect).
    - *He said that he **had finished**.* (Indirect: Past Perfect).

### Section 6.3 | The Past Simple vs Past Perfect Decision Matrix

This is the most challenging distinction in the past tense system. Use this cognitive filter:

| Scenario | Tense | Reasoning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Single past action. | Past Simple | No need to sequence. |
| Two past actions, sequence is chronological. | Past Simple for both | *I woke up, ate breakfast, and left.* |
| Two past actions, one clearly happened before the other, and the order matters. | Past Perfect (earlier action) + Past Simple (later action) | *I **had eaten** breakfast before I **left**.* |
| One action interrupted another. | Past Continuous (interrupted) + Past Simple (interrupter) | *I **was eating** when he **arrived**.* |

[alert type="danger"]
**The "After/Before" Trap:** When the sequence is explicitly signposted by *after* or *before*, the Past Perfect is often **not required** because the conjunction already establishes the order.
- =*After he **had finished** his work, he left.* (Correct but redundant).
- =*After he **finished** his work, he left.* (Also correct and more concise).
- **Exam Tip:** The SSC often tests this redundancy. If the question asks you to choose the correct sentence, the simpler form (both in Past Simple) is usually considered correct when *after* or *before* is present. The Past Perfect is necessary only when the sequence is not otherwise signposted or when the earlier action is emphasised.
[/alert]

[example]
**Question:** Choose the correct sentence:
- A) I had met him yesterday.
- B) I met him yesterday. (Correct)
- C) I have met him yesterday.
- D) I had meet him yesterday.
**Solution:** 'Yesterday' is a specific past time. The Past Simple is required. The Past Perfect is not used with specific past time markers unless it is embedded in a sequence (e.g., *I had met him once before yesterday*).
[/example]

## Chapter 7: Past Perfect Continuous Tense

### Section 7.1 | Structure and the Duration-Before-Past Concept

The Past Perfect Continuous focuses on the *duration* of an action that was in progress before another past event. It answers the question: "How long had something been happening?"

**Structure:**

| Sentence Type | Formula | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Affirmative | Subject + had + been + V1 + -ing | *She **had been waiting**.* |
| Negative | Subject + had + not + been + V1 + -ing | *She **had not been waiting**.* |
| Interrogative | Had + Subject + been + V1 + -ing + ? | ***Had** she **been waiting**?* |

### Section 7.2 | The Two Core Uses

1.  **Action in Progress Before Another Past Event (Emphasising Duration):**
    - *I **had been waiting** for two hours when the bus finally **arrived**.*
    - *She **had been working** at the company for ten years before she **resigned**.*
    - **Signal Words:** *for, since, for hours/days, all morning.*

2.  **Visible Result Before Another Past Event:**
    - *The ground was wet; it **had been raining**.* (It had stopped raining, but the result was visible).
    - *She was tired because she **had been studying** all night.*

### Section 7.3 | Past Perfect vs Past Perfect Continuous

| Aspect | Past Perfect | Past Perfect Continuous |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Focus** | Completion of the earlier action. | Duration of the earlier action. |
| **Use with Stative Verbs** | Yes (*I had known him for years*). | No (Stative verbs are not used in continuous). |
| **Result Orientation** | Emphasises that the action was finished. | Emphasises how long it had been happening. |

- **Example:** *I **had worked** there for ten years.* (Emphasis on the fact of the completed employment).
- **Example:** *I **had been working** there for ten years.* (Emphasis on the duration and the tiring or ongoing nature of the work).

## Chapter 8: Common Errors in Past Tenses

### Section 8.1 | The Tense Sequencing Error

- **Error:** *I **have seen** him yesterday.*
- **Correction:** *I **saw** him yesterday.* (Specific past time → Past Simple).
- **Error:** *When he **arrives**, I **was** cooking.*
- **Correction:** *When he **arrived**, I **was cooking**.* (Past actions must use past tenses).

### Section 8.2 | The 'Had' Confusion Error

- **Error:** *He **had** went to the market before she called.*
- **Correction:** *He **had gone** to the market before she called.* (Use V3 with 'had').
- **Error:** *He **had** not **went** to school.*
- **Correction:** *He **had** not **gone** to school.*

### Section 8.3 | The 'Since/For' Error in Past Tenses

- **Error (With a completed period):** *I **have worked** there for five years before I quit.*
- **Correction:** *I **worked** there for five years before I quit.* (The time period is over; use Past Simple, not Present Perfect).
- **Error (With a duration before another past action):** *I **worked** there for five years before I quit.* (This is correct if the focus is on the fact. If the focus is on the duration leading up to the resignation, Past Perfect Continuous is better: *I **had been working** there for five years before I quit.*)

### Section 8.4 | The Conditional Trap

- **Error:** *If I **would have known**, I would have come.*
- **Correction:** *If I **had known**, I would have come.* (In third conditional sentences, the 'if' clause takes the Past Perfect, not 'would have').

## Chapter 9: Narrative Past vs Simple Past Distinction

### Section 9.1 | The Cognitive Shift in Narration

In narrative writing (stories, anecdotes, historical accounts), the Past Simple is the dominant tense. It moves the plot forward. However, other past tenses are used to provide depth, background, and sequence.

**Narrative Past (Storytelling Mode):**

| Tense | Function in Narrative | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Past Simple | The main storyline; drives events forward. | *He **walked** into the room and **saw** the letter.* |
| Past Continuous | Sets the scene; provides background actions. | *The sun **was setting**, and birds **were singing**.* |
| Past Perfect | Provides background or flashback to an earlier event. | *He **had received** the news the previous day.* |
| Past Perfect Continuous | Provides background duration to a past event. | *He **had been waiting** for this moment for years.* |

### Section 9.2 | The "Past in the Past" Hierarchy

When narrating a complex past event, use this hierarchy:

1.  **The Most Recent Past Action:** Past Simple.
2.  **The Action in Progress at that Time:** Past Continuous.
3.  **The Action that Happened Earlier:** Past Perfect.
4.  **The Action that was Ongoing Earlier:** Past Perfect Continuous.

[example]
**Question:** Choose the correct option: "By the time we ______ at the stadium, the match ______ already ______."
- A) arrived; had; started (Correct)
- B) had arrived; had; started
- C) arrived; was; starting
- D) have arrived; has; started
**Solution:** "By the time" indicates a sequence where one action happened before another. The arrival (Past Simple) happened after the match started (Past Perfect). The correct structure is Past Perfect for the earlier action.
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** Spot the error: "She was driving to work when she had seen an accident."
**Solution:** The error is "had seen." When an action interrupts a continuous action, the interrupting action takes the Past Simple. The corrected sentence is: "She was driving to work when she **saw** an accident."
[/example]

[alert type="success"]
**Final Mastery Principle:** The past tense system is a chronology machine. Your task is to place events on a timeline and select the tense that accurately reflects their position and duration relative to the present and to each other. When you see a sentence with multiple past actions, your first question must always be: **"Which happened first?"** That single question, answered correctly, will guide you to the correct tense every time.
