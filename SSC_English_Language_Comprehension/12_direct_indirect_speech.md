# Direct & Indirect Speech
> The Art of Filtration: Reconstructing Utterances Without Distorting Intent

## Chapter 12: Direct & Indirect Speech

### Section 1 | The Fundamental Distinction: Quotation vs. Narration

Every spoken or written utterance can be relayed to a third party in one of two ways. The choice between them is not merely mechanical; it alters the listener's relationship to the original words.

**Direct Speech** presents the exact words of the original speaker, enclosed within quotation marks (inverted commas). It is a word-for-word reproduction, a linguistic photograph.

- *Ravi said, "I am feeling unwell today."*

**Indirect Speech** (also called Reported Speech) conveys the *content* of the original utterance without using the speaker's exact words. The quotation marks disappear, and the sentence structure transforms to integrate the reported words into the reporting clause.

- *Ravi said that he was feeling unwell that day.*

The transformation from direct to indirect speech requires systematic adjustments across four dimensions: **tense**, **pronouns**, **time and place references**, and **sentence structure**. The Reporting Verb (e.g., *said, told, asked*) serves as the anchor around which the entire conversion pivots.

=The reporting verb dictates the framework; the reported speech provides the content to be reshaped.=

### Section 2 | The Golden Rules: Pronouns, Tenses, and the Logic of Perspective

#### Pronoun Changes: The Speaker's Eye

Pronouns shift according to the relationship between the speaker, the listener, and the person being reported. This is governed by a simple principle of perspective.

**The First-Person Rule:** First-person pronouns (*I, we, my, our, me, us*) in direct speech change according to the *subject* of the reporting verb.

- *Direct: He said, "I am going."* → *Indirect: He said that he was going.*
- *Direct: I said, "I am going."* → *Indirect: I said that I was going.* (No change, as subject is 'I')

**The Second-Person Rule:** Second-person pronouns (*you, your*) change according to the *object* of the reporting verb.

- *Direct: He said to me, "You are right."* → *Indirect: He told me that I was right.*
- *Direct: He said to her, "You are right."* → *Indirect: He told her that she was right.*

**The Third-Person Rule:** Third-person pronouns (*he, she, it, they, his, her, their, him, them*) remain unchanged. They have no direct connection to the speaker or listener in the reporting context.

- *Direct: He said, "She will come."* → *Indirect: He said that she would come.*

[alert type="warning"]
**The Object-Omission Trap:** The pronoun changes for second person hinge entirely on the *presence* of an object after the reporting verb. If no object exists, the default change follows the subject. Examinees frequently apply the second-person rule even when the reporting verb lacks an object.
[/alert]

#### Tense Changes: The Sequence of Tenses

The tense of the reported verb typically shifts "backward" when the reporting verb is in the past tense. This is called the **Sequence of Tenses** and follows a predictable pattern.

| Direct Speech Tense | Indirect Speech Tense | Example (Direct → Indirect) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Simple Present (V1) | Simple Past (V2) | "I *play* well." → He said he *played* well. |
| Present Continuous | Past Continuous | "I *am playing*." → He said he *was playing*. |
| Present Perfect | Past Perfect | "I *have played*." → He said he *had played*. |
| Present Perfect Continuous | Past Perfect Continuous | "I *have been playing*." → He said he *had been playing*. |
| Simple Past | Past Perfect | "I *played*." → He said he *had played*. |
| Past Continuous | Past Perfect Continuous | "I *was playing*." → He said he *had been playing*. |
| Past Perfect | Past Perfect (No Change) | "I *had played*." → He said he *had played*. |
| Will/Shall | Would/Should | "I *will* come." → He said he *would* come. |
| Can/May | Could/Might | "I *can* do it." → He said he *could* do it. |
| Must | Must/Had to | "I *must* go." → He said he *must* go. |
| Should/Ought to | Should/Ought to (No Change) | "I *should* leave." → He said he *should* leave. |

**The Cognitive Logic:** This backward shift is not arbitrary. It reflects the fact that the original utterance occurred *before* the reporting act. The past tense in the reporting verb pushes all other actions further into the past relative to that point.

### Section 3 | Statement and Assertive Sentences

Statements are the simplest declarative structures. The transformation is straightforward: the reporting verb *said/said to* becomes *said/told*, and the conjunction **that** connects the reporting clause to the reported clause.

**The 'Said' vs. 'Told' Distinction:**
- **Said:** Used without an object. (He said that...)
- **Told:** Used *only* when there is a personal object. (He told *me* that...) **'Told' cannot be used without an object.**

[alert type="danger"]
**The Elite-Level Trap:** In error-detection questions, examinees often encounter the incorrect pairing "said me." This is a cardinal sin in formal English. If there is an object, the verb *must* be 'told'. If there is no object, the verb *must* be 'said'.
[/alert]

**The Complete Transformation Algorithm for Statements:**
1. Remove the quotation marks and comma.
2. Insert the conjunction 'that'.
3. Apply the Sequence of Tenses (change the tense if the reporting verb is past).
4. Change pronouns according to the speaker, listener, and object rules.
5. Change time and place words (see Section 6).

[example]
**Question:** Convert to Indirect Speech: *She said to her friend, "I have finished my homework."*

**Solution:**
- Step 1: Remove quotes and comma.
- Step 2: 'Said to' becomes 'told' (because 'friend' is the object).
- Step 3: Insert 'that'.
- Step 4: Tense change: Present Perfect → Past Perfect ('have finished' becomes 'had finished').
- Step 5: Pronoun change: 'I' (first person) → 'she' (subject of reporting verb). 'My' → 'her'.
- **Final Answer:** *She told her friend that she had finished her homework.*
[/example]

### Section 4 | Interrogative Sentences: The 'If/Whether' and 'Wh-' Divide

Questions in indirect speech cease to be questions in form; they become statements. The most critical structural change is the **removal of the interrogative word order** and the **restoration of the subject-verb order of a declarative sentence**. The question mark disappears; a full stop takes its place.

**Two Distinct Categories:**

**1. Yes/No Questions (No question word):** These questions expect a 'yes' or 'no' answer. They begin with auxiliary verbs (*is, are, am, was, were, has, have, had, do, does, did, will, shall, can, may, must*).
- **Rule:** The reporting verb changes to *asked* or *inquired*. The conjunction **if** or **whether** is used. The auxiliary verb moves after the subject.
- *Direct: He said to me, "Are you coming?"*
- *Indirect: He asked me if I was coming.*

**2. Wh-Questions:** These questions begin with interrogative words (*what, who, whom, whose, which, when, where, why, how*).
- **Rule:** The reporting verb changes to *asked* or *inquired*. The wh-word itself serves as the conjunction. The wh-word is followed by the subject, then the verb.
- *Direct: He said to me, "Where do you live?"*
- *Indirect: He asked me where I lived.*

**The Auxiliary Verb Placement Trap:** This is the most frequent source of errors in narration change questions. Examinees often retain the auxiliary verb before the subject, failing to restore the declarative structure.

- *Incorrect: He asked me where did I live.*
- *Correct: He asked me where I lived.*

[alert type="success"]
**Cognitive Rule of Thumb:** When converting an interrogative sentence, force yourself to mentally rephrase the question as a statement before adding the 'if' or wh-word. For *"Are you coming?"* the statement is *"You are coming."* Then apply the conjunction: *"if you were coming."*
[/alert]

### Section 5 | Imperative Sentences: Orders, Requests, Advice, and Prohibitions

Imperatives express commands, requests, suggestions, or prohibitions. They do not have a grammatical subject in their surface structure. The transformation logic is fundamentally different from statements and questions.

**The Structural Algorithm:**
1. The reporting verb *said/said to* is replaced by a verb that indicates the **illocutionary force** of the utterance. (See table below).
2. The imperative verb is converted into a **'to' infinitive** (V1 preceded by 'to').
3. The comma and quotation marks are removed. No conjunction like 'that' is used.

| Direct Speech (Imperative) | Reporting Verb Choice | Indirect Speech Structure |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *"Open the door."* | ordered, commanded, told | He told/ordered me **to open** the door. |
| *"Please help me."* | requested, begged, implored | He requested me **to help** him. |
| *"Work hard."* | advised, urged, encouraged | He advised me **to work** hard. |
| *"Do not smoke."* | forbade, warned, advised against | He forbade me **to smoke**. (OR: He warned me **not to smoke**.) |

**The Prohibition Rule:** The negative imperative (*"Do not..."*) has two acceptable passive forms:
- Direct: He said, "Do not touch the wire."
- Indirect (Using 'forbade'): He **forbade** me to touch the wire.
- Indirect (Using 'not to'): He **told** me not to touch the wire.

[alert type="warning"]
**The 'Forbade' vs. 'Not To' Decision:** 'Forbade' is a single verb that inherently carries the negative sense. When using 'forbade', you do *not* add 'not' with the infinitive. The construction is *forbade + object + to + V1*. Adding 'not' would create a double negative. If you use 'told' or 'advised', you must use the *not to + V1* construction.
[/alert]

### Section 6 | Exclamatory Sentences: The Grammar of Emotion

Exclamations express strong feelings—surprise, joy, sorrow, disgust, or a wish. The transformation is subtle and often involves changing a noun phrase or an adjective into a clause.

**The Transformation Protocol:**
1. The reporting verb *said* is replaced by an expressive reporting verb like *exclaimed, shouted, cried, exclaimed with joy/sorrow,* or *wished*.
2. The exclamatory marker (*How, What, Alas!, Hurrah!, Bravo!*) is omitted.
3. The exclamation mark is replaced by a full stop.
4. The exclamatory structure is converted into a declarative sentence using the words *very*, *great*, or *big* to convey the intensity.

| Direct Speech (Exclamatory) | Indirect Speech Structure |
| :--- | :--- |
| *He said, "What a beautiful painting!"* | He exclaimed that it was a very beautiful painting. |
| *She said, "How fast he runs!"* | She exclaimed that he ran very fast. |
| *He said, "Hurrah! We have won."* | He exclaimed with joy that they had won. |
| *She said, "Alas! I am ruined."* | She exclaimed with sorrow that she was ruined. |
| *He said, "Good morning!"* | He wished me good morning. |
| *She said, "May God bless you!"* | She prayed that God might bless me. |

### Section 7 | The Lexicon of Time and Place: Shifting the Deictic Center

Deictic words—those that point to a specific time or place relative to the speaker—must shift when the perspective changes from the original speaker to the reporter. This is a mechanical rule but requires careful attention.

| Direct Speech | Indirect Speech |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Now** | **Then** |
| **Today** | **That day** |
| **Yesterday** | **The previous day / The day before** |
| **Tomorrow** | **The next day / The following day** |
| **Last night/week** | **The previous night/week** |
| **Next week/month** | **The following week/month** |
| **Ago** | **Before / Previously** |
| **Here** | **There** |
| **This** | **That** |
| **These** | **Those** |
| **Hence** | **Thence** |
| **Thus** | **So / In that way** |

[alert type="info"]
**The Contextual Exception:** These changes are *not* automatic. If the reporting takes place in the same location or on the same day as the original utterance, the deictic words do not change. For example: *Direct: He said, "I will meet you here tomorrow."* If I report this the same day in the same place, the indirect speech remains *"He said he would meet me here tomorrow."* The rule yields to the reality of the communicative context.
[/alert]

### Section 8 | Universal Truths, Historical Facts, and Habitual Actions: The Immutable Exception

Certain categories of statements are so fundamentally true or fixed in time that they do not obey the normal tense-change rules. If the reported clause expresses a **universal truth**, a **historical fact**, a **scientific law**, or a **habitual action**, its tense remains unchanged, regardless of the tense of the reporting verb.

- *Direct: The teacher said, "The earth revolves around the sun."*
- *Indirect: The teacher said that the earth revolves around the sun.* (Tense does NOT change)

- *Direct: He said, "India became independent in 1947."*
- *Indirect: He said that India became independent in 1947.* (Tense does NOT change)

- *Direct: She said, "I usually wake up early."*
- *Indirect: She said that she usually wakes up early.* (Tense does NOT change)

[alert type="danger"]
**The Examiner's Favorite:** This rule is a perennial favorite in competitive exams. Examiners will present a sentence with a universal truth and tempt you to shift the tense. The correct response is to recognize the exception and leave the tense unchanged.
[/alert]

### Section 9 | The Cognitive Map: Common Errors and Mental Models

#### Error Cluster 1: The Conjunction Omission
Examinees often forget to add 'that' in assertive sentences or add it unnecessarily in interrogative or imperative sentences. The rule is: **'That' is used exclusively for assertive (declarative) sentences.**

- *Correct (Assertive): He said that he was tired.*
- *Correct (Interrogative): He asked me where I lived. (No 'that')*
- *Correct (Imperative): He ordered me to sit down. (No 'that')*

#### Error Cluster 2: The 'Said to' vs. 'Told' Blindness
If the direct speech has 'said to' and an object is present, it *must* change to 'told'. If there is no object, it remains 'said'. The object is not optional for 'told'.

- *Incorrect: He said me that he was late.*
- *Correct: He told me that he was late.*

#### Error Cluster 3: The Interrogative Inversion
Forgetting to revert to subject-verb order in question transformations is the most persistent error. This is a cognitive habit from reading questions as questions.

- *Incorrect: He asked me what is my name.*
- *Correct: He asked me what my name was.*

#### Error Cluster 4: The Imperative Pronoun Gap
In imperative sentences, the implied subject is 'you'. When reporting, the object of the reporting verb provides the reference for the infinitive. If no object is specified in the direct speech, one is often implied.

- *Direct: He said, "Go away."* (Implied: to me or to him)
- *Indirect: He told me to go away.* OR *He told him to go away.* (Context decides)
