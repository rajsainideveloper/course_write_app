# Conjunctions
> Conjunctions are the logical circuitry of language—they wire together words, phrases, and clauses, encoding the relationships of addition, contrast, cause, and condition that give sentences their intellectual architecture.

## Chapter 9: The Architecture of Connection

### Section 9.1 | The Cognitive Framework of Conjunctions

A conjunction is a word that connects. But the nature of that connection varies dramatically. Some conjunctions join equals (coordinating); others create hierarchies (subordinating); and still others work in pairs to create balanced structures (correlative). The exam tests not just your ability to identify these connectors, but your ability to apply the grammatical rules that govern them—parallelism, punctuation, and the logical relationship they encode.

[alert type="warning"]
**The Cognitive Starting Point:** Do not treat conjunctions as mere "joining words." Each conjunction encodes a specific logical relationship: addition (*and*), contrast (*but*), cause (*because*), condition (*if*), time (*when*), and many more. The correct conjunction is the one that accurately maps the logical relationship between the connected elements.
[/alert]

### Section 9.2 | The Two Primary Categories

| Category | Function | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Coordinating Conjunctions** | Join equal grammatical elements (words, phrases, independent clauses) | *and, but, or, nor, for, yet, so* |
| **Subordinating Conjunctions** | Join an independent clause with a dependent clause (creating a hierarchy) | *because, although, when, while, since, unless, if* |
| **Correlative Conjunctions** | Pairs of conjunctions that work together to join equal elements | *either...or, neither...nor, both...and, not only...but also* |

## Chapter 10: Coordinating Conjunctions – FANBOYS

### Section 10.1 | The Seven Coordinators

The coordinating conjunctions are remembered by the acronym **FANBOYS**: **F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et, **S**o. They join grammatically equal elements.

| Conjunction | Function | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **For** | Reason/cause (similar to 'because') | *He stayed home, **for** he was ill.* |
| **And** | Addition | *She sings **and** dances.* |
| **Nor** | Negative addition | *He did not call, **nor** did he write.* |
| **But** | Contrast | *I wanted to go, **but** I was tired.* |
| **Or** | Alternative/choice | *Would you like tea **or** coffee?* |
| **Yet** | Contrast (similar to 'but') | *She is poor, **yet** she is happy.* |
| **So** | Result/consequence | *He was late, **so** he missed the train.* |

### Section 10.2 | The Rule of Parallel Structure

When a coordinating conjunction joins elements, those elements must be **grammatically parallel**—they must have the same grammatical form.

- =*She likes **reading**, **writing**, **and** **painting**.* (Nouns/gerunds – parallel).
- =*He is **intelligent**, **hardworking**, **and** **honest**.* (Adjectives – parallel).
- =*I came, **I saw**, **and** **I conquered**.* (Clauses – parallel).

[alert type="danger"]
**The Parallelism Trap:** This is one of the most frequently tested errors on competitive exams. The examiner will give you a sentence where the elements joined by 'and' or 'or' are not parallel.
- **Error:** *She likes to sing, dance, and painting.*
- **Correction:** *She likes **singing**, **dancing**, and **painting**.* (All gerunds).
- **Or:** *She likes **to sing**, **to dance**, and **to paint**.* (All infinitives).
- **Error:** *He is smart, hardworking, and has honesty.*
- **Correction:** *He is **smart**, **hardworking**, and **honest**.* (All adjectives).
[/alert]

### Section 10.3 | Punctuation with Coordinating Conjunctions

- When a coordinating conjunction joins **two independent clauses**, use a **comma** before the conjunction.
    - *I wanted to go to the party, **but** I was too tired.*
- When a coordinating conjunction joins **two words** or **two phrases**, no comma is needed.
    - *I like tea **and** coffee.*
    - *He ran quickly **but** clumsily.*

[alert type="info"]
**The 'And' Comma Rule (Oxford Comma):** In a list of three or more items, a comma before the final 'and' (the Oxford comma) is optional but recommended for clarity.
- *I bought apples, bananas, and oranges.* (With Oxford comma – clear).
- *I bought apples, bananas and oranges.* (Without Oxford comma – also acceptable).
[/alert]

## Chapter 11: Subordinating Conjunctions

### Section 11.1 | The Hierarchy of Clauses

Subordinating conjunctions introduce a **dependent clause** and connect it to an **independent clause**. The dependent clause cannot stand alone; it relies on the main clause for its meaning.

- =*I stayed home **because** I was ill.* (Independent: *I stayed home*; Dependent: *because I was ill*).

### Section 11.2 | The Seven Functional Categories

Subordinating conjunctions encode specific logical relationships.

| Category | Conjunctions | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cause/Reason** | *because, since, as* | *I went to bed **because** I was tired.* |
| **Contrast/Concession** | *although, though, even though, whereas, while* | *She passed the exam **although** she did not study.* |
| **Condition** | *if, unless, provided that, as long as* | *I will go **if** you come with me.* |
| **Time** | *when, while, as, before, after, until, since, whenever* | *She called **when** I was sleeping.* |
| **Purpose** | *so that, in order that* | *He studied hard **so that** he could pass.* |
| **Place** | *where, wherever* | *She goes **wherever** she pleases.* |
| **Manner** | *as, as if, as though* | *She acted **as if** she knew the answer.* |

### Section 11.3 | 'Since' – The Time vs Reason Trap

'Since' can function as both a subordinating conjunction of time and of cause/reason. This ambiguity is frequently tested.

- **Time:** *I have not seen him **since** he left Mumbai.*
- **Reason:** *I went home early **since** I was feeling unwell.*
- **The Trap:** When 'since' is used to mean 'because', it is acceptable but can cause ambiguity. In formal exam writing, 'because' is preferred for clear cause-reason statements. The examiner may test whether you choose the more precise conjunction.

### Section 11.4 | Punctuation with Subordinating Conjunctions

- When the dependent clause comes **first**, place a **comma** after it.
    - ***Because** I was tired, I went to bed early.*
- When the dependent clause comes **second** (after the independent clause), no comma is needed.
    - *I went to bed early **because** I was tired.*

[alert type="danger"]
**The 'Although/But' Trap:** Do not use a subordinating conjunction (*although*) and a coordinating conjunction (*but*) in the same sentence to express contrast. This creates a double contrast—a redundancy.
- **Error:** ***Although** he is rich, **but** he is not happy.*
- **Correction:** ***Although** he is rich, he is not happy.*
- **Or:** *He is rich, **but** he is not happy.*
[/alert]

## Chapter 12: Correlative Conjunctions

### Section 12.1 | The Balanced Pairs

Correlative conjunctions are pairs that work together to connect equal grammatical elements. They demand **parallel structure**.

| Pair | Function | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Either...or** | Choice between two | *You can **either** go **or** stay.* |
| **Neither...nor** | Negative choice between two | *He is **neither** intelligent **nor** hardworking.* |
| **Both...and** | Addition of two | *She is **both** kind **and** generous.* |
| **Not only...but also** | Emphatic addition | *He is **not only** smart **but also** dedicated.* |
| **Whether...or** | Choice/uncertainty | *I will go **whether** you come **or** not.* |

### Section 12.2 | The Parallelism Requirement

Correlative conjunctions must be followed by grammatically equal elements.

- **Correct:** *She is **not only** intelligent **but also** hardworking.* (Adjective + Adjective).
- **Error:** *She is **not only** intelligent **but also** works hard.* (Adjective + Verb phrase).
- **Correct:** *He **both** sings **and** dances.* (Verb + Verb).
- **Error:** *He **both** sings **and** is a dancer.* (Verb + Noun phrase).

[alert type="danger"]
**The Subject-Verb Agreement Trap with 'Either/Or' and 'Neither/Nor':** The verb agrees with the subject closest to it (the Proximity Rule).
- *Neither the teacher **nor** the students **are** present.*
- *Neither the students **nor** the teacher **is** present.*
- *Either the manager **or** his assistants **have** the authority.*
- *Either the assistants **or** the manager **has** the authority.*
[/alert]

### Section 12.3 | 'Not Only...But Also' – The Inversion Rule

When 'not only' begins a sentence, it often triggers **subject-verb inversion**.

- ***Not only** **is** she intelligent, **but also** she is hardworking.*
- **The Trap:** The second part ('but also') must be parallel and the inversion is optional but formal. In standard sentences, 'not only' can appear in the middle of the sentence without inversion:
    - *She is **not only** intelligent **but also** hardworking.*
- The exam expects you to recognise both forms.

[flashcards title="Correlative Conjunction Rules"]
| Pair | Rule | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *Either...or* | Parallel structure; verb agrees with closest subject | *Either you **or** I **am** wrong.* |
| *Neither...nor* | Parallel structure; verb agrees with closest subject | *Neither he **nor** we **are** ready.* |
| *Both...and* | Always plural verb | *Both he **and** she **are** coming.* |
| *Not only...but also* | Parallel structure | *Not only did she sing, but also she danced.* |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 13: Conjunctive Adverbs

### Section 13.1 | The Transitional Connectors

Conjunctive adverbs are not conjunctions in the grammatical sense—they do not join clauses with the same syntactic status. Instead, they show logical relationships between independent clauses. They are **transitional words** that require specific punctuation.

| Conjunctive Adverb | Function | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **However** | Contrast | *He is rich; **however**, he is not happy.* |
| **Therefore** | Result/consequence | *She studied hard; **therefore**, she passed.* |
| **Moreover** | Addition | *The plan is expensive; **moreover**, it is risky.* |
| **Furthermore** | Addition | *She is talented; **furthermore**, she is hardworking.* |
| **Nevertheless** | Contrast (despite) | *He failed; **nevertheless**, he tried again.* |
| **Consequently** | Result | *He missed the bus; **consequently**, he was late.* |
| **Meanwhile** | Time | *She was cooking; **meanwhile**, he was setting the table.* |
| **Otherwise** | Alternative/condition | *Hurry up; **otherwise**, we will miss the train.* |
| **Thus** | Result/conclusion | *He was honest; **thus**, he was trusted.* |

### Section 13.2 | Punctuation with Conjunctive Adverbs

Conjunctive adverbs require specific punctuation to connect independent clauses.

1.  **Semicolon + Conjunctive Adverb + Comma:**
    - *She worked hard; **therefore**, she succeeded.*
2.  **Period + Conjunctive Adverb + Comma (as a new sentence):**
    - *She worked hard. **Therefore**, she succeeded.*

[alert type="danger"]
**The Comma Splice Trap:** Do not use a comma to join two independent clauses with a conjunctive adverb. This creates a comma splice.
- **Incorrect:** *She worked hard, **therefore**, she succeeded.*
- **Correct:** *She worked hard; **therefore**, she succeeded.*
[/alert]

## Chapter 14: Common Errors with Conjunctions

### Section 14.1 | Double Conjunction Error

- **Error:** *I will go **if** **only** you come.*
- **Correction:** *I will go **if** you come.* (Avoid redundant pairs).
- **Error:** ***Although** he tried hard, **yet** he failed.*
- **Correction:** ***Although** he tried hard, he failed.*
- **Or:** *He tried hard, **yet** he failed.*

### Section 14.2 | Unnecessary Conjunction Error

- **Error:** *The reason **why** he left **was because** he was angry.* (Double cause: 'reason' + 'why' + 'because' is redundant).
- **Correction:** *The reason he left **was that** he was angry.*
- **Correction:** *He left **because** he was angry.*
- **Error:** *I **have** never seen such a beautiful place **before**.* ('Have' and 'before' are redundant when used together in this way).
- **Correction:** *I have never seen such a beautiful place.*

### Section 14.3 | 'Because' vs 'Since' Error

- **Error:** *I went home early **since** I was feeling unwell.* (Acceptable, but 'because' is more direct).
- **Correction (for clarity):** *I went home early **because** I was feeling unwell.*

### Section 14.4 | 'Unless' with Negative Verb Error

- **Error:** *Unless you **do not** hurry, you will miss the bus.*
- **Correction:** *Unless you hurry, you will miss the bus.*
- **Rule:** 'Unless' means 'if not'; do not use a negative verb with it.

### Section 14.5 | 'No sooner...than' and 'Hardly/Scarcely...when' Errors

- **Rule:** *No sooner* is followed by **than** (not 'but' or 'when').
    - ***No sooner** had I arrived **than** it started raining.*
- **Rule:** *Hardly/Scarcely* is followed by **when** (not 'than').
    - ***Hardly** had I arrived **when** it started raining.*
- **The Inversion:** In formal English, the auxiliary verb is inverted with the subject.
    - *No sooner **had** I arrived than it started raining.*
    - *Scarcely **had** I entered when the phone rang.*

[alert type="warning"]
**The 'No Sooner' Trap:** This is a high-frequency SSC question. The examiner will test whether you know that 'no sooner' must be followed by 'than', not 'when' or 'but'.
- **Correct:** *No sooner did he leave **than** the door closed.*
- **Incorrect:** *No sooner did he leave **when** the door closed.*
[/alert]

## Chapter 15: Punctuation Rules with Conjunctions

### Section 15.1 | The Comma with Coordinating Conjunctions

- **Rule:** Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction that joins two independent clauses.
    - *She wanted to go to the party, **but** she was too tired.*
- **No Comma:** When joining two words or phrases, no comma is needed.
    - *She sang **and** danced.*
    - *He ran quickly **but** clumsily.*

### Section 15.2 | The Semicolon with Conjunctive Adverbs

- **Rule:** Use a semicolon (;) before a conjunctive adverb and a comma after it.
    - *He was exhausted; **however**, he continued working.*

### Section 15.3 | The Comma with Subordinating Conjunctions

- **Rule:** Use a comma when the dependent clause comes first.
    - ***Because** it was raining, we stayed home.*
- **No Comma:** No comma is needed when the dependent clause comes second.
    - *We stayed home **because** it was raining.*

[flashcards title="Punctuation Rules - Quick Reference"]
| Structure | Punctuation | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Coordinating + 2 independent clauses | Comma before conjunction | *I came, **and** I saw.* |
| Coordinating + 2 words/phrases | No comma | *I like tea **and** coffee.* |
| Subordinating (clause first) | Comma after dependent clause | ***Although** I was tired, I worked.* |
| Subordinating (clause second) | No comma | *I worked **although** I was tired.* |
| Conjunctive adverb | Semicolon + adverb + comma | *I was tired; **however**, I worked.* |
[/flashcards]

[example]
**Question:** Choose the correct sentence:
- A) Although he was tired, but he finished the project.
- B) Although he was tired, he finished the project. (Correct)
- C) He was tired, but he finished the project. (Also correct)
- D) Both B and C (Correct)
**Solution:** 'Although' and 'but' are both contrast markers. Using them together is redundant. Option B uses 'although' correctly; Option C uses 'but' correctly. Both are grammatically correct.
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** Identify the error: "No sooner did he enter the room when the alarm started ringing."
**Solution:** The error is 'when'. 'No sooner' must be followed by 'than'. The corrected sentence is: "No sooner did he enter the room **than** the alarm started ringing."
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** Fill in the blank: "The reason ______ he failed ______ because he did not study."
- A) why; was (Correct: "The reason why he failed was that...")
- B) why; is
- C) that; was
- D) No error
**Solution:** The correct construction is "The reason why... was that..." Avoid redundancy: don't use 'because' after 'reason why'. 'The reason' already implies a cause.
[/example]

[alert type="success"]
**Final Mastery Principle:** Conjunctions are the logical connectors of language. Each conjunction encodes a specific relationship between ideas—addition, contrast, cause, condition, or time. The exam tests not just your knowledge of these connectors, but your ability to apply the grammatical rules that govern them: parallelism, punctuation, and the avoidance of redundancy. When you encounter a conjunction question, your first questions must be: **"What is the logical relationship between these two elements?"** and **"Are they grammatically parallel?"** Master these two questions, and you will navigate any conjunction question with precision.
[/alert]
