# Synonyms (Advanced & Context Based)
> The architecture of meaning: how precise synonym selection reveals the hidden scaffolding of the English language and separates competent readers from master linguists.

## Chapter 17: The Architecture of Lexical Choice

The world-class linguist Uriel Weinreich once observed that synonyms are not words that mean the same thing, but words that mean the *nearly* same thing—and that *nearly* is where the entire edifice of advanced English comprehension either stands or collapses. Competitive examinations at the SSC Tier-II, Bank PO, and CDS levels are not testing your ability to match words with dictionary definitions. They are testing your cognitive capacity to discriminate among shades of meaning, register, connotation, and collocational context. This chapter dismantles the naïve assumption that synonyms are interchangeable and reconstructs a sophisticated mental model for lexical precision.

### Section 17.1 | The Cognitive Error of "Same Meaning"

=Synonyms are approximate, not identical. The illusion of interchangeability is the single greatest source of error in advanced vocabulary questions.=

When students encounter a word like `obdurate`, they memorise "stubborn." When they encounter `intransigent`, they memorise "stubborn." When they encounter `recalcitrant`, they memorise "stubborn." Then they sit for the examination and discover that only one of these fits a particular blank, and the other two are traps. The examination is not testing your knowledge of *stubborn*. It is testing your knowledge of the *type* of stubbornness, the *context* in which it appears, and the *grammatical environment* it demands.

Consider this cognitive framework: every word occupies a coordinate in a multidimensional semantic space. The axes of this space include:

- **Denotation**: the literal, dictionary meaning
- **Connotation**: the emotional or cultural overlay (positive, negative, neutral)
- **Register**: formal, informal, colloquial, literary, technical, archaic
- **Collocational field**: the set of words that naturally accompany it
- **Intensity**: the degree or strength of the meaning
- **Grammatical behaviour**: whether it takes a preposition, a clause, a gerund, or an infinitive

Mastery of synonyms means navigating this space with precision. The examination rewards the candidate who can distinguish `censure` (formal, public, institutional) from `reprimand` (official, hierarchical, workplace) from `rebuke` (sharp, personal, emotional) from `reproach` (disappointment, moral failure, quieter in tone).

[alert type="danger"]
**The Examination Trap**: SSC and Bank PO examinations frequently pair synonyms that share a broad meaning but differ in transitivity, prepositional requirement, or countable/uncountable status. A word like `comprise` takes no preposition in its active form ("The team comprises eleven players"), while `compose` requires `of` in the passive ("The team is composed of eleven players"). Synonyms they are not; traps they most certainly are.
[/alert]

### Section 17.2 | Advanced Synonyms: The Rare Lexicon of Literary and Formal English

The SSC Tier-II examination has, in recent years, elevated its vocabulary section to include words that appear in the editorials of *The Hindu*, the literary criticism of *The New Yorker*, and the administrative reports of the civil services. These are not the `ebullient` and `ephemeral` of the Tier-I examination; these are the words that require a reading age of seventeen or above and an acquaintance with Latin and Greek roots.

Let us establish a working taxonomy of advanced synonyms organised not alphabetically but by semantic field. This structure mirrors how the brain actually stores lexical items—not in alphabetical lists but in associative networks.

#### The Lexicon of Deception and Concealment

The examination frequently presents a cluster of words meaning "to hide" or "to deceive," but each operates in a distinct grammatical and contextual environment.

`Dissemble`: to conceal one's true motives, feelings, or beliefs by pretence. *He dissembled his disappointment with a forced smile.* —Grammatically transitive; the object is typically an emotion or intention, not a physical object. The critical distinction: dissembling involves *pretence of the opposite*—you feign pleasure when you feel displeasure.

`Dissimulate`: similar to dissemble, but with a stronger implication of *hiding under a false appearance*. The distinction is subtle but real: dissimulate often carries the sense of *simulating* something that is not there, whereas dissemble often carries the sense of *concealing* something that *is* there. Examiners love this pair for its ability to differentiate advanced from intermediate candidates.

`Obscure`: to make unclear, to hide from view or understanding. This is the everyday word, but at advanced levels, it appears in collocations: *obscure the issue*, *obscure the truth*, *obscure the line between X and Y*. The prepositional behaviour is critical: `obscured by` (passive agent), `obscured from` (view), `obscured in` (location).

`Obfuscate`: to render obscure, to confuse or bewilder. The distinction from `obscure` is intent: obfuscation is *deliberate* confusion. *The politician obfuscated the statistics to hide the economic decline.* —Always transitive; the object is usually an idea, argument, or set of facts.

`Prevaricate`: to speak or act evasively, to deviate from the truth. This is not lying outright; it is *avoiding the truth* through equivocation. *When asked about his whereabouts, he prevaricated.* —Not transitive; takes no direct object. Compare with `equivocate`: to use ambiguous language so as to conceal the truth. The subtle difference: equivocation is a *linguistic* strategy; prevarication can be behavioural.

`Equivocate`: to use deliberately ambiguous or unclear language. *The witness equivocated under cross-examination.* —More specific to *speech acts* than prevaricate.

[alert type="info"]
**Memory Architecture**: Group these words not by meaning but by *grammatical behaviour*:
- Transitive: dissemble, dissimulate, obscure, obfuscate
- Intransitive: prevaricate, equivocate, fabricate (when meaning "to tell lies")
This grammatical clustering prevents the error of using an intransitive verb with a direct object—a mistake that immediately signals weakness to the examiner.
[/alert]

#### The Lexicon of Flattery and Praise

The distinction between genuine praise and manipulative flattery is one of the most frequently tested semantic distinctions in competitive examinations.

`Laud`: to praise highly, especially in a formal or public context. *The critics lauded the director's innovative technique.* —Register: formal to literary. The collocational field includes `laudable` (admirable), `laudatory` (expressing praise), and the noun `laud` (praise, now archaic in common use but appearing in literary contexts).

`Extol`: to praise enthusiastically, often with a moral or spiritual dimension. *The hymns extol the virtues of the divine.* —More elevated and fervent than `laud`. The difference is intensity and tone: extol is *exalted* praise; laud is *official* praise.

`Acclaim`: to praise openly and enthusiastically, often by a group or public. *The novel was acclaimed as a masterpiece.* —Implies public recognition and approval. The noun `acclaim` appears frequently in collocations like `critical acclaim`, `popular acclaim`, `international acclaim`.

`Eulogise`: to praise highly in speech or writing, especially in a formal tribute; often used of someone deceased. *He was eulogised as the greatest poet of his generation.* —The distinctiveness comes from the *occasion*: eulogy is praise at a funeral or memorial, though it can be extended metaphorically.

`Panegyrize`: to praise in a formal, elaborate speech or writing. This is the most formal and literary of the group, appearing almost exclusively in academic or classical contexts. *The poet panegyrised the monarch in elaborate verse.* —Not tested frequently, but when it appears, it separates the top 1% of candidates.

[alert type="success"]
**Key Rule for Connotation**: 
=Words of praise are distinguished not by their core meaning but by their COLLOCATIONAL FIELD and GRAMMATICAL BEHAVIOUR.=
`Eulogise` takes `as` or `for`: "He was eulogised as a hero." `Extol` takes `for`: "They extolled him for his courage." `Laud` takes `for` or the infinitive: "She was lauded for her work" or "She was lauded to have achieved the impossible." Memorise these prepositional requirements; they are the examination's favourite hidden weapon.
[/alert]

### Section 17.3 | Context-Based Synonyms: The Chameleon Words

The most formidable questions in the Tier-II examination are those that present a single word with multiple meanings and ask you to select the synonym that fits the *specific context* provided. These words are not rare—they are familiar—but they are chameleons. Their meaning changes not merely with context but with syntactic environment, collocational neighbour, and even the implied temporal frame.

#### The Behaviour of "Qualify"

Consider the word `qualify`. In one context, it means "to meet the requirements": *She qualified for the examination.* In another, it means "to limit or modify": *He qualified his statement with a caveat.* In yet another, it means "to describe or characterise": *The adjective qualifies the noun.* The SSC examination is not testing your knowledge of *qualify*; it is testing your ability to read the context and select the precise synonym among `appropriate`, `certify`, `moderate`, `restrict`, `diminish`, and `attribute`.

The cognitive strategy: when you see a common word in a synonym question, immediately **suspect** that the tested meaning is the *least common* one. The examination sets traps by offering the obvious synonym as the first option—the one that fits the word's most frequent usage—while the correct answer is the one that fits the *specific sentence*.

[example]
**Question:** Select the synonym of "qualify" as used in the following sentence:
*"The judge's instructions served to qualify the jury's understanding of the burden of proof."*

- A) Certify
- B) Restrict
- C) Clarify
- D) Enhance

**Solution:** B) Restrict. The context indicates that the instructions *limited* or *modified* the jury's understanding, not that they clarified, certified, or enhanced it. The sentence is from a legal context where `qualify` means "to make less general; to restrict or limit." Notice how `clarify` appears as a distractor—it is semantically adjacent but contextually incorrect. The judge's instructions in this type of sentence are typically cautionary and limiting, not explanatory.
[/example]

#### The Behaviour of "Flag"

`Flag` presents a beautiful example of contextual polysemy:
- *The national flag fluttered in the breeze.* — Noun: a piece of cloth
- *The officers flagged the suspicious vehicle.* — Verb: to signal or mark
- *His energy began to flag after the marathon.* — Verb: to decline, weaken
- *The editor flagged the error for correction.* — Verb: to mark for attention

The SSC examination tests `flag` in its verbal forms almost exclusively. The critical distinction is between `flag` meaning "to mark" (transitive) and `flag` meaning "to weaken" (intransitive). This grammatical difference is the key to solving questions.

[alert type="warning"]
**The Intransitive Trap**: When `flag` means "to decline in strength, enthusiasm, or effort," it is intransitive—it takes no direct object. *The conversation flagged.* *His interest flagged.* When `flag` means "to mark or signal," it is transitive. *He flagged the taxi.* *The inspector flagged the violation.* The examination will place a transitive context and offer `weaken` as an option; or place an intransitive context and offer `signal` as an option. Your grammatical awareness is your only defence.
[/alert]

#### The Behaviour of "Affect"

The distinction between `affect` (verb) and `effect` (usually noun, occasionally verb) is well-known, but the advanced synonym question targets a deeper distinction: `affect` as "to influence" versus `affect` as "to feign" or "to assume a mannerism."

- *The weather affected the crops.* — To influence, change.
- *She affected an air of indifference.* — To put on a false appearance, to feign.

The synonym for the first is `influence` or `alter`; the synonym for the second is `pretend`, `feign`, or `simulate`. The examination will provide a sentence where the word means "to feign" and offer `influence` as the tempting but incorrect option.

[flashcards title="Homographic Synonyms - Same Spelling, Different Meaning"]
| Flag (V, trans.) | To mark, signal, or draw attention to |
| Flag (V, intrans.) | To decline, weaken, or lose energy |
| Affect (V, trans.) | To influence or produce a change |
| Affect (V, trans., different meaning) | To feign or assume artificially |
| Apprehend (V) | To arrest OR to understand (the two meanings are tested in legal vs. philosophical contexts) |
| Temper (V) | To moderate or soften OR to harden metal (the scientific vs. figurative meaning) |
[/flashcards]

#### The Behaviour of "Secure"

`Secure` is a word that advanced candidates confidently answer incorrectly. Its primary meaning—"safe, protected"—is familiar. But in formal and administrative contexts, `secure` appears in two distinct registers:

- *The embassy was secure.* — Safe, protected (adjective)
- *He secured a loan from the bank.* — To obtain, acquire (verb)
- *The engineers secured the bridge against collapse.* — To fasten, make firm (verb)
- *The general secured the city against invasion.* — To protect, make safe (verb)

The examination is most likely to test the verb `secure` in the sense of "to obtain" or "to fasten." When a sentence reads, *"The diplomat secured a crucial agreement through back-channel negotiations,"* the correct synonym is `obtained`, not `protected`. The presence of `through back-channel negotiations` signals a process of *acquisition*, not safety.

### Section 17.4 | Synonyms in Reading Comprehension: The Art of Contextual Inference

The Tier-II Reading Comprehension section is not a test of vocabulary in isolation; it is a test of your ability to infer meaning from *surrounding textual clues*. The question format is standard: a paragraph is provided, a word is highlighted, and you must select the synonym that best matches its meaning *in that passage*.

The cognitive architecture of this task has four components, each of which must be executed with precision:

**Step 1: Locate the word in its immediate sentence.** Read the sentence in which the word appears, plus the sentence before and the sentence after. Meaning is not contained in a word; it emerges from the word's relationship to its neighbours.

**Step 2: Identify the syntactic role of the word.** Is it a noun, verb, adjective, or adverb? This immediately eliminates options from a different part of speech. The examination sometimes places a word that can function as multiple parts of speech and offers options from each category; the syntactic context eliminates the distractors.

**Step 3: Examine the collocational environment.** What words accompany the target word? A word like `rancorous` is accompanied by words like `debate`, `dispute`, `acrimony`, or `parting`. A word like `ebullient` is accompanied by words like `enthusiasm`, `spirits`, `welcome`, or `crowd`. The collocational field is the fingerprint of meaning.

**Step 4: Determine the register and tone of the passage.** Is the passage formal, journalistic, literary, scientific, or informal? Is the author's attitude approving, disapproving, neutral, or ironic? The register of the synonym must match the register of the passage. You cannot replace a formal word with a colloquial synonym, nor can you replace a literary word with a scientific one.

[example]
**Question:** Read the following passage and select the correct synonym for the highlighted word:

*"The new legislation, while ostensibly designed to protect consumer rights, has been widely criticised as a**nodyne** by legal experts who argue that its provisions are so vague as to be practically unenforceable. The absence of a robust enforcement mechanism renders the statute, in their assessment, little more than a symbolic gesture."*

Select the synonym for "nodyne" as used in the passage:

- A) Beneficial
- B) Inoffensive
- C) Ineffective
- D) Contradictory

**Solution:** C) Ineffective. The passage states that the legislation is criticised as `nodyne` and that its provisions are "vague," "practically unenforceable," and "little more than a symbolic gesture." The word `nodyne` literally means "not causing harm or offence," but in this context, it is being used *ironically* to mean "so bland and vague as to have no effect." The clue is in the phrase "widely criticised"—the legal experts are not praising the legislation for being harmless; they are condemning it for being ineffective. Option B is the literal meaning but misses the contextual irony and the collocational evidence of `unenforceable` and `symbolic gesture`.
[/example]

[alert type="info"]
**The Inference Principle**: In Reading Comprehension questions, the correct synonym is often *not* the dictionary definition but the *contextual implication*. The examination expects you to understand that words are not fixed in meaning; they are fixed in *tendency*. The context shifts the meaning from denotation to implication, from literal to rhetorical, from surface to depth.
[/alert]

### Section 17.5 | Antonym vs. Synonym Distinction in Context

The most deceptive questions in the vocabulary section are those that ask for an antonym but present options that are synonyms of the word in a *different sense*. The trap exploits your assumption that "antonym" is a simple inverse relationship. It is not.

Consider the word `clear`. Its antonyms depend entirely on sense:
- Clear (transparent): opaque, murky
- Clear (unambiguous): vague, obscure
- Clear (unobstructed): blocked, clogged
- Clear (innocent): guilty, culpable

When the examination asks for the antonym of `clear` in the sentence, *"The instructions were clear,"* the correct answer is `ambiguous`, not `opaque`. The context—instructions—determines the sense. `Opaque` is a synonym of `clear` only in the sense of "transparent"; it is an antonym in *that* sense but a distractor in this context.

The cognitive strategy for antonym questions is identical to that for synonym questions: **read the context first, retrieve the sense second, select the opposite of that sense third.**

[alert type="danger"]
**The False Cognate Trap**: Words like `sanction` (permission OR penalty), `cleave` (split OR adhere), `fast` (quick OR fixed), and `dust` (remove dust OR apply dust) are *contronyms*—words that can mean their own opposite. The examination will present such a word in a context that strongly signals one meaning and offer the opposite meaning as the synonym, or vice versa. Your awareness of contronyms is not optional; it is essential.
[/alert]

### Section 17.6 | Synonym Cloze: Fill-in-the-Blank with Precision

The cloze test at the advanced level is the purest expression of synonym discrimination. You are presented with a sentence missing one word, and you must select the word that best completes it. The options are not random; they are carefully selected synonyms that differ in connotation, intensity, grammatical requirement, or collocational fit.

The architecture of the cloze test mirrors the architecture of real language use. The brain predicts upcoming words based on syntactic and semantic constraints. The examination exploits this predictive mechanism by offering options that satisfy the syntactic constraints but not the semantic ones, or vice versa.

**Step 1: Identify the grammatical slot.** What part of speech is required? Is the verb transitive or intransitive? Does the noun require a particular preposition? Does the adjective precede a countable or uncountable noun? Grammar eliminates 40-60% of options immediately.

**Step 2: Identify the semantic field.** What domain of meaning is being invoked? Is it political, emotional, legal, scientific, or everyday? The correct word must belong to the same semantic domain as the surrounding words.

**Step 3: Assess connotation and register.** Is the tone positive, negative, or neutral? Is it formal, informal, or technical? The correct word must match the tone and register of the entire sentence.

**Step 4: Test each remaining option in the blank.** Read the sentence aloud (in your mind) with each option. Which one *sounds* right? The brain's phonological loop is a surprisingly accurate judge of collocational and structural fit.

[example]
**Question:** Select the word that best completes the following sentence:

*"Despite the committee's repeated attempts to reach a consensus, the two factions remained _______, refusing to concede even the smallest point of disagreement."*

- A) Obdurate
- B) Tenacious
- C) Intransigent
- D) Pertinacious

**Solution:** C) Intransigent. The context involves a committee, factions, and refusal to concede points of disagreement. `Intransigent` specifically means "refusing to compromise" in a political or principled context—exactly the situation described. `Obdurate` carries a moral or wrongdoer's stubbornness, which is not indicated. `Tenacious` is positive and suggests persistence, not refusal to compromise. `Pertinacious` suggests holding onto an opinion but lacks the political/negotiation context that `intransigent` provides.
[/example]

[alert type="success"]
**The Elimination Strategy**: In synonym cloze questions, if two options are extremely similar in meaning, the correct answer is likely the one that differs in *register* or *collocational requirement*. The examination sets multiple near-synonyms, knowing that the candidate who cannot discriminate will be trapped. Your strategy should be to identify the *distinctive feature* of each option—what makes it different from its peers—rather than to memorise their definitions.
[/alert]

[example]
**Question:** Select the word that best completes the following sentence:

*"The archaeologist's findings were initially met with _______, but subsequent discoveries vindicated his controversial hypothesis."*

- A) Censure
- B) Reprimand
- C) Ostracism
- D) Scepticism

**Solution:** D) Scepticism. The context indicates that the findings were initially met with *doubt* or *disbelief*, which was later overturned by subsequent discoveries. `Scepticism` fits both the semantic field (academic, intellectual) and the collocational environment (a hypothesis, vindication). `Censure` implies official disapproval, which is too strong and carries a moral or procedural tone not indicated. `Reprimand` is hierarchical and personal, entirely inappropriate. `Ostracism` is social exclusion, which is irrelevant to the academic context.
[/example]

[quiz title="Advanced Synonym Discrimination"]
Q: Select the synonym of "dissemble" as used in the sentence: "The witness was accused of dissembling during cross-examination."
- A) Fabricating (Correct: dissembling in this context is concealing truth through pretence, often by avoiding direct answers; fabricating is outright lying, which is a related but distinct concept)
- B) Prevaricating (Correct: both involve evading the truth, with prevaricate being a near-perfect match for a witness context)
- C) Equivocating (Also correct in some contexts, but the witness is specifically accused of *pretending* or *concealing*, not merely using ambiguous language)
- D) Obscuring (Incorrect: obscuring refers to making something unclear, not to personal pretence)
*Note: The examination often has only one correct answer; here, B is the most precise match for a witness under questioning.*

Q: Select the antonym of "intransigent" as used in the sentence: "The management's intransigent stance on wages led to a prolonged strike."
- A) Flexible (Correct: management refusing to budge is intransigent; its opposite is flexible or accommodating)
- B) Conciliatory (Also a strong candidate, as it suggests a willingness to appease and compromise)
- C) Obdurate (Incorrect: this is a synonym, not an antonym)
- D) Recalcitrant (Incorrect: this is also a synonym, referring to resistance to authority)

Q: Complete the sentence: "The bureaucrat's _______ explanations only served to confuse the citizens further."
- A) Obfuscatory (Correct: this means deliberately confusing, which fits the deliberate action of a bureaucrat)
- B) Obscure (Less precise: obscure is more about being unclear than about deliberate confusion)
- C) Prevaricative (Incorrect: prevarication is about evading the truth, not about making explanations confusing)
- D) Equivocal (Possible but weaker: equivocal suggests ambiguity, whereas obfuscatory suggests *deliberate* confusion, which is indicated by "only served to confuse")
[/quiz]

### Section 17.7 | The Grammar-Synonym Interface: Transitivity, Prepositions, and Countability

The most sophisticated vocabulary questions are not vocabulary questions at all—they are grammar questions disguised as vocabulary questions. A word's synonyms are constrained by its grammatical properties. This is a law of the English language that many advanced candidates fail to appreciate.

#### Transitivity and Its Consequences

`Rue` (to regret) is transitive: *I rue the day I met him.* You cannot say, "I rued." The object is obligatory. Therefore, a synonym in the blank must also be transitive. `Repent` is either transitive (*repent one's sins*) or intransitive (*repent of one's sins*)—but note the prepositional shift. `Regret` is transitive: *I regret my decision.* The examination will place a context requiring a transitive verb and offer `repent` (which, in its intransitive form, takes `of`) as a distractor.

=Transitivity is not optional. If the sentence has a direct object, the verb must be transitive. If it does not, the verb must be intransitive.=

#### Prepositional Requirements

`Admonish` takes `for` (*admonished for his negligence*) and sometimes `against` (*admonished against such behaviour*). `Censure` takes `for` (*censured for misconduct*). `Reprimand` takes `for` (*reprimanded for insubordination*). The examination will test your knowledge of these prepositional requirements by offering synonyms that take different prepositions.

[alert type="warning"]
**The Prepositional Trap**: If a sentence reads, *"The committee ______ the official for his dereliction of duty,"* the correct synonym must take `for`. `Castigate` works (*castigated for*), `reprimand` works (*reprimanded for*), but `excoriate` works only with `for` in some contexts and `over` in others. The option that takes a different preposition—such as `rebuke` which can take `for` but not always—is a distractor. The examination exploits this subtle variation.
[/alert]

#### Countability and Article Use

The distinction between `enmity` (uncountable, abstract) and `animosity` (countable or uncountable, but often countable in specific contexts) can determine the correct answer. A sentence like, *"There was deep ______ between the two communities,"* requires an uncountable noun. `Enmity` fits; `animosity` also fits but may require an article in some contexts. The examination will provide collocational evidence: `deep enmity` is a standard collocation; `deep animosity` is also standard, but `an animosity` would require a different context.

### Section 17.8 | The Examination Pattern: SSC Tier-II and Its Demands

The SSC Tier-II examination tests vocabulary at a level that demands a systematic approach. The pattern is consistent:

1. **20-25 vocabulary questions** in the English Language & Comprehension section
2. **6-8 questions** on direct synonym selection
3. **4-5 questions** on contextual synonym selection (word given in a sentence)
4. **3-4 questions** on antonym selection (often with contextual clues)
5. **5-7 questions** on cloze test (synonym discrimination within a passage)

The time allocation is critical: you have approximately 45 seconds per question in the English section. This means you cannot afford to ponder each option. You must have a decision-making heuristic.

#### The Heuristic for Synonym Questions

1. **Read the word or sentence once** (3 seconds).
2. **Identify the sense** (if the word is given in isolation, recall all meanings; if in context, identify the specific meaning) (5 seconds).
3. **Eliminate options** based on:
   - Part of speech mismatch (immediate elimination)
   - Register mismatch (formal vs. informal)
   - Intensity mismatch (too strong or too weak)
   - Collocational mismatch (wrong preposition, wrong object)
4. **Select from the remaining options** based on the closest match of denotation and connotation.

[flashcards title="Examination Power Words - SSC Tier-II Favourites"]
| Propitiate | To appease, to win favour with (takes a person or deity as object) |
| Importune | To ask persistently or urgently (takes a person as object; contrasts with `beseech` which takes a person but has a more emotional register) |
| Inveigh | To speak or write bitterly against (takes `against`; intransitive) |
| Gainsay | To deny or contradict (transitive; formal; often used in negative contexts like "no one can gainsay") |
| Eschew | To deliberately avoid (transitive; implies moral or practical choice) |
| Foment | To instigate or encourage (takes trouble, revolution, discord; implies intentional stirring) |
| Enjoin | To instruct or urge authoritatively (takes `to` or `from`: "enjoined him to desist" OR "enjoined from acting") |
| Abjure | To renounce formally (transitive; moral or legal context) |
| Adjure | To command or entreat solemnly (takes a person and infinitive: "adjured them to tell the truth") |

Q: Select the synonym of "inveigh" as used in the sentence: "The journalist inveighed against the government's policy on social media."
- A) Lauded (Incorrect: opposite meaning)
- B) Railed (Correct: "railed against" is the exact idiomatic match; both take "against" and mean to protest bitterly)
- C) Demurred (Incorrect: demur means to object hesitantly, not to attack bitterly)
- D) Assented (Incorrect: opposite meaning)

Q: Select the antonym of "propitiate" as used in the sentence: "The king sought to propitiate the angry gods with lavish sacrifices."
- A) Annoy (Incorrect: too mild; propitiate is about appeasing, annoying is a weak opposite)
- B) Antagonise (Correct: to propitiate is to win favour; to antagonise is to provoke hostility—the strongest and most precise opposite in this context)
- C) Ignore (Incorrect: ignoring is not the opposite of appeasing in a ritual context; it is merely irrelevant)
- D) Neglect (Incorrect: neglect implies omission, not deliberate opposition)

Q: Complete the sentence: "The legal expert ______ the evidence with such forensic precision that the defence's case collapsed entirely."
- A) Obscured (Incorrect: the opposite of what the sentence indicates)
- B) Dissembled (Incorrect: dissembling is about personal pretence, not about evidence)
- C) Dissected (Correct: to dissect is to analyse in detail, which fits the context of forensic precision and evidence analysis)
- D) Prevaricated (Incorrect: prevarication is about evading truth, not analysing evidence)

This chapter has provided the framework.
