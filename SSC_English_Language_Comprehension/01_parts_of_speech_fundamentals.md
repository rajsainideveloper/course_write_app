# Parts of Speech & Fundamentals
> The architecture of English sentences lies not in memorising labels, but in understanding the *functional jobs* each word performs. Master these roles, and you command the language.

## Chapter 1: The Eight Functional Categories – A Cognitive Map

### Section 1.1 | The Word’s Job Description

Every word in an English sentence performs a specific *function*. The part of speech is not an inherent property of the word itself—it is a *role* it plays in a given context. The same word can be a noun in one sentence, a verb in another, and an adjective in a third. This is the first cognitive hurdle most students stumble over.

- `The part of speech is determined by syntax and meaning within the sentence, not by the word's spelling.`
- Examine this: *"I **book** a ticket"* (verb) vs *"I read a **book**"* (noun). The word is identical; its function is not.

The eight functional categories are:

| Part of Speech | Core Function | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Noun** | Names a person, place, thing, or idea | *dog, London, bravery* |
| **Pronoun** | Replaces a noun to avoid repetition | *she, they, it* |
| **Adjective** | Modifies (describes) a noun or pronoun | *blue, quick, three* |
| **Verb** | Expresses an action, occurrence, or state of being | *run, is, think* |
| **Adverb** | Modifies a verb, adjective, or another adverb | *quickly, very, here* |
| **Preposition** | Shows the relationship between a noun/pronoun and another word | *in, on, at, by* |
| **Conjunction** | Connects words, phrases, or clauses | *and, but, because* |
| **Interjection** | Expresses strong emotion or sudden feeling | *Wow! Ouch!* |

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:** Examiners love to test your ability to identify the part of speech of a word *in context*. A word like "fast" can be an adjective (*a fast car*), an adverb (*he runs fast*), a noun (*to break a fast*), or a verb (*to fast for a day*). Never assume; always analyse the word's syntactic position.
[/alert]

### Section 1.2 | The Hierarchy of Identification

When you encounter a word, apply this cognitive filter in sequence:

1.  **Does it express an action or a state?** → Verb.
2.  **Does it name a thing, person, place, or concept?** → Noun.
3.  **Does it replace a noun?** → Pronoun.
4.  **Does it describe a noun?** → Adjective.
5.  **Does it describe a verb, adjective, or adverb?** → Adverb.
6.  **Does it show a relationship (time, place, direction) between two words?** → Preposition.
7.  **Does it connect words, phrases, or clauses?** → Conjunction.
8.  **Is it a standalone exclamation?** → Interjection.

This hierarchy is your mental shortcut. The first question that yields a "yes" is usually your answer, but beware of verbs that function as nouns (gerunds) and adjectives that function as nouns (nominal adjectives).

## Chapter 2: Nouns – The Naming Architecture

### Section 2.1 | Classification by Meaning and Form

A noun is the fundamental building block. Its classifications are not arbitrary; they reflect how we perceive reality. Understanding these distinctions is crucial for subject-verb agreement and article usage.

#### Proper vs Common
- **Common Noun:** A general, non-specific name for a person, place, or thing. (e.g., *city, boy, river*).
- **Proper Noun:** The specific name of a particular person, place, or thing, always capitalised. (e.g., *Mumbai, Ravi, Ganga*).
    - =A proper noun is a *unique identifier*; a common noun is a *category label*.
    - **The Trap:** Words like "father" are common. "Father" (when used as a name, e.g., "I asked Father") becomes a proper noun. Capitalisation is your clue.

#### Abstract vs Concrete
- **Concrete Noun:** Perceptible to the senses; you can see, touch, hear, smell, or taste it. (e.g., *table, music, perfume*).
- **Abstract Noun:** An idea, quality, or state; it exists only in the mind. (e.g., *honesty, courage, childhood, democracy*).
    - **Cognitive Link:** Abstract nouns are often formed from adjectives (*kind* → *kindness*), verbs (*know* → *knowledge*), or common nouns (*friend* → *friendship*). Recognising this suffix pattern ( -ness, -ment, -tion, -ship, -hood ) is a powerful vocabulary tool.

#### Collective Noun
- Names a group of people or things considered as a single unit. (e.g., *team, flock, jury, committee, mob*).
    - **The Subject-Verb Agreement Minefield:** A collective noun takes a **singular** verb when the group acts as one unit: *The jury **has** reached its verdict.* It takes a **plural** verb when members act individually: *The jury **are** divided in their opinions.*
    - **SSC Pattern:** Expect questions with collective nouns and relative pronouns. "The team *who* were wearing their new jerseys..." (Plural verb and plural pronoun because individuals are being referred to).

#### Material Noun
- Names the raw substance or matter from which things are made. (e.g., *gold, wood, water, cotton, iron*).
    - Material nouns are generally uncountable. We do not say "a water" or "two golds." They are used with quantifiers like *some, much, a piece of*.
    - They become countable when referring to specific types or servings: *We offer **two wines** from this region.*

[flashcards title="Noun Type Mnemonics & Key Rules"]
| Type | Core Rule / Test |
| :--- | :--- |
| Proper | Always capitalised; names a *specific* entity. |
| Common | Not capitalised; names a *general* entity. |
| Abstract | Cannot be perceived by the five senses; names *concepts*. |
| Collective | Refers to a group; verb singular or plural based on *unity* vs *individuality*. |
| Material | Names the substance; usually uncountable; uses no article. |

## Chapter 3: Pronouns – The Replacements with Rules

### Section 3.1 | The Seven Categories of Substitution

Pronouns are not a stylistic choice to avoid repetition; they are syntactically complex systems that maintain reference chains. Errors in pronoun usage are frequent and heavily tested.

#### Personal Pronouns
These shift form based on person (1st, 2nd, 3rd), number (singular/plural), gender, and case (Subjective, Objective, Possessive).

| Person | Subjective | Objective | Possessive |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1st Sg. | I | me | my/mine |
| 2nd Sg. | you | you | your/yours |
| 3rd Sg. (Masc.) | he | him | his |
| 3rd Sg. (Fem.) | she | her | her/hers |
| 3rd Sg. (Neut.) | it | it | its |
| 1st Pl. | we | us | our/ours |
| 3rd Pl. | they | them | their/theirs |

- **Cognitive Rule:** Use the **Subjective** case when the pronoun is the *subject* of a verb. Use the **Objective** case when the pronoun is the *object* of a verb or preposition.
- **Exam Trap (The Preposition Trap):** "Between you and *I*" is grammatically incorrect. The preposition "between" requires the objective case. The correct form is "Between you and *me*."
- **Exam Trap (Than/As):** After "than" and "as," the case depends on the implied verb. *"She is taller than I"* is grammatically correct because it is short for "She is taller than *I am*." *"She loves him more than me"* is ambiguous: does it mean "more than she loves me" or "more than I love him"? Context is key, but SSC usually tests the first rule.

#### Reflexive Pronouns
End in -self/-selves. Used when the subject and object of a verb are the *same* entity. (e.g., *He hurt **himself**.*).
- **Imperative Rule:** Do not use a reflexive pronoun where a personal pronoun is required. "He killed *himself*" (correct). "He killed *him*" (implies someone else).
- **Emphatic Usage:** Used for emphasis after a noun or pronoun. *I **myself** saw it.* They do not change the sentence's core meaning.

#### Demonstrative Pronouns
Point to specific things. (This, These, That, Those).
- This/These = near (in time or space). That/Those = distant.
- **Trap:** *This* and *that* must agree in number with their verb. *"These are my books"* (plural). *"This is my book"* (singular). They are often confused with demonstrative *adjectives*. A demonstrative *pronoun* stands alone. A demonstrative *adjective* modifies a noun: *This book* (adj) vs *This is a book* (pronoun).

#### Indefinite Pronouns
Refer to non-specific persons or things. (e.g., *anybody, someone, everybody, nobody, few, many, all, none*).

[alert type="danger"]
**Singular vs Plural Error (High Frequency SSC Topic):** Indefinite pronouns are the single biggest source of subject-verb agreement errors.
- **Always Singular:** *anyone, everyone, someone, no one, each, either, neither, anybody, nobody, somebody, one, nothing, everything.*
    - =*Everyone **is** responsible for **his or her** own work.* (Not "their," though this is changing in modern usage, the traditional exam rule holds).
- **Always Plural:** *few, many, several.*
    - =*Few **have** the courage to challenge him.*
- **Can be Singular or Plural:** *all, any, none, some, more, most.* These depend on the noun they refer to.
    - *Some of the water **is** gone.* (Singular)
    - *Some of the players **are** tired.* (Plural)
[/alert]

#### Relative Pronouns
Connect a relative clause to a noun/pronoun (the antecedent). (e.g., *who, whom, which, that, whose*).
- **Who/Whom:** *Who* is subjective (for the subject of the clause). *Whom* is objective (for the object of the clause).
    - *The man **who** stole my car* (He stole it – subject).
    - *The man **whom** I saw* (I saw him – object).
- **Which vs That:** *Which* introduces non-restrictive (non-essential) clauses, set off by commas. *That* introduces restrictive (essential) clauses, without commas.
    - *The book, **which** is on the table, is mine.* (The clause adds extra info; we know which book).
    - *The book **that** is on the table is mine.* (The clause identifies which book; it's essential).
- **Whose:** Used for people and things to show possession. (e.g., *The car **whose** engine is broken.*)

#### Interrogative Pronouns
Used to ask questions. (e.g., *who, whom, which, what, whose*).
- The difference from relative pronouns is purely functional. "Who" asks a question; in a relative clause, it connects.

#### Distributive Pronouns
Refer to members of a group individually. (e.g., *each, either, neither*).
- **Singular:** =*Each of the boys **has** a pen.*
- **Trap:** "Neither" and "either" are always singular. =*Neither of the two options **is** acceptable.*
- "Either...or" / "Neither...nor" – the verb agrees with the noun closest to it.
    - *Neither the teacher nor the students **are** coming.*
    - *Neither the students nor the teacher **is** coming.*

[flashcards title="Pronoun Case & Agreement Rules"]
| Rule | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| Object of a preposition | *Between you and **me*** (not "I"). |
| After "than"/"as" with implied verb | *He is taller than **I** (am).* |
| Reflexive vs Personal | *He hurt **himself**.* vs *He hurt **him**.* |
| Indefinite (Always Singular) | *Everyone **is** here.* |
| Indefinite (Plural) | *Few **have** arrived.* |
| Either/Neither (Singular) | *Neither **is** correct.* |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 4: Adjectives – The Modifiers of Nouns

### Section 4.1 | The Descriptive and Determinate Categories

Adjectives are not mere decorations; they provide essential information that helps identify, quantify, or describe a noun. They modify only nouns and pronouns.

#### Descriptive Adjective
Express a quality or characteristic. (e.g., *beautiful, intelligent, red, tall, soft*).
- These are the most common and can be used in a sentence before a noun or after a linking verb.
    - *She is a **brilliant** student.* (Attributive – before noun).
    - *The student is **brilliant**.* (Predicative – after linking verb).

#### Quantitative Adjective
Indicate the quantity of a noun. (e.g., *some, few, many, much, enough, three, ten*).
- **Distinction:** Quantitative adjectives answer "How much?" or "How many?"
- **Countable vs Uncountable:** Use *many* (countable) vs *much* (uncountable). *Few* (negative implication, "not many") vs *a few* (positive implication, "some"). *Little* (negative, "not much") vs *a little* (positive, "some").
    - *I have **little** money.* (Almost none).
    - *I have **a little** money.* (Some, enough).
    - *There are **few** visitors.* (Not many).
    - *There are **a few** visitors.* (Some).

#### Demonstrative Adjective
Point to specific nouns. (This, These, That, Those).
- Always followed by a noun. *This book* (not "This is a book," where it is a demonstrative pronoun).

#### Possessive Adjective
Show ownership or possession. (My, Your, His, Her, Its, Our, Their).
- **Cognitive Shift:** These are adjectives, not pronouns. They modify a noun. *That is **my** book.* "My" tells us *whose* book.
- **The Trap:** Do not confuse "its" (possessive adjective) with "it's" (contraction of "it is"). =*The cat licked **its** paw.* =*It's raining.*

#### Interrogative Adjective
Used to ask questions about a noun. (Which, What, Whose).
- *Which book do you want?* (Modifies "book").
- *Whose car is this?* (Modifies "car").

#### Distributive Adjective
Refer to individual members of a group. (Each, Every, Either, Neither).
- They always modify a singular noun and take a singular verb.
    - =*Each student **has** a copy.*
    - =*Every man **is** responsible.*

[alert type="info"]
**The Order of Adjectives (The Royal Order):** When multiple adjectives are used, they must follow a specific order: **Opinion → Size → Age → Shape → Colour → Origin → Material → Purpose → Noun**.
- *A beautiful (opinion) large (size) old (age) round (shape) red (colour) wooden (material) table.*
- An SSC exam will not ask you to list the order, but you must be able to identify which sentence *sounds* correct. The correct order is instinctively known to native speakers; for non-natives, practice is key. The most important rule is that **opinion adjectives (e.g., lovely, ugly) always come before factual adjectives (e.g., tall, square).**
[/alert]

## Chapter 5: Verbs – The Engine of the Sentence

### Section 5.1 | Action vs. State: The Two Core Types

Verbs are the core of the predicate, indicating what the subject is doing or being.

#### Action Verbs
Express a physical or mental action. (e.g., *run, write, think, eat, argue*).
- They can be transitive or intransitive.

#### Linking Verbs
Do not express an action; they connect the subject to a subject complement (a noun or adjective that renames or describes the subject).
- Core linking verbs: **be** (is, am, are, was, were, been, being) and verbs of the senses (look, seem, appear, feel, sound, taste, smell).
- =*She **is** a doctor.* (Links "she" to "doctor").
- =*The soup **tastes** delicious.* (Links "soup" to "delicious").
- **The Trap:** "Feel," "smell," "taste," "look," and "appear" can be action verbs in other contexts. *I felt the fabric* (action). *The fabric felt soft* (linking). The distinction depends on whether the subject is performing an action or experiencing a state.

### Section 5.2 | Transitive vs. Intransitive: The Object Question

This is a functional distinction, not a fixed property. It defines a verb's relationship with its object.

#### Transitive Verb
Requires a direct object to complete its meaning. The action transfers from the subject to the object.
- *She **threw** the ball.* (What did she throw? "The ball" – direct object).
- *He **ate** an apple.* (What did he eat? "An apple").

#### Intransitive Verb
Does **not** require an object to complete its meaning. The action stops with the subject.
- *She **laughed**.* (No object needed).
- *He **ran** quickly.* (No object).
- **The Trap:** Many verbs can be both transitive and intransitive. *"The door **opened**"* (intransitive). *"I **opened** the door"* (transitive).

[alert type="warning"]
**The Linking Verb vs Intransitive Action Verb Confusion:** Students often confuse a linking verb + adjective (subject complement) with a transitive verb + adverb (adverbial modifier).
- *He looked **angry**.* ("Angry" is an adjective describing "he" – linking verb).
- *He looked **angrily** at me.* ("Angrily" is an adverb modifying the verb "looked" – action verb).
This distinction is crucial for adjective vs adverb questions on the exam.
[/alert]

[flashcards title="Verb Types - Quick Reference"]
| Verb Type | Definition | Test |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Action (Transitive) | Takes a direct object. | *She **kicked** the ball.* |
| Action (Intransitive) | Does not take a direct object. | *She **slept**.* |
| Linking | Links subject to a complement (noun/adjective). | *She **is** a doctor.* |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 6: Adverbs – The Modifiers of Everything Else

### Section 6.1 | The Six Functional Categories

Adverbs are the most flexible modifiers; they can modify a verb, an adjective, or another adverb. They are the key to providing context—*how, where, when, to what extent*.

#### Adverb of Manner
Tell *how* an action is performed. Usually end in -ly. (e.g., *slowly, carefully, well, fast*).
- *He drives **slowly**.* (Modifies "drives").

#### Adverb of Place
Tell *where* an action occurs. (e.g., *here, there, everywhere, outside, inside*).
- *She placed the book **here**.* (Modifies "placed").

#### Adverb of Time
Tell *when* an action occurs. (e.g., *now, then, today, yesterday, soon, later*).
- *We will leave **soon**.* (Modifies "will leave").

#### Adverb of Frequency
Tell *how often* an action occurs. (e.g., *always, never, often, seldom, rarely, usually, twice*).
- *I **always** wake up early.* (Modifies "wake up").

#### Adverb of Degree
Tell *to what extent* or *how much* an action/quality occurs. (e.g., *very, quite, rather, almost, too, enough, extremely*).
- *The soup is **too** hot.* (Modifies the adjective "hot").
- *She sings **quite** well.* (Modifies the adverb "well").

#### Interrogative Adverb
Used to ask questions about manner, place, time, or reason. (e.g., *how, where, when, why*).
- *Where are you going?*

#### Relative Adverb
Introduces a relative clause and modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb in that clause. (e.g., *where, when, why*).
- *I know the place **where** she lives.*

[alert type="info"]
**The Adverb Trap:** Avoid the mistake of using an adjective where an adverb is required, and vice versa.
- **Adjective:** *She is a **good** singer.* (Modifies "singer" – a noun).
- **Adverb:** *She sings **well**.* (Modifies "sings" – a verb).
- **With Linking Verbs:** Use adjectives, not adverbs, after linking verbs.
    - *She feels **bad**.* (Not "badly").
    - *It smells **good**.* (Not "well").
    However, "I feel *badly*" is also possible but means you are physically impaired in the sense of touch. This nuance is a top-tier SSC trap.
[/alert]

## Chapter 7: Prepositions – The Relational Connectors

### Section 7.1 | Building Relationships with Nouns

A preposition shows the relationship between a noun/pronoun (its object) and some other word in the sentence. They are the "glue" of English, indicating time, place, direction, cause, and manner.

#### Simple Prepositions
One-word prepositions. (e.g., *in, on, at, by, with, of, for, from, to, up, down*).

#### Compound Prepositions
Formed by prefixing a word to a simple preposition, or two-word combinations. (e.g., *into, upon, within, without, throughout, behind, across*).

#### Phrasal Prepositions
Multi-word units that function as a single preposition. (e.g., *because of, in spite of, in front of, due to, in addition to, with regard to*).
- **The Cognitive Rule:** The object of a preposition is always a noun or pronoun in the objective case.
- **The Trap:** A phrase ending in "of" is almost always a preposition. *In spite of* is a phrasal preposition; it takes an object.

[alert type="danger"]
**Prepositional Idioms (Fixed Collocations):** This is one of the most heavily tested areas. You cannot "reason" these; you must memorise them.
- *Accuse **of*** (not "for").
- *Agree **to*** (a proposal) vs *agree **with*** (a person).
- *Differ **from*** (to be unlike) vs *differ **with*** (to disagree).
- *Insist **on***.
- *Prevent **from***.
- *Congratulate **on***.
- *Consist **of*** (not "comprise of"; "comprise" means "consist of" and needs no "of").
- *Angry **with*** (a person) vs *angry **at*** (a thing or situation).
[/alert]

[flashcards title="Essential Preposition Collocations for SSC"]
| Phrase | Correct Preposition |
| :--- | :--- |
| Accord / Accordance | *in accordance with* |
| Comply | *comply with* |
| Adhere | *adhere to* |
| Different | *different from* (US: *than*; UK: *to*, but *from* is safest) |
| Prefer | *prefer to* (not "than") |
| Superior / Inferior | *superior to*, *inferior to* |
| Beside (by the side of) / Besides (in addition to) | *Besides* means "in addition to"; *Beside* means "next to". |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 8: Conjunctions – The Sentence Joiners

### Section 8.1 | The Logic of Connection

Conjunctions connect words, phrases, or clauses. Their correct use is essential for complex sentence structures. They indicate logical relationships like addition, contrast, cause-effect, and condition.

#### Coordinating Conjunctions
Connect two equal (grammatically parallel) elements: two words, two phrases, or two independent clauses.
- **FANBOYS:** For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So.
- =*I wanted to go, **but** I was tired.* (Connects two independent clauses).

#### Subordinating Conjunctions
Connect an independent clause (main clause) with a dependent clause (subordinate clause). The dependent clause cannot stand alone.
- Examples: *although, because, since, when, while, after, before, if, unless, whereas, despite*.
- =***Because** she was tired, she left early.* ("Because she was tired" cannot stand alone).

#### Correlative Conjunctions
Pairs of conjunctions that work together to connect parallel elements.
- **Pairs:** *either...or, neither...nor, both...and, not only...but also, whether...or*.
- **The Trap:** *Not only...but also* requires parallelism. =*She is **not only** intelligent **but also** hardworking.* (Both adjectives).
- **Subject-Verb Agreement with Correlatives:** With *either...or* and *neither...nor*, the verb agrees with the subject closest to it. (As discussed in the Pronoun section).

[alert type="warning"]
**The Subordinating Conjunction "Since" vs "Because":** The word "since" can mean "because" (reason) or "from that time" (time). While both are acceptable, the exam often prefers "because" to clearly indicate a reason, to avoid the ambiguity of time. "Since you are here, let's start" is fine, but "Because you are here" is unambiguous.
[/alert]

## Chapter 9: Interjections – The Emotional Outbursts

### Section 9.1 | Function, Not Grammar

Interjections are words or phrases that express sudden emotion or strong feeling. They are grammatically independent from the rest of the sentence.

- **Common Interjections:** *Wow! Ouch! Oh! Alas! Hurrah! Bravo! Hello!*
- They are often followed by an exclamation point.
- **The Trap:** Interjections are not tested heavily for grammar, but they appear in punctuation and comprehension questions to identify tone. *"Alas!"* expresses sorrow or regret, while *"Hurrah!"* expresses joy.

## Chapter 10: The Unified Cognitive Map – Solving Exam Questions

The entire English language exam hinges on your ability to identify the **job** of each word. Each section of the exam—error detection, fill-in-the-blanks, sentence improvement—tests your ability to apply these functional categories.

### Section 10.1 | Error Detection: The "Parts of Speech" Test

The most common errors are:
1.  **Adjective/Adverb Confusion:** Using an adjective when an adverb is needed or vice versa.
    - *Incorrect:* She spoke **beautiful**.
    - *Correct:* She spoke **beautifully**.
2.  **Pronoun Case Errors:** Objective vs Subjective.
    - *Incorrect:* Between you and **I**.
    - *Correct:* Between you and **me**.
3.  **Subject-Verb Agreement:** Especially with collective nouns and indefinite pronouns.
    - *Incorrect:* Everyone **are** happy.
    - *Correct:* Everyone **is** happy.
4.  **Prepositional Idioms:**
    - *Incorrect:* He is angry **against** me.
    - *Correct:* He is angry **with** me.
5.  **Parallelism:** With correlative conjunctions.
    - *Incorrect:* She is not only **beautiful** but also **has a good heart**.
    - *Correct:* She is not only **beautiful** but also **kind**.

[example]
**Question:** Identify the error in the sentence: "The reason he gave for his absence was because he was unwell."
**Solution:** The error is "because." The sentence is a classic redundant construction. "The reason... was because" is incorrect. The correct phrasing is "The reason... was that." The conjunction "because" introduces a dependent clause of reason, but the noun "reason" already implies the cause. The correct structure is "The reason... was that." (Noun clause).
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** Select the correct option: "Each of the boys, as well as their teacher, ____ going to the museum."
- A) is (Correct)
- B) are
- C) were
- D) has
**Solution:** The subject is "Each" (singular), not "boys" or "teacher." Phrases like "as well as," "along with," "together with," "in addition to," "accompanied by" are parenthetical. They do not change the number of the subject. The verb must agree with the main subject. Hence, "is" is correct.
[/example]
