# History: Post-Gupta & Early Medieval Kingdoms

> The fragmentation of the Gupta empire gave rise to regional powers—from Harsha's short-lived reunification to the Pallava-Chalukya rivalry that defined South Indian politics for centuries.

## Chapter 1: Harshavardhana—The Last Great Hindu Emperor

### Section 1 | The Rise of the Pushyabhuti Dynasty

The Gupta empire had *fragmented* by the mid-6th century. In its wake, several *regional* powers emerged—the *Pushyabhutis* of Thaneshwar (modern Haryana) were one such dynasty. They rose to *prominence* under *Prabhakaravardhana*, the father of Harsha.

**The Political Context:**

- The *Hunas* had been *defeated* but the *north* was *divided*.
- The *Maukharis* ruled *Kanauj* (a *strategic* city on the Ganga).
- The *Gaudas* ruled *Bengal*.
- The *Pushyabhutis* ruled *Thaneshwar*.

**Prabhakaravardhana (≈580–605 CE):**

- He was the *first* major Pushyabhuti king.
- He *expanded* the kingdom to include *Punjab* and *Haryana*.
- He *died* in =605 CE=—his *death* triggered a *succession* crisis.

**The Succession Crisis:**

- Prabhakaravardhana had *two* sons: *Rajyavardhana* and *Harshavardhana*.
- After his death, *Rajyavardhana* became king.
- He was *assassinated* by *Sasanka* (the Gauda king)—a *treacherous* act that *galvanized* Harsha into action.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The *Pushyabhuti* dynasty is *not* the same as the *Gupta* dynasty. The Guptas *declined* in the 6th century; the Pushyabhutis *rose* in the 7th century. The SSC often tests this *chronological* distinction—do not confuse Harsha's dynasty with the Guptas.
[/alert]

---

### Section 2 | Harshavardhana (≈606–647 CE)—The Reunifier

Harsha *ascended* the throne in =606 CE=—he was only *16* years old. His *reign* marks the *last* attempt to *reunify* northern India after the Guptas.

**The Expansion:**

- Harsha *defeated* Sasanka (the Gauda king) and *avenged* his brother's death.
- He *conquered* Kanauj—and *made* it his *capital* (he was *invited* by the Maukharis to rule after their *dynasty* ended).
- He *expanded* his empire from *Kashmir* in the north to *Narmada* in the south, and from *Bengal* in the east to *Sindh* in the west.

**The Conflict with the Chalukyas:**

- Harsha *marched* south to *conquer* the Deccan—but he was *defeated* by *Pulakeshin II* (the Chalukya king) at the *Narmada* river.
- The *Aihole Inscription* of Pulakeshin II *records* this victory—it states that Harsha *lost* his *harshas* (joy) after the defeat.

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
The *Aihole Inscription* is the *primary source* for Harsha's *defeat*—it was composed by the Chalukya court poet *Ravikirti*. The *inscription* is written in *Sanskrit* and *Kannada*—it is a *bilingual* record.
[/alert]

**The Reign of Harsha:**

- Harsha ruled for =41 years= (606–647 CE).
- He was a *patron* of *Buddhism*—but he was *tolerant* of *Hinduism* and *Jainism*.
- He *convened* a *great assembly* at *Prayag* (Allahabad) every *five* years—he *donated* his *entire* treasury to the *poor* at these assemblies.

---

### Section 3 | Hiuen Tsang (Xuanzang)—The Chinese Pilgrim

The *most* important source for Harsha's reign is the *account* of the Chinese Buddhist monk *Hiuen Tsang* (Xuanzang).

**The Journey:**

- Hiuen Tsang *traveled* through Central Asia to India—he *arrived* in =630 CE=.
- He *spent* several years at *Nalanda* University—he *studied* Mahayana Buddhism under the *venerable* Silabhadra.
- He *visited* the court of Harsha at *Kanauj*—he *describes* the *grandeur* of the king and the *prosperity* of his empire.

**Hiuen Tsang's Account:**

Hiuen Tsang *describes*:
- **The People**: The *people* were *honest* and *peaceful*—taxes were *light* and *criminals* were *few*.
- **The Administration**: Harsha *employed* a *bureaucracy*—the *officials* were *paid* from the *state* treasury.
- **The Economy**: Trade was *flourishing*—there were *roads*, *rest houses*, and *hospitals*.
- **The Religions**: Buddhism was *flourishing*—but *Brahmins* and *Jains* also *existed*.
- **Nalanda University**: It had *8,000* students and *100* professors—it was *supported* by Harsha and *other* kings.

[alert type="info"]
**Examiner's Focus:**
Hiuen Tsang's account is *not* entirely *objective*—he was a *Buddhist* monk, so he *praises* Buddhism and *criticizes* Hinduism. However, his account is *invaluable* for understanding the *political* and *social* conditions of 7th-century India.
[/alert]

**The Return to China:**

- Hiuen Tsang *returned* to China in =645 CE=—he *carried* over *600* Buddhist texts.
- He *wrote* the *Ta-T'ang-Hsi-Yu-Ki* (The Record of the Western Regions)—a *detailed* account of his journey.

**The Prayag Assembly:**

- Hiuen Tsang *describes* the *great assembly* at Prayag—Harsha *donated* *hundreds* of *thousands* of *gold*, *silver*, and *gems* to the *poor*.
- The assembly lasted *three* months—it *included* *Buddhists*, *Brahmins*, and *Jains*.

| Capital | Thaneshwar (early), Kanauj (later) |
| Defeated by | Pulakeshin II (Chalukya) at Narmada |

### Section 4 | Harsha's Administration and Social Policies

**The Revenue System:**

- Harsha *collected* taxes from *agriculture*, *trade*, and *forests*.
- The tax rate was *one-sixth* of the produce—the *same* as in the Mauryan period.
- The *officials* were *paid* from the *state* treasury—they were *not* granted *land* (which *weakened* the later Guptas).

**Dharamshalas and Hospitals:**

- Harsha *established* *Dharamshalas* (rest houses) for travelers.
- He *established* *hospitals* for the *poor*—free *medicines* and *treatment* were provided.

**Charity:**

- Harsha was *generous* to the *poor*—his *Prayag* assemblies *distributed* *wealth* to the *needy*.
- Hiuen Tsang *describes* the *king* as *liberal* and *compassionate*.

[flashcards title="Harsha's Administration"]
| Policy | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| Revenue | One-sixth of produce |
| Dharamshalas | Rest houses for travelers |
| Hospitals | Free treatment for the poor |
| Charity | Massive donations at Prayag assemblies |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 2: The Pallavas—The Masters of South Indian Art

### Section 1 | The Rise of the Pallavas

The Pallavas were a *major* power in *South India*—they ruled from the *4th to the 9th century* CE. Their *capital* was *Kanchipuram* (modern Tamil Nadu).

**The Origins:**

- The Pallavas *originated* as *vassals* of the *Satavahanas*.
- They *rose* to *independence* in the *4th century* CE.
- Their *early* rulers *built* the *rock-cut* temples of *Mahabalipuram*.

---

### Section 2 | The Great Pallava Rulers

**Mahendravarman I (≈600–630 CE):**

- He was a *patron* of *art* and *architecture*—he *built* the *rock-cut* temples at *Mahabalipuram*.
- He was a *poet*—he *composed* the *Mattavilasa Prahasana* (a *Sanskrit* satire on *Buddhists* and *Jains*).
- He *converted* from *Jainism* to *Shaivism*—under the *influence* of *Appar* (the Tamil saint).

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
Mahendravarman I is *often* confused with *Narasimhavarman I*—the *former* was a *patron* of *architecture*; the *latter* was a *warrior*. The exam tests the *distinction*—Mahendravarman was the *builder*; Narasimhavarman was the *conqueror*.
[/alert]

**Narasimhavarman I (≈630–668 CE):**

- He was the *greatest* Pallava king—he *defeated* the *Chalukya* king *Pulakeshin II*.
- He *captured* the Chalukya capital *Vatapi*—and *assumed* the title *Vatapikonda* (Conqueror of Vatapi).
- He *built* the *Shore Temple* at *Mahabalipuram*—a *UNESCO* World Heritage site.

---

### Section 3 | Pallava Art and Architecture

The Pallavas are *renowned* for their *art*—they *created* the *Dravidian* style of architecture.

**The Five Rathas (Mahabalipuram):**

- The *Five Rathas* are *rock-cut* monolithic temples—they *resemble* *chariots* (rathas).
- They are *named* after the *five* Pandavas (though the *association* is *later*—they were *not* built by the Pandavas).
- They *represent* the *transition* from *rock-cut* to *structural* architecture.

**The Shore Temple (Mahabalipuram):**

- The *Shore Temple* is a *structural* temple—it is *built* of *granite*.
- It *stands* on the *shore* of the *Bay of Bengal*—it is *dedicated* to *Shiva*.
- It is *one* of the *earliest* structural temples in South India.

**The Kailashanatha Temple (Kanchipuram):**

- The *Kailashanatha Temple* is *built* by *Rajasimha* (≈700–728 CE)—a *later* Pallava king.
- It is *dedicated* to *Shiva*—the *sanctum* is *surrounded* by *smaller* shrines.
- It *represents* the *peak* of Pallava *architecture*.

[flashcards title="Pallava Art & Architecture"]
| Monument | Location | Significance |
|----------|----------|--------------|
| Five Rathas | Mahabalipuram | Rock-cut monolithic temples |
| Shore Temple | Mahabalipuram | Structural temple (granite) |
| Kailashanatha Temple | Kanchipuram | Peak of Pallava architecture |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 3: The Chalukyas of Badami—The Deccan Power

### Section 1 | The Rise of the Chalukyas

The Chalukyas of *Badami* (≈6th–8th century CE) were the *dominant* power in the *Deccan*—they *challenged* the *Pallavas* in the south and *Harsha* in the north.

**The Founder: Pulakeshin I (≈540–566 CE):**

- He *established* the Chalukya kingdom at *Badami* (modern Karnataka).
- He *performed* the *Ashvamedha* sacrifice to *legitimize* his rule.

**Pulakeshin II (≈610–642 CE):**

- He was the *greatest* Chalukya king.
- He *defeated* Harsha on the *Narmada* river—this is *recorded* in the *Aihole Inscription*.
- He *conquered* the *Kalinga* region (modern Odisha).
- He was *defeated* and *killed* by the Pallava king *Narasimhavarman I* in ≈642 CE.

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
The *Aihole Inscription* is *written* in *Sanskrit* by the Chalukya court poet *Ravikirti*. It *describes* Pulakeshin II's *victories*—including his *defeat* of Harsha.
[/alert]

---

### Section 2 | Chalukya Architecture—The Vesara Style

The Chalukyas *developed* the *Vesara* style of architecture—a *fusion* of the *Dravidian* (south) and *Nagara* (north) styles.

**The Badami Caves:**

- The *Badami Caves* are *rock-cut* temples—they *house* *Shiva*, *Vishnu*, and *Jain* *images*.
- They are *excavated* from *sandstone* cliffs.
- They *exhibit* the *early* Chalukya style—*simple* and *elegant*.

**The Aihole Temples:**

- Aihole is *called* the "cradle of Indian temple architecture."
- Over *70* temples *exist* at Aihole—they *represent* the *early* experiments in temple building.
- The *Lad Khan* temple is the *most* famous—it has a *flat* roof and *dormer* windows.

[flashcards title="Chalukyas of Badami"]
| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Founder | Pulakeshin I (≈540 CE) |
| Greatest king | Pulakeshin II (610–642 CE) |
| Defeated | Harsha (Narmada) |
| Defeated by | Narasimhavarman I (Pallava) |
| Architecture | Vesara style (fusion of Nagara and Dravidian) |
| Inscription | Aihole (composed by Ravikirti) |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 4: The Rashtrakutas—The Imperial Deccan Power

### Section 1 | The Rise of the Rashtrakutas

The Rashtrakutas *overthrew* the Chalukyas of Badami in ≈753 CE—they *established* the *most powerful* dynasty in the Deccan (≈8th–10th century CE).

**The Founder: Dantidurga (≈735–756 CE):**

- Dantidurga *defeated* the *Chalukya* king *Kirtivarman II*.
- He *performed* the *Hiranyagarbha* ritual—a *ritual* that *re-born* him as a *Kshatriya* (he was *originally* a *Vaishya*).

**Krishna I (≈756–774 CE):**

- Krishna I was the *brother* of Dantidurga.
- He *built* the *Kailasa Temple* at *Ellora*—the *greatest* rock-cut temple in the world.
- The *Kailasa Temple* is *dedicated* to *Shiva*—it is *carved* from a *single* rock.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The *Kailasa Temple* is *not* at *Mahabalipuram*—it is at *Ellora* (Maharashtra). The *Shore Temple* is at *Mahabalipuram* (Tamil Nadu). The exam often tests this *geographic* distinction—know the *location* of each monument.
[/alert]

**Amoghavarsha I (≈814–878 CE):**

- Amoghavarsha was the *greatest* Rashtrakuta king—he *reigned* for *64 years* (the *longest* reign in Indian history).
- He was a *patron* of *Jainism*—he *wrote* the *Kavirajamarga* (the *earliest* Kannada text).
- He *built* the *Jain* temples at *Ellora* and *Shravanabelagola*.

---

### Section 2 | The Kailasa Temple (Ellora)

The *Kailasa Temple* is the *masterpiece* of Rashtrakuta architecture—it is *carved* from a *single* rock *mass*.

**The Dimensions:**

- Length: ≈50 m
- Width: ≈32 m
- Height: ≈30 m

**The Features:**

- The temple has *three* *storeys*—each *storey* has *sculptural* panels.
- The *walls* are *carved* with *scenes* from the *Ramayana*, the *Mahabharata*, and the *Shiva Purana*.
- The *sanctum* houses a *Shiva* linga.

[alert type="info"]
**Examiner's Focus:**
The *Kailasa Temple* is *not* just a temple—it is a *testament* to the *engineering* skill of the Rashtrakutas. The *rock* was *cut* from *top* to *bottom*—the *entire* temple was *excavated* from the *hill*. This *technique* is *unique*—no other temple in the world is *carved* from a *single* rock.
[/alert]

[flashcards title="Rashtrakuta Essentials"]
| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Founder | Dantidurga (≈735 CE) |
| Greatest king | Amoghavarsha I (814–878 CE) |
| Kailasa Temple | Ellora (built by Krishna I) |
| Amoghavarsha | Kavirajamarga (Kannada); patron of Jainism |
| Overthrown by | Chalukyas of Kalyani (≈10th century) |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 5: The Gurjara-Pratiharas—The Defenders of the North

### Section 1 | The Rise of the Pratiharas

The Gurjara-Pratiharas *emerged* in the *8th century*—they *ruled* *western* and *northern* India.

**The Founder: Nagabhatta I (≈730–760 CE):**

- Nagabhatta I *repelled* the *Arab* invasion of *Sindh*—he *defended* the *western* frontier.
- He *established* the *Pratihara* kingdom at *Mandore* (modern Rajasthan).

**Mihir Bhoja (≈836–885 CE):**

- Bhoja was the *greatest* Pratihara king—he *ruled* from *Kanauj* (the *strategic* city).
- He was *devoted* to *Vishnu*—he *adopted* the title *Adivaraha* (the *boar* incarnation).
- He *patronized* *Sanskrit* literature—the *Bhojaprabandha* and *Bhojasambita* are *attributed* to his *patronage*.

**The Conflict with the Palas and Rashtrakutas:**

- The Pratiharas were *locked* in the *tripartite* struggle with the *Palas* (Bengal) and the *Rashtrakutas* (Deccan).
- The *struggle* was for *Kanauj*—the *control* of the *Ganga* valley.
- The *Rashtrakuta* king *Govinda III* (≈793–814 CE) *defeated* the Pratiharas and *occupied* Kanauj.
- The *Pala* king *Dharmapala* (≈770–810 CE) also *claimed* Kanauj.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The *tripartite struggle* is a *frequent* exam topic. The *three* powers were: *Pratiharas* (north/west), *Palas* (east/Bengal), and *Rashtrakutas* (Deccan). The *prize* was *Kanauj*. The *struggle* *weakened* all three—and *facilitated* the *rise* of the *Turks*.
[/alert]

---

### Section 2 | The Architecture of the Pratiharas

The Pratiharas *built* the *Khadiravati* temple at *Bhilsa* (Madhya Pradesh)—but the *most* famous Pratihara *monument* is the *Somnath* temple in *Gujarat* (though it was *built* by the *Solanki* kings later).

| Founder | Nagabhatta I (≈730 CE) |
| Greatest king | Mihir Bhoja (836–885 CE) |
| Capital | Kanauj |
| Title | Adivaraha |
| Tripartite struggle | Pratihara vs. Pala vs. Rashtrakuta |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 6: The Palas—The Patrons of Buddhism

### Section 1 | The Rise of the Palas

The Palas *ruled* *Bengal* and *Bihar* from the *8th to the 12th century* CE—they were the *last* major Buddhist dynasty in India.

**The Founder: Gopala (≈750–770 CE):**

- Gopala was *elected* by the *Nobles* of Bengal—he was the *first* Buddhist king of the Pala dynasty.
- He *established* the *Odantapuri* monastery in *Bihar*—a *predecessor* to Nalanda.

**Dharmapala (≈770–810 CE):**

- Dharmapala was the *second* Pala king—he *expanded* the empire to *Bengal*, *Bihar*, and *parts* of *Uttar Pradesh*.
- He *established* the *Vikramshila* monastery—a *rival* to Nalanda.
- He *assumed* the title *Paramasaugata* (devotee of the Buddha).

**Devapala (≈810–850 CE):**

- Devapala was the *greatest* Pala king—he *expanded* the empire to *Assam*, *Odisha*, and *Nepal*.
- He *patronized* *Nalanda*—he *built* a *temple* and *donated* *villages* to the *university*.
- He was the *last* great Pala king—the *later* Palas *declined* due to *feudalism* and *Turkic* invasions.

---

### Section 2 | The Pala Monasteries

The Palas *built* three *major* Buddhist monasteries:

| Monastery | Location | Significance |
|-----------|----------|--------------|
| Nalanda | Bihar | Oldest and most famous (pre-dates Palas) |
| Vikramshila | Bihar (Bhagalpur) | Founded by Dharmapala |
| Odantapuri | Bihar | Founded by Gopala |

**Nalanda University:**

- Nalanda *flourished* under the Palas—they *built* *new* *halls* and *donated* *villages*.
- The *Chinese* pilgrim *Hiuen Tsang* had *studied* at Nalanda in the 7th century—the *Pala* period *continued* this tradition.
- Nalanda was *destroyed* by the *Turks* in =1193 CE=—by *Bakhtiyar Khilji*.

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
The *Vikramshila* monastery was *founded* by *Dharmapala*—it was *one* of the *largest* Buddhist universities in India. It was *destroyed* by the *Turks* in the *13th* century. The exam often tests the *association*: Dharmapala → Vikramshila; Gopala → Odantapuri; Devapala → Nalanda (patronage).
[/alert]

[flashcards title="Pala Dynasty Essentials"]
| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Founder | Gopala (≈750 CE) |
| Greatest king | Devapala (810–850 CE) |
| Patrons of | Buddhism |
| Monasteries | Nalanda, Vikramshila, Odantapuri |
| Defeated by | Turks (≈12th century) |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 7: The Tripartite Struggle—The Contest for Kanauj

### Section 1 | The Strategic Importance of Kanauj

Kanauj was the *most* important city in *northern* India after the Gupta period—it was the *key* to *controlling* the *Ganga* valley.

**Why Kanauj?**

- *Strategic* location: It *lay* on the *Ganga* river—it *controlled* the *trade* routes *east-west*.
- *Prestige*: Kanauj was the *capital* of Harsha—it *represented* *imperial* legitimacy.

---

### Section 2 | The Three Contenders

| Dynasty | Region | Greatest King |
|---------|--------|---------------|
| Gurjara-Pratihara | Western/Northern India | Mihir Bhoja |
| Pala | Eastern India (Bengal, Bihar) | Devapala |
| Rashtrakuta | Deccan | Govinda III, Amoghavarsha I |

**The Sequence of the Struggle:**

1. **≈770–810 CE**: Dharmapala (Pala) *occupied* Kanauj and *installed* a *vassal* king.
2. **≈793–814 CE**: Govinda III (Rashtrakuta) *defeated* the Pratiharas and *occupied* Kanauj.
3. **≈836–885 CE**: Mihir Bhoja (Pratihara) *recaptured* Kanauj—the *Pratiharas* *finally* *won* the *contest*.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The *tripartite struggle* *weakened* *all* three dynasties—it *facilitated* the *rise* of the *Turks* in the *12th* century. The exam often tests the *consequence* of the struggle—*not* just the *players*.
[/alert]

## Chapter 8: Synthesis—The Legacy of the Post-Gupta Period

### Section 1 | The Conceptual Takeaway

The post-Gupta period is *not* a "dark age"—it is a *period* of *regional* *flourishing*.

1. **Harsha**: The *last* attempt at *northern* reunification—the *cultural* *synthesis* of Buddhism and Hinduism.

2. **Pallavas**: The *masters* of *Dravidian* *architecture*—the *rock-cut* temples and the *Shore* Temple.

3. **Chalukyas**: The *developers* of the *Vesara* style—the *fusion* of north and south.

4. **Rashtrakutas**: The *architects* of the *Kailasa* Temple—the *engineers* of the *single-rock* excavation.

5. **Pratiharas**: The *defenders* of the *north* against the *Arabs*—the *kingdom* that *finally* *won* Kanauj.

6. **Palas**: The *patrons* of *Buddhism*—the *builders* of *Vikramshila* and *Odantapuri*.

---

### Section 2 | Solved Examples

[example]
**Question:** The *Aihole Inscription* records the *defeat* of Harsha by:
- A) Pulakeshin II
- B) Narasimhavarman I
- C) Mahendravarman I
- D) Govinda III

**Solution:** The correct answer is =A) Pulakeshin II=. The Aihole Inscription is *composed* by *Ravikirti*—it *describes* Pulakeshin II's *victory* over Harsha. The *trap*: Students often *associate* the defeat with the *Pallavas*—but it was the *Chalukyas* who *defeated* Harsha.
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** Which of the following *monasteries* was *founded* by *Dharmapala*?
- A) Nalanda
- B) Vikramshila
- C) Odantapuri
- D) Taxila

**Solution:** The correct answer is =B) Vikramshila=. Nalanda *pre-dates* the Palas; Odantapuri was *founded* by *Gopala*; Vikramshila was *founded* by *Dharmapala*. The *trap*: Students often *assume* Nalanda was *founded* by the Palas—it was *not*; it was *pre-Pala*.
[/example]

### Section 2 | The Examination Mindset

1. **Identify the "Which":** Is the question asking about a *king*, a *monument*, a *text*, or a *dynasty*? Your mental checklist: *Person*, *Place*, *Text*, *Event*.

2. **Identify the "When":** The post-Gupta period is *chronologically diverse*—Harsha (7th cent.), Pallavas (4th–9th cent.), Chalukyas (6th–8th cent.), Rashtrakutas (8th–10th cent.). Use the *monuments* as *chronological anchors*: Shore Temple (Pallava, 7th cent.), Kailasa Temple (Rashtrakuta, 8th cent.).

3. **Identify the "Where":** The *regions* are *critical*:
   - Harsha: *North* (Thaneshwar, Kanauj)
   - Pallavas: *South-east* (Kanchipuram, Mahabalipuram)
   - Chalukyas: *Deccan* (Badami, Aihole)
   - Rashtrakutas: *Deccan* (Ellora, Manyakheta)
   - Pratiharas: *North-west* (Kanauj, Gujarat)
   - Palas: *East* (Bengal, Bihar)

4. **Avoid the "Isolationist Trap":** These kingdoms were *not* isolated—they *traded* with *Southeast Asia* (the Pallavas), *China* (Hiuen Tsang), and *Arabia* (the Pratiharas). The exam tests the *international* connections of this period.
