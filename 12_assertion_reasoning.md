# Assertion & Reasoning
> The logic of justification: separating factual claims from their explanatory warrants. This is not about whether you agree with a statement; it is about whether the reason provided genuinely accounts for the assertion.

## Chapter 1: The Foundations of Assertion-Reasoning Logic

### Section 1 | What Is Assertion-Reasoning?

Assertion-Reasoning (A-R) questions present two statements:
- **Assertion (A)**: A claim, proposition, or factual statement.
- **Reason (R)**: A statement that purports to explain, justify, or provide grounds for the Assertion.

Your task is to evaluate two independent dimensions:
1. **Truth Value**: Is the Assertion true? Is the Reason true?
2. **Logical Connection**: Does the Reason *correctly explain* the Assertion?

**The Critical Distinction**: A true Reason does not automatically make it a valid explanation for the Assertion. The Reason must be *the cause, basis, or justification* for the Assertion, not merely a true statement on the same topic.

[alert type="danger"]
**The Correlation-Causation Trap**: Just because the Assertion and Reason are both true does not mean the Reason explains the Assertion. They might be independently true facts without a causal or explanatory link. This is the most common source of errors.
[/alert]

### Section 2 | The Five Response Categories

Every A-R question has the same five answer options. Memorize them precisely:

| Option | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| A | Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A. |
| B | Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A. |
| C | A is true, but R is false. |
| D | A is false, but R is true. |
| E | Both A and R are false. |

**The Decision Tree**:
1. **Is A true?** If no, options are D or E.
2. **Is R true?** If no, options are C or E.
3. **If both true**, does R explain A? If yes → A; if no → B.

## Chapter 2: Establishing Logical Links

### Section 1 | The Explanation Criteria

For R to be the *correct explanation* of A, it must satisfy all three criteria:

1. **Sufficiency**: R must be enough to account for A. If R is a partial cause but not the complete cause, it may not be the "correct explanation."
2. **Necessity**: R must be relevant to A. If R is true but unrelated to A, it fails.
3. **Causal or Logical Direction**: R must show *why* A is true, not merely restate A or describe a consequence of A.

**Example**:
- A: "Water boils at 100°C at sea level."
- R: "Water is a liquid."
- Both are true, but R does not explain A. R is a property of water, not the reason for its boiling point. So option B.

**Example**:
- A: "Water boils at 100°C at sea level."
- R: "Atmospheric pressure at sea level is 1 atm, which is the standard pressure for defining the boiling point of water."
- R explains A. Option A.

### Section 2 | The Restatement Fallacy

A common trap is R that simply restates A in different words. This does not constitute an explanation.

- A: "The Earth revolves around the Sun."
- R: "The Earth orbits the Sun."
- R is a restatement, not an explanation. Option B (both true, but R does not explain A).

[alert type="success"]
**The "Why" Test**: After reading R, ask: "Does this answer the question *why* A is true?" If R merely says *that* A is true, it is a restatement. If R provides the underlying mechanism, it is an explanation.
[/alert]

[flashcards title="A-R Explanation Criteria"]
| Criterion | Question to Ask | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Truth | Is the statement factually correct? | "Water is a liquid" is true. |
| Relevance | Does R relate to A? | "Water is a liquid" relates to water, but not to boiling point. |
| Sufficiency | Does R fully account for A? | "Water boils at 100°C because of atmospheric pressure" is sufficient. |
| Direction | Does R explain why A, not vice versa? | R must provide the cause, not the effect. |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 3: True/False Statement Analysis

### Section 1 | Evaluating Truth Value

Determining truth value requires factual knowledge or logical deduction. In A-R questions, the truth values are usually straightforward—they test common knowledge or logical principles.

**Categories of Truth**:
1. **Factual Truth**: Verified by observation or established knowledge (e.g., "The Ganges flows into the Bay of Bengal").
2. **Logical Truth**: True by definition or logical necessity (e.g., "All bachelors are unmarried").
3. **Empirical Truth**: True based on scientific or historical evidence (e.g., "The Earth is round").

**The Trap**: When the Assertion is a value judgment or opinion, the exam usually avoids it. If you encounter one, treat it as false unless it is universally accepted.

### Section 2 | The Partial Truth Problem

Sometimes a statement is *partially* true—true in some cases but not all. In A-R questions, such statements are considered false unless the assertion is qualified.

**Example**:
- A: "All birds can fly."
- This is false (ostriches, penguins cannot fly). So A is false.
- R: "Birds have wings."
- R is true, but A is false. So option D (A false, R true).

[alert type="warning"]
**The Absolute Language Alert**: Words like "all," "always," "never," "every" make a statement vulnerable to being false. If you can think of a single counterexample, the statement is false. In A-R questions, this is often the intended trap.
[/alert]

[flashcards title="Truth Value Indicators"]
| Word | Implication | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| All | Universal; requires no exceptions | "All humans are mortal" (true) |
| Some | Existential; requires at least one | "Some birds are flightless" (true) |
| Always | Universal over time | "The sun always rises" (true) |
| Never | Universal negative | "Pigs never fly" (true) |
| Usually | Qualifies as "most" | "It usually rains in June" (often true) |
[/flashcards]

[example]
**Question:** Assertion (A): The ozone layer is depleting. Reason (R): Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) are released into the atmosphere.
**Solution:**
Step 1: Is A true? Yes, the ozone layer is indeed depleting (scientific consensus).
Step 2: Is R true? Yes, CFCs are released into the atmosphere.
Step 3: Does R explain A? Yes, CFCs are the primary cause of ozone depletion.
Step 4: Both true, and R explains A.
**Final Answer:** Option A
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** Assertion (A): The Earth has one moon. Reason (R): The moon is a natural satellite of the Earth.
**Solution:**
Step 1: Is A true? Yes.
Step 2: Is R true? Yes.
Step 3: Does R explain A? R describes *what* the moon is, but does not explain *why* the Earth has only one moon. It is a restatement/description, not an explanation.
Step 4: Both true, but R does not explain A.
**Final Answer:** Option B
[/example]
