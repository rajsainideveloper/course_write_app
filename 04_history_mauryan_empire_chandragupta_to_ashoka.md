# History: Mauryan Empire (Chandragupta to Ashoka)

> From the visionary founder who united a subcontinent to the emperor who renounced conquest—the political, administrative, and spiritual legacy of India's first imperial dynasty.

## Chapter 1: Chandragupta Maurya—The Architect of Empire

### Section 1 | The Rise of a Usurper

The establishment of the Mauryan empire was not a simple dynastic succession—it was a calculated political coup engineered by two extraordinary minds: *Chandragupta Maurya*, the ambitious young ruler, and *Kautilya* (also known as *Chanakya* or *Vishnugupta*), the political strategist whose *Arthashastra* would become the manual of statecraft for generations.

**The Overthrow of Dhana Nanda (≈322 BCE)**

The circumstances of Chandragupta's rise are shrouded in legend, but the historical core is clear:
- Dhana Nanda, the last Nanda king, was deeply unpopular due to his *excessive taxation* and *avarice*.
- Kautilya, a Brahmin from Taxila, had been *insulted* by Dhana Nanda at a royal gathering. He swore vengeance and sought a worthy candidate to overthrow the Nandas.
- He found Chandragupta—a young man of *low birth* (the Puranas call him a *Shudra*), but with *ambition* and *leadership qualities*.
- Through a combination of *military campaigns* and *political subversion*, Chandragupta defeated Dhana Nanda and occupied Pataliputra, the Nanda capital.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The *Mudrarakshasa* (a Sanskrit drama by Vishakhadatta, composed centuries later) dramatizes Chandragupta's rise as a *purely* Machiavellian plot. The exam does not test the *dramatic* version—it tests the *historical* facts: Chandragupta used *military force* and *political alliances* to overthrow the Nandas. The *Mudrarakshasa* is a *literary* source, not a *historical* one. Be careful with the distinction.
[/alert]

**Kautilya (Chanakya): The Kingmaker**

Kautilya was not merely a minister—he was the *architect* of the Mauryan empire. His treatise, the *Arthashastra*, outlines a *political science* that is pragmatic, cynical, and ruthlessly effective.

Key aspects of Kautilya's thought:
- *Saptanga* Theory: The state consists of seven elements—King, Minister, Country, Fortress, Treasury, Army, and Ally.
- *Matsya Nyaya* (The Law of the Fish): In the absence of a strong king, the strong devour the weak—order requires *force*.
- *Four Methods of Policy*: *Sama* (conciliation), *Dana* (gifts/bribery), *Bheda* (sowing dissension), and *Danda* (punishment/force).

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
The *Arthashastra* was *lost* for centuries and was rediscovered in =1905= by *R. Shamasastry* (a Sanskrit scholar) in a manuscript library in Mysore. This discovery revolutionized the study of ancient Indian political thought.
[/alert]

---

### Section 2 | Chandragupta's Conquests and the Defeat of Seleucus

**The Extent of the Mauryan Empire under Chandragupta**

By the time of his retirement, Chandragupta's empire extended from *Bengal* in the east to *Afghanistan* in the west, and from the *Himalayas* in the north to the *Vindhyas* in the south (though the southern conquests were largely completed by Bindusara).

**The Defeat of Seleucus Nicator (≈305 BCE)**

The most *diplomatically significant* event of Chandragupta's reign was his confrontation with *Seleucus Nicator*, one of Alexander's generals who had taken control of the Persian empire after Alexander's death.

- Seleucus attempted to *reclaim* the Indian territories that Alexander had conquered.
- Chandragupta met him in battle in the *Indus Valley* and *decisively defeated* the Greek forces.
- **The Treaty of 305 BCE**:
  - Seleucus ceded *Arachosia* (Kandahar), *Gedrosia* (Balochistan), and *Paropanisadae* (Kabul) to Chandragupta.
  - In return, Chandragupta gave Seleucus *500 war elephants*.
  - A *marriage alliance* was sealed—a Greek princess (likely a daughter or relative of Seleucus) was given to Chandragupta.

[alert type="info"]
**Examiner's Focus:**
The *war elephants* are significant—they allowed Seleucus to defeat his rivals in the *Hellenistic* world (especially the Ptolemies in Egypt). This exchange demonstrates that the Mauryan empire was *not* isolated—it was engaged in *international diplomacy* with the Greek world.
[/alert]

**Megasthenes: The Greek Ambassador**

Seleucus sent an ambassador, *Megasthenes*, to the Mauryan court at Pataliputra. His account, the *Indica*, is the *most important* source for Chandragupta's reign.

Megasthenes describes:
- The *grandeur* of Pataliputra—its massive timber walls and palisades.
- The *administration* of the city—the six committees of five members each.
- The *social structure*—the seven castes (his classification, which differs from the Vedic varnas).
- The *agriculture*—the fertility of the Gangetic plains.

---

### Section 3 | Chandragupta's Later Years and Death

Chandragupta abdicated the throne in ≈298 BCE and became a *Jain ascetic*.
- According to Jain tradition, he traveled to *Shravanabelagola* (in present-day Karnataka) with his guru, *Bhadrabahu*.
- He practiced *sallekhana*—the ritual of fasting until death—and died in the *Kaveri* region.

## Chapter 2: Bindusara—The Consolidator

### Section 1 | The Succession (≈298–273 BCE)

Bindusara, the son of Chandragupta, inherited a vast but *fragile* empire. His reign was dedicated to *consolidating* the territories his father had conquered and extending the empire *southward*.

**The "Slayer of Enemies"**

The name *Bindusara* means "one who has many weapons" or "the slayer of enemies." He was also called *Amitraghata*—"the destroyer of foes."

**Extension of the Empire:**

- Bindusara *completed* the conquest of the *Deccan*—the southern regions up to present-day *Karnataka*.
- He brought *sixteen* states under Mauryan control (according to the *Purana*).
- The empire now stretched from the *Himalayas* to the *Kaveri* in the south.

**The Greek Connection: Deimachus**

- *Deimachus* was the Greek ambassador sent to the court of Bindusara by *Antiochus I* (the Seleucid king).
- Deimachus's account (now lost) was used by later Greek writers (Strabo, Pliny) to describe India.

**Ashoka as Governor of Taxila and Ujjain**

- Before ascending the throne, Ashoka served as the *governor* of *Taxila* (the northwestern province) and *Ujjain* (the central-western province).
- In Taxila, Ashoka faced a *rebellion*—he suppressed it firmly.
- In Ujjain, he married *Devi* (the daughter of a merchant), who later gave birth to his *children*—*Mahinda* and *Sanghamitra*.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The SSC often asks about the *Greek ambassadors*: Megasthenes (Chandragupta), Deimachus (Bindusara), and later *Dionysius* (Ptolemy, under Ashoka). Memorize this *sequence*—Megasthenes → Deimachus → Dionysius. This is a *chronological anchor* for the Mauryan period.
[/alert]

---

### Section 2 | The Succession Crisis

Bindusara had *several* sons, and the succession was *contested*. Ashoka was not the eldest—his elder brother *Susima* was the crown prince.
- Ashoka's *mother* was of *low birth* (according to some traditions), which made him an unlikely heir.
- However, Ashoka's *abilities* as a governor and *military leader* won him the support of the *court ministers*.
- On Bindusara's death (≈273 BCE), Ashoka *seized* the throne—likely with the help of the *Mantriparishad* (council of ministers). Susima was *killed* in the succession struggle.

[alert type="danger"]
**Cognitive Trap:**
Some traditions romanticize Ashoka's succession as *peaceful*—they are *wrong*. The succession was *bloody*. The exam does not test the *romantic* version—it tests the *historical* reality that Ashoka *fought* for the throne and *eliminated* his rivals.
[/alert]

| Greek Ambassador | Deimachus |

## Chapter 3: Ashoka the Great—The Transformation

### Section 1 | The Early Reign and the Kalinga War

Ashoka ascended the throne in ≈273 BCE, but his coronation was delayed until ≈269 BCE (the four-year gap likely due to the succession struggle). His early reign was *expansionist*—he continued the Mauryan policy of conquest.

**The Kalinga War (≈261 BCE)**

- **Kalinga** (present-day Odisha) was a *prosperous* kingdom—it controlled the *trade routes* to Southeast Asia.
- Ashoka's army *invaded* Kalinga, and the war was *brutal*.
- **The Toll**:
  - 100,000 Kalingans killed
  - 150,000 taken captive
  - Mauryan casualties: approximately 10,000
- Ashoka himself witnessed the *carnage*—the fields covered with corpses, the wailing of the widows, the burning of villages.

**The Conversion to Buddhism**

The *psychological shock* of the Kalinga war transformed Ashoka. He was *not* a Buddhist before Kalinga—he converted *after* witnessing the suffering.

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
Ashoka's conversion to Buddhism was a *gradual* process—not an *instantaneous* epiphany. The *Rock Edict XIII* describes his *remorse* and his *adoption* of the *Dhamma*. This is the *primary source* for his conversion.
[/alert]

---

### Section 2 | Ashoka's Dhamma—The Moral Empire

Ashoka's *Dhamma* (not Buddhism per se, but a *universal moral code*) was the *foundation* of his governance.

**The Principles of Dhamma:**

1. **Non-violence (Ahimsa)**: The state must *protect* all living beings—animals are *not* to be slaughtered for sacrifice.
2. **Tolerance and Respect**: *Respect* for all religions and sects—Ashoka encouraged *debate* but *forbade* religious persecution.
3. **Welfare of Subjects**: The king must work for the *happiness* of his people—this included building hospitals, planting medicinal herbs, and digging wells.
4. **Moral Conduct (Dharma)**: People should *obey* their parents, *respect* teachers, and *be charitable* to the poor.
5. **Non-Sectarianism**: The state should *not* favor one religion over another—all sects deserve *respect*.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
Ashoka's *Dhamma* is *not* equivalent to Buddhism. The Dhamma is a *universal moral code*—it is *not* the *Four Noble Truths* or the *Eightfold Path*. The SSC often tests this distinction: Ashoka propagated *Dhamma*—a *civic religion*—and *not* necessarily *Buddhist* doctrine. The *Pataliputra* edicts make this clear.
[/alert]

**The Dhamma Mahamatras**

Ashoka appointed *Dhamma Mahamatras* (officers of the Dhamma) to *spread* the moral code across the empire.
- They were *independent* of the regular bureaucracy.
- They were responsible for *reporting* on the conditions of the people.
- They could *intervene* in judicial cases if they found *injustice*.

---

### Section 3 | Ashoka's Edicts—The Inscriptional Record

Ashoka's edicts are the *most important* primary source for his reign. They are inscribed on *rocks*, *pillars*, and *cave walls* across the Indian subcontinent.

**Types of Edicts:**

| Type | Number | Content |
|------|--------|---------|
| Major Rock Edicts | 14 | General principles of Dhamma; Kalinga war; welfare measures |
| Minor Rock Edicts | Varied | Personal statements; Ashoka's conversion |
| Pillar Edicts | 7 | Specific policies; animal protection; religious tolerance |
| Minor Pillar Edicts | Varied | Local issues; monastic disciplinary matters |

**The Language and Script:**

- **Prakrit** (in *Brahmi* script) — the most common; used for the *mass* of the empire.
- **Greek** and **Aramaic** — found in the northwestern provinces (Kandahar, Taxila). This proves that Ashoka *communicated* with his Greek subjects in their *own language*.

**The Major Rock Edicts—A Systematic Analysis:**

1. **Edict I**: Prohibition of animal sacrifice and slaughter.
2. **Edict II**: Establishment of *hospitals* for humans and animals; planting of medicinal herbs.
3. **Edict III**: Gifts to Brahmins and ascetics; instruction on *filial piety*.
4. **Edict IV**: Encouragement of *morality*; criticism of *cruelty*.
5. **Edict V**: Appointment of *Dhamma Mahamatras*.
6. **Edict VI**: Regular reports on the *welfare* of the people.
7. **Edict VII**: Religious tolerance—respect for *all* sects.
8. **Edict VIII**: Ashoka's *pilgrimages* to Buddhist sites.
9. **Edict IX**: The *inefficacy* of ceremonies; *Dhamma* is the real merit.
10. **Edict X**: The *virtue* of truth; the king does not seek *fame* but the *welfare* of his people.
11. **Edict XI**: Explanation of *Dhamma* in terms of morality and charity.
12. **Edict XII**: Respect for *all* religions; the king's *neutrality*.
13. **Edict XIII**: **The Kalinga War**—the *fullest* description of the war and Ashoka's *remorse*.
14. **Edict XIV**: The edicts are *abbreviated*—the *full* account is in the *cave* inscriptions.

**The Pillar Edicts:**

The Pillar Edicts (7 in total) are inscribed on stone pillars that Ashoka erected across the empire. They are *more concise* than the Rock Edicts and focus on *specific* policies:
- *Dharma* is good.
- *Heaven* is attained by *good deeds*.
- The king's *duties* are to *protect* the people.

[alert type="info"]
**Examiner's Focus:**
The *location* of the edicts is often tested: *Major Rock Edicts* are found at *Girnar* (Gujarat), *Dhauli* (Odisha), *Kandahar* (Afghanistan), *Shahbazgarhi* (Pakistan), and *Jaugada* (Odisha). The *Pillar Edicts* are at *Delhi*, *Laurya-Nandangarh*, *Laurya-Araraj*, and *Allahabad*. The *Delhi* pillar is the most famous—it is now in the *Feroz Shah Kotla* area.
[/alert]

**The National Emblem of India**

The *Lion Capital* of the Ashokan pillar at *Sarnath* is the *National Emblem* of India. It depicts:
- Four lions (back-to-back) on a *circular abacus*.
- The abacus has *four animals*—horse, bull, lion, elephant.
- The *Ashoka Chakra* (24 spokes) is at the base.

[alert type="success"]
**Important Fact:**
The *National Emblem* is *not* the complete Ashoka pillar—it is the *top portion* (the capital). The *significance* of the four lions: the *lion* was the *symbol* of the Buddha (the *Lion of the Shakyas*). The *Ashoka Chakra* is derived from the wheel (dharma-chakra) of the Dhamma.
[/alert]

---

### Section 4 | Ashoka's Legacy

**The Third Buddhist Council (≈250 BCE)**

Ashoka convened the Third Buddhist Council at *Pataliputra* under the presidency of *Moggaliputta Tissa*.
- The council was convened to *purify* the Buddhist order (Sangha) from *heretical* monks.
- It *codified* the Buddhist *canon* (the Tripitaka) and *authored* the *Kathavatthu* (a text on *controversial points* of doctrine).

**The Spread of Buddhism:**

Ashoka *sent missionaries* to spread the Dhamma across the known world:
- **Sri Lanka**: Sent *Mahinda* (his son) and *Sanghamitra* (his daughter). Mahinda converted the Sinhalese king Devanampiya Tissa.
- **Hellenistic World**: Sent missionaries to the Greek kingdoms—Syria, Egypt, Macedonia, and Cyrene.
- **Central Asia**: Buddhists reached Bactria and Sogdiana.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The *Third Buddhist Council* is often confused with the *First* and *Second* Buddhist Councils. The *First* was at Rajagriha (under Ajatashatru), the *Second* at Vaishali (under Kalasoka), and the *Third* at Pataliputra (under Ashoka). This *triple sequence* is *essential* for exam preparation.
[/alert]

**Ashoka's Later Years**

Ashoka reigned until ≈232 BCE. His later years were dedicated to *pilgrimages* to Buddhist sites—Lumbini (Buddha's birth), Bodh Gaya (enlightenment), Sarnath (first sermon), and Kushinara (parinirvana). He erected *pillars* at these sites.

[flashcards title="Ashoka the Great"]
| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Reign | 273–232 BCE (coronation 269 BCE) |
| Kalinga War | 261 BCE (100,000 killed) |
| Conversion | After Kalinga War |
| Dhamma | Universal moral code (not sectarian Buddhism) |

[/flashcards]

## Chapter 4: Mauryan Administration—The Imperial Apparatus

### Section 1 | Central Administration

The Mauryan state was *centralized*—all authority flowed from the king. But it was *not* an autocracy—a *bureaucracy* of officials managed the empire.

**The King:**

- The king was the *ultimate* authority—he was the *head* of the government, *commander* of the army, and *chief* judge.
- The king was *not* divine (the *Arthashastra* does not advocate kingship by divine right)—he was a *servant* of the state (the *state* as an abstract entity).

**The Mantriparishad (Council of Ministers):**

The Mantriparishad *advised* the king on all matters:
- *Purohita*: Chief priest.
- *Senapati*: Commander-in-chief.
- *Sannidhatri*: Treasurer.
- *Samahartri*: Chief collector of revenue.
- *Mantri*: The minister who *advised* the king on *state* affairs.
- *Amatyas*: The *bureaucrats* (functionaries) who implemented the policies.

[alert type="info"]
**Examiner's Focus:**
The *Amatyas* were the *executive* arm—they were *trained* in the *Arthashastra* (statecraft). The *Mantri* was the *advisory* arm—he was the *strategist* who *plotted* the *course* of policy.
[/alert]

---

### Section 2 | Provincial Administration

The empire was divided into *four provinces* (each governed by a *Kumara*—a royal prince):

| Province | Capital | Region |
|----------|---------|--------|
| Uttarapatha | Taxila | Northwest (Punjab, Gandhara) |
| Avantipatha | Ujjain | Central-west (Malwa) |
| Prachyapatha | Toshali | Eastern (Orissa, Bengal) |
| Dakshinapatha | Suvarnagiri | Southern (Deccan) |

**The Role of the Kumara:**

- The Kumara was the *king's representative*—he was *not* independent; he *executed* the king's orders.
- He was *assisted* by a *viceroy's council* of *ministers*.
- He had *judicial* and *executive* powers in his province.

---

### Section 3 | Local Administration

At the *local level*, the administration was *detailed* and *systematic*.

**Village Administration (Gramika):**

- The *Gramika* (village headman) was the *chief* of the village.
- He was *responsible* for *tax collection*, *law and order*, and *maintaining* the village records.
- The *Gramika* reported to the *Sthanika* (district officer).

**Municipal Administration (Pataliputra):**

Megasthenes describes the *city council* of Pataliputra:
- The city was divided into *six committees* of *five members* each:
  1. **Supervision of public works** (roads, wells, bridges)
  2. **Registration of foreigners** (visitors, traders)
  3. **Collection of taxes** (the *city tax*)
  4. **Record of births and deaths** (census)
  5. **Supervision of commerce** (markets, weights and measures)
  6. **Maintenance of public buildings** (temples, palaces)

**The Spies (Pativedakas):**

Kautilya's spy network was *elaborate*—spies reported *directly* to the king on *political dissidents*, *corrupt officials*, and *public opinion*.

---

### Section 4 | The Judicial System

The Mauryan state had a *hierarchical* judicial system:
- **The King**: The *highest* court of appeal.
- **The District Courts**: Staffed by *dharmasthas* (judges) who applied *customary law* and *royal edicts*.
- **The Village Courts**: The *Gramika* presided over minor disputes.

**The Types of Law:**

- *Dharma* (religious law) governed *personal* matters (marriage, inheritance).
- *State law* (royal decrees) governed *public* matters (taxation, trade, crime).

## Chapter 5: Kautilya's Arthashastra—The Treatise on Statecraft

### Section 1 | The Structure and Content

The *Arthashastra* is divided into *15 books* (Adhikaranas). The *most important* for the exam are the *first five* books (administration), the *sixth* (foreign policy), and the *tenth* (warfare).

**The Concept of State (Saptanga):**

Kautilya defines the *state* as consisting of *seven elements*:

| Element | Sanskrit | Function |
|---------|----------|----------|
| King | Swami | The ruler |
| Minister | Amatya | The advisor |
| Country | Janapada | The territory |
| Fortress | Durga | The military base |
| Treasury | Kosha | The economic base |
| Army | Danda | The military power |
| Ally | Mitra | The diplomatic support |

**The Four Methods of Policy:**

- *Sama*: Conciliation—diplomatic negotiation.
- *Dana*: Gifts and bribery—offering or threatening *economic* inducements.
- *Bheda*: Sowing dissension—creating *internal* conflicts in the enemy state.
- *Danda*: Punishment—military force.

**The Mandala Theory:**

Kautilya's theory of *foreign policy*: The *king* is at the center; neighboring states are *potential enemies*; states beyond them are *potential allies*. The *circle of states* (mandala) is a *theory of balance of power*.

---

### Section 2 | The Arthashastra and Mauryan Administration

The *Arthashastra* is *not* a *description* of Mauryan administration—it is a *manual* that *prescribed* how administration *should* be organized. However, the *parallels* between the *Arthashastra* and the administrative *realities* described by Megasthenes are *remarkable*—indicating that Kautilya's principles were actually *implemented*.

**Key Administrative Concepts:**

- *Danda* (punishment): The king must *enforce* the law—*without* punishment, the state *collapses*.
- *Artha* (wealth): The king must *accumulate* wealth—the treasury is the *source* of power.
- *Dharma* (morality): The king must be *just*—but *justice* serves the *stability* of the state.

[flashcards title="Arthashastra Essentials"]
| Term | Concept |
|------|---------|
| Saptanga | Seven elements of the state |
| Sama-Dana-Bheda-Danda | Four methods of policy |
| Mandala Theory | Circle of states; foreign policy |
| Danda | Punishment; state force |
| Arthashastra | Science of statecraft; attributed to Kautilya |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 6: Art and Architecture of the Mauryan Period

### Section 1 | Stupas: The Buddhist Monument

The *stupa* is the *most iconic* architectural form of the Mauryan period. It is a *hemispherical* mound containing *relics* of the Buddha or his disciples.

**The Stupa at Sanchi:**

- Sanchi (in Madhya Pradesh) has the *oldest* surviving stupa in India—the *Great Stupa*.
- It was *built* by Ashoka (with a *mud* core) and later *enlarged* by the Shungas.
- The *toranas* (gateways) are *later* additions (Shunga and Satavahana periods).

**The Stupa at Bharhut:**

- Bharhut (in Madhya Pradesh) is *earlier* than Sanchi—dating to the *Shunga* period (≈2nd century BCE).
- The *relief* panels depict *Jataka* tales and *Buddhist* symbols.

**The Architectural Significance of Stupas:**

The stupa is *not* a temple—it is a *reliquary* (a structure that contains relics). The *shape* (hemispherical) symbolizes the *cosmic mountain* (Mount Meru). The *umbrella* at the top represents the *dhamma* (the wheel of law).

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The *Great Stupa* at Sanchi is *not* a Mauryan structure in its *present* form—it was *enlarged* by the Shungas. The *core* is Mauryan, but the *stone casing* and *toranas* are *later*. The exam often tests this *stratigraphic* fact: the Mauryan stupas were *mud* structures; the *stone* stupas came later.
[/alert]

---

### Section 2 | The Ashokan Pillars

Ashoka erected stone pillars across the empire—inscribed with his *edicts*. They are *technologically sophisticated*—they were *single monoliths* (carved from *single* blocks of stone).

**The Lion Capital (Sarnath):**

- The *National Emblem* of India.
- The *four lions* standing back-to-back on a *circular abacus*.
- The *abacus* is *decorated* with a *wheel* (the dharma-chakra) and *animals* (horse, bull, lion, elephant).

**The Pillar at Allahabad:**

- It contains *three* sets of inscriptions: Ashoka's edicts, Samudragupta's *prashasti* (praise), and Jahangir's *memorandum*.
- This *multi-layered* nature is *historically significant*—it shows how later rulers *appropriated* the pillars.

**The Pillar at Laurya-Nandangarh:**

- Contains Ashoka's *Pillar Edict I* (about the *protection* of animals).
- The *capital* is *missing*—only the *shaft* remains.

---

### Section 3 | Rock-cut Caves: The Barabar Caves

The *Barabar Caves* (in Bihar) are the *earliest* rock-cut caves in India—they were *dedicated* to the *Ajivika* sect (an ascetic tradition that Ashoka patronized).

**The Lomas Rishi Cave:**

- The *most famous* cave at Barabar.
- The *facade* is *imitation* of timber architecture—the *horseshoe* arch is characteristic of *later* Buddhist cave architecture.
- The *inscription* on the cave *records* that it was *given* to the Ajivikas by Ashoka.

| Monument | Location | Significance |
|----------|----------|--------------|
| Great Stupa | Sanchi (MP) | Mauryan core; later Shunga enlargement |
| Lion Capital | Sarnath (UP) | National Emblem |
| Ashokan Pillar | Allahabad (UP) | Multiple inscriptions |
| Barabar Caves | Bihar | Earliest rock-cut caves; Ajivika dedication |
[/flashcards]

## Chapter 7: The Decline of the Mauryan Empire

### Section 1 | The Weak Successors

Ashoka died in ≈232 BCE. The *successors* were *weak*—they lacked Ashoka's *charisma*, *administrative skill*, and *military capability*.

**The Succession:**

- *Dasaratha* (Ashoka's grandson) ruled from ≈232–224 BCE.
- *Samprati* (another grandson) ruled from ≈224–215 BCE.
- *Sališuka* ruled from ≈215–202 BCE.
- *Devavarman* ruled from ≈202–195 BCE.
- *Saṁdamana* ruled from ≈195–187 BCE.

[alert type="warning"]
**Exam Trap:**
The SSC often asks: "Who was the *last* Mauryan king?" The answer is =Brihadratha Maurya=—he was *assassinated* by his *commander-in-chief* *Pushyamitra Shunga* in =185 BCE=. The *Shunga* dynasty *replaced* the Mauryas.
[/alert]

---

### Section 2 | Causes of Decline

**Economic Crisis:**

- The Mauryan state *over-taxed* the peasantry (the tax rate was one-fourth to one-sixth of the produce).
- The *bureaucracy* became *corrupt*—the *Dhamma Mahamatras* lost their *moral authority*.
- The *treasury* was *depleted* by Ashoka's *generous donations* and *costly pilgrimages*.

**Weak Successors:**

- The kings after Ashoka were *incompetent*—they *divided* the empire among themselves.
- The *provinces* began to *revolt*—the *Viceroys* (Kumaras) often declared *independence*.

**Brahminical Reaction:**

- The Brahmins were *resentful* of Ashoka's *favouring* of Buddhism.
- The *Shunga* dynasty (which overthrew the Mauryas) was *Brahminical*—Pushyamitra Shunga *revived* the *Vedic* sacrifices and *persecuted* Buddhists (though this is *disputed*).
- The *Arthashastra* was *not* a Brahminical text—Kautilya was a Brahmin, but his *pragmatism* was *opposed* to the *ritualism* of the Brahmins.

**Military Weakness:**

- The Mauryan army was *huge*—but it was *expensive* to maintain.
- The *war elephants* were *difficult* to *replace*.
- The *northwestern* provinces fell to the *Indo-Greeks* (the Bactrian Greeks) after the Mauryas weakened.

---

### Section 3 | The End of the Mauryas

=185 BCE=: *Pushyamitra Shunga* assassinated *Brihadratha Maurya* while he was *reviewing* his troops.

**The Shunga Dynasty:**

- Pushyamitra Shunga established the *Shunga* dynasty (≈185–73 BCE).
- The Shungas ruled *Magadha* and the *central* parts of the empire.
- They were *patrons* of *Brahminism*—they *revived* the *Vedic* sacrifices.
- However, they *did not* end the *Buddhist* tradition—the *Sanchi* stupa was *enlarged* during the Shunga period.

[alert type="info"]
**Examiner's Focus:**
The Mauryan decline was *gradual*—it was *not* an abrupt collapse. The *Shunga* dynasty did *not* reconquer the *south* or the *northwest*—the empire *fragmented*. The *Indo-Greeks* (Menander) invaded the *northwest* in the 2nd century BCE, and the *Satavahanas* rose in the south. The *Mauryan* empire was *not* replaced by a *single* empire—it was *fragmented* into *regional* states.
[/alert]

## Chapter 8: Synthesis—The Mauryan Legacy

### Section 1 | The Conceptual Takeaway

The Mauryan empire was the *first* pan-Indian state—it *integrated* the subcontinent politically, economically, and culturally. Its *legacy* extends *far* beyond its chronological limits:

1. **Political Integration**: The Mauryas *introduced* the concept of a *centralized empire*—a model that later empires (Guptas, Mughals, British) *adapted*.

2. **Administrative Tradition**: The Mauryan *bureaucracy*—with its *systematic* taxation, *hierarchical* governance, and *espionage* network—was the *foundation* of Indian statecraft.

3. **Buddhist Expansion**: Ashoka's *missionaries* spread Buddhism to *Sri Lanka*, *Central Asia*, and the *Hellenistic* world—making it a *global* religion.

4. **The Arthashastra**: Kautilya's treatise remains the *most important* text on Indian political thought—it is still *studied* in military academies today.

5. **Ashoka's Dhamma**: The concept of *moral governance*—the *welfare state*—was *first* articulated by Ashoka. His edicts are the *earliest* evidence of a *state* committed to the *well-being* of its *subjects*.

### Section 2 | Solved Examples

[example]
**Question:** The *Kalinga War* is described in which of Ashoka's edicts?
- A) Rock Edict I
- B) Rock Edict XIII
- C) Pillar Edict V
- D) Minor Rock Edict IV

**Solution:** The correct answer is =B) Rock Edict XIII=. This edict contains Ashoka's *detailed* account of the war, the *loss of life*, and his *remorse*. The *trap*: Many students assume the Kalinga War is described in the *major* edicts (it is), but they often *confuse* the *number*—Edict XIII is the *answer*.
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** Consider the following statements about the Mauryan administration:
1. The *Mantriparishad* was the advisory body to the king.
2. The *Dhamma Mahamatras* were the *judges* in the courts.
3. The *Kumara* were the provincial governors.

Which of the above statements is/are *correct*?
- A) 1 and 2 only
- B) 1 and 3 only
- C) 2 and 3 only
- D) 1, 2, and 3

**Solution:** The correct answer is =B) 1 and 3 only=. Statement 1 is correct—the Mantriparishad advised the king. Statement 2 is *incorrect*—the Dhamma Mahamatras were *moral officers*, not judges. The *dharmasthas* were the judges. Statement 3 is correct—the Kumara were the governors of provinces. The *trap*: Students often confuse the *Dhamma Mahamatras* with the *judicial* officials—they are *separate* institutions.
[/example]

[example]
**Question:** The *Arthashastra* of Kautilya prescribes *four methods* of state policy. They are:
- A) Sama, Dana, Bheda, Danda
- B) Dharma, Artha, Kama, Moksha
- C) Satya, Ahimsa, Dana, Tapa
- D) Raja, Rishi, Yogi, Sannyasa

**Solution:** The correct answer is =A) Sama, Dana, Bheda, Danda=. These are the *four methods* of *statecraft*—conciliation, gifts/bribery, sowing dissension, and punishment/force. The *trap*: Option B refers to the *four Purusharthas* (the four goals of life)—not the *Arthashastra*.
[/example]

### Section 2 | The Examination Mindset

1. **Identify the "What":** Is the question asking about a *king*, a *battle*, an *edict*, or a *concept*? Your mental checklist: *Person*, *Event*, *Text*, *Institution*.

2. **Identify the "When":** The Mauryan period is *chronologically locked*: Chandragupta (322–298) → Bindusara (298–273) → Ashoka (273–232). This *triple sequence* is the *anchor* for the entire period.

3. **Identify the "Where":** The empire had *four* provinces—Taxila (northwest), Ujjain (central-west), Toshali (east), Suvarnagiri (south). Map the *geography* of the edicts—Girnar, Dhauli, Kandahar, Shahbazgarhi, Jaugada.

4. **Avoid the "Moralistic Trap":** Ashoka's Dhamma is *not* pacifist (he maintained a *huge* army). It is *moral*—not *strategic*. His *Dhamma* did *not* abolish the *army*—it *reformed* the *use* of force.

[alert type="success"]
**Final Exam Mantra:**
The Mauryan empire is the *foundational* chapter in Indian history—it is the *first* time that the *subcontinent* was *unified*. The *examination* of this period is *not* about memorizing *all* the edicts—it is about *understanding* the *logic* of the empire: the *political consolidation* of Chandragupta, the *expansion* of Bindusara, the *transformation* of Ashoka, and the *fragmentation* of the successors. When you *understand* this *trajectory*, you will answer *any* question correctly.
[/alert]
